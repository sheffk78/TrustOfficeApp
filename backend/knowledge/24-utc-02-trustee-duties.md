# UTC Doctrine 02 — The Trustee's Duties, in Plain English

> ⚠️ **Scope note — a duty is not a defense.**
> The Code's remedies section lists the powers a court has, including surcharge, removal, and reduction of compensation. Keeping good records shows the reasoning behind your decisions and makes those decisions easier to produce; it is not a guarantee of any outcome, and nothing here should be read as one.

**UTC anchor:** art. 8 — ss. 801, 802, 803, 804, 805, 806, 807, 808, 809, 810, 811, 812, 815, 816; art. 6 — s. 603; art. 10 — ss. 1005, 1009

## Why this matters

The statutory skeleton underneath the four core duties: the Code's structure for duty, skill, delegation, and property, and the sections a trustee gets pointed to when a question is genuinely legal. This section keeps every concept anchored to what a solo trustee actually does.

## The umbrella duty

Section 801 is the whole thing in one sentence. Upon acceptance of a trusteeship, the trustee shall administer the trust in good faith, in accordance with its terms and purposes and the interests of the beneficiaries, and in accordance with the Code.

Three obligations, not one. A trustee who satisfies the document but not the beneficiaries' interests has not satisfied s. 801. And the duty starts upon acceptance — not upon signing, and not upon the first distribution.

## Loyalty

Section 802(a) is one line and it is strict: a trustee shall administer the trust solely in the interests of the beneficiaries.

The rest of s. 802 is machinery for the cases that actually generate disputes.

Subsection (b) makes a self-interested transaction *voidable* by an affected beneficiary unless an exception applies: the transaction was authorized by the trust terms, approved by the court, the beneficiary did not sue within the time allowed by s. 1005, the beneficiary consented, ratified, or released the trustee under s. 1009, or the transaction involves a contract entered into or claim acquired by the trustee before the person became or contemplated becoming trustee.

Subsection (c) is what trustees most often miss. A transaction involving the investment or management of trust property is *presumed* conflicted if the trustee enters into it with the trustee's spouse; the trustee's descendants, siblings, parents, or their spouses; an agent or attorney of the trustee; or a corporation or enterprise in which the trustee, or a person owning a significant interest in the trustee, has an interest that might affect the trustee's best judgment. The trustee is not the only one who triggers this — a brother-in-law's company is enough to create a presumption, and overcoming the presumption is the trustee's burden.

Subsection (d) catches transactions that do not concern trust property but occur during the trust's existence or while the trustee retains significant influence over a beneficiary, and from which the trustee obtains an advantage. Those are voidable unless the trustee establishes the transaction was fair to the beneficiary.

Subsection (e) catches a transaction in the trustee's individual capacity concerning an opportunity properly belonging to the trust. If the trust could have taken it, the trustee taking it personally is a conflict.

Subsection (h) lists what is not precluded if fair to the beneficiaries: an agreement between trustee and beneficiary about appointment or compensation, payment of reasonable compensation, a transaction between the trust and another trust, decedent's estate, or conservatorship where the trustee is a fiduciary or a beneficiary has an interest, a deposit of trust money in a regulated financial-service institution operated by the trustee, and an advance by the trustee of money to protect the trust.

Subsection (i) lets a court appoint a special fiduciary to decide a proposed transaction that might violate s. 802 — the practical path for a trustee who wants a clean route on a genuinely conflicted deal.

That is the duty of loyalty in practice: self-dealing avoidance, including disclosure and stepping aside. The Code adds a legal presumption here: where the trust document would permit the conflict, and the trustee does not address it, the Code presumes the trustee did not act in good faith.

## Impartiality

Section 803 is short: if a trust has two or more beneficiaries, the trustee shall act impartially in investing, managing, and distributing the trust property, giving due regard to the beneficiaries' respective interests.

"Due regard," not "equal treatment." The current-versus-remainder tension is exactly what this addresses. The Code's instruction is to weigh both, which is why the trust document's allocation language usually does most of the work.

## Prudence, costs, and skill

Section 804: a trustee shall administer the trust as a prudent person would, by considering the purposes, terms, distributional requirements, and other circumstances of the trust, and in satisfying it shall exercise reasonable care, skill, and caution. That list — purposes, terms, distributional requirements, other circumstances — is a usable pre-flight check before any investment decision.

Section 805 limits costs: a trustee may incur only costs reasonable in relation to the trust property, the purposes of the trust, and the skills of the trustee.

Section 806 is a trap worth knowing: a trustee who has special skills or expertise, *or who was named trustee in reliance on a representation that they have it*, shall use those skills. Being named because you are a doctor, a lawyer, or a CPA raises the floor.

## Delegation

Section 807: a trustee may delegate duties and powers that a prudent trustee of comparable skills could properly delegate under the circumstances, and shall exercise reasonable care, skill, and caution in selecting the agent, establishing the scope and terms of the delegation consistent with the purposes and terms of the trust, and **periodically reviewing the agent's actions**.

Subsections (b)–(d) close the loop: the agent owes a duty to the trust; a trustee who complies with (a) is not liable for the agent's action; and an agent who accepts a delegation submits to the courts of the state.

What (a)(3) requires that a lot of trustees skip: periodic review. Hiring a financial advisor and never reading the reports is not a delegation that satisfies s. 807.

## Property, records, and enforcement

Section 809: a trustee shall take reasonable steps to take control of and protect the trust property.

Section 810 is the separation rule with teeth. A trustee shall keep adequate records of the administration of the trust (a); keep trust property separate from the trustee's own property (b); and, except as provided in (d), cause trust property to be designated so that the interest of the trust, to the extent feasible, appears in records maintained by a party other than a trustee or beneficiary (c). Subsection (d) allows investing two or more separate trusts as a whole if the trustee maintains records clearly indicating the respective interests.

Subsection (c) is the one people miss: a ledger in the trustee's own spreadsheet is not enough. The trust's interest has to show up in a third party's records — the bank's, the brokerage's, the transfer agent's. That is a concrete, checkable standard, and it maps directly onto the separation checklist the Trustee-101 course already teaches.

Section 811: a trustee shall take reasonable steps to enforce claims of the trust and defend claims against it. Section 812: a trustee shall take reasonable steps to compel a former trustee or other person to deliver trust property, and to redress a breach of trust *known* to have been committed by a former trustee. "Known" is doing work in (b), and the Code's comment notes pursuit is not required where the amount, cost, and likelihood of recovery make action uneconomic.

## Powers

Section 815 grants general powers: powers conferred by the trust terms, and — except as limited by the terms — all powers over trust property which an unmarried competent owner has over individually owned property, plus any other powers appropriate to achieve proper investment, management, and distribution. Subsection (b) makes every one of them subject to the Article 8 fiduciary duties.

Section 816 is the specific-powers list: collecting, buying and selling, borrowing and mortgaging, continuing a business interest, voting securities, managing real property, leasing, insuring, environmental response, paying and contesting claims, paying taxes, exercising tax elections, making loans to beneficiaries (with a lien on future distributions for repayment), and pledging trust property to guarantee a beneficiary's loans. It answers "can I even do this?" without a second opinion.

A note on s. 808: it is reserved. The former settlor-directs-trustee power was largely superseded by the Uniform Directed Trust Act, with its substance relocated into s. 603 (see the amendments file).

### What this means for a trustee

- Use s. 801 as the frame for every decision: good faith, the document's terms and purposes, and the beneficiaries' interests. If your rationale cannot state all three, it is not finished.
- Before any transaction with a family member, a business you have an interest in, or a business you control, assume s. 802(c) creates a conflict presumption and plan documentation accordingly. Name the statutory family members in your thinking, not just the obvious one.
- Check that trust property is titled so the trust's interest appears in a third party's records — bank, brokerage, county recorder — not only in your own ledger.
- When you delegate, write the scope down and schedule the periodic review. Both are required by s. 807.
- If you are named because of a professional specialty, s. 806 raises your standard whether or not you keep using the specialty.
- Keep a running list of your powers under ss. 815 and 816. It answers most "am I allowed to" questions without a lawyer.

### Common question we get

**"I made a good decision but I can't prove I made it. Am I in trouble?"**
The Code's answer is that breach of trust is judged on what the trustee did, and the remedies run to compelling performance, ordering an accounting, removal, reduction of compensation, and making the trustee pay. So proof matters, but "am I in trouble" is a legal conclusion that no tool can make for you. What a contemporaneous record can do is show what you decided, what you relied on, and when — the difference between a documented decision and an undocumented one. Whether it was a breach is for a court.

**"My cousin sold me a property at a fair price. Does that count as self-dealing?"**
Possibly. Section 802(c) lists the trustee's spouse, and the trustee's descendants, siblings, parents, and their spouses. A cousin is not on that list, so the transaction is not automatically presumed conflicted. But s. 802(b) makes any personal-interest transaction voidable unless an exception applies, and s. 802(e) reaches an opportunity properly belonging to the trust that the trustee took in a personal capacity. If the property could have gone to the trust, that is the more important question. Get it reviewed before you close, not after.

**"Can I just hire a financial advisor and let them handle the investments?"**
You can delegate, and s. 807 sets the conditions: the delegation must be one a prudent trustee of comparable skills could properly make, and you must use reasonable care in selecting the agent, setting scope and terms consistent with the trust's purposes and terms, and periodically reviewing the agent's actions. If you comply, s. 807(c) means you are not liable for the agent's action. If you hire and never look at the reporting, you have not completed the third step. A written investment policy statement with a trustee certification is the cleanest way to evidence scope and review.
