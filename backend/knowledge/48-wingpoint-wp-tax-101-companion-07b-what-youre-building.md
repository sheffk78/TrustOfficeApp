# Lesson 7b: First Time in the Trust — What You're Actually Building

**Course:** WP-TAX-101 — The Tax Question
**Lesson:** Companion 7b (after "Standing Firm")

You've now seen what the law says and how to stand firm on it. This lesson is different. It walks you through the questions you're actually asking now that you're *getting into* an ecclesiastical trust for the first time — not the law in the abstract, but your next steps, your bank, your family, your doubt, and the structure you're building.

If you're new here, the concept has two parts that travel together:

1. **The 508(c)(1)(a) religious organization** — the tax-exempt shell. Automatic by law. This is the *roof* over the whole structure.
2. **The private, irrevocable, ecclesiastical trust** — the structure beneath that roof, created by contract under common law. This is the "one person, three seats" trust you administer.

Here's what people ask first.

---

## 1. "What exactly am I building?"

An ecclesiastical trust is a trust organized around sincerely held religious beliefs and purposes — built to hold and steward assets in service of those beliefs. "Ecclesiastical" means of the church. Your structure layers a religious-purpose organization (the 508(c)(1)(a) shell) over a private, irrevocable trust.

The trust is its own legal person. It has its own name, its own EIN, its own bank accounts, its own contracts. The trustee signs as fiduciary — not as the individual who happened to create it. The role you act in when you sign determines the legal character of the act: trustee-you signs differently than beneficiary-you receives.

**The seat diagram is simple:** every trust has three seats — Grantor, Trustee, Beneficiary. They cannot be collapsed into one another, but they do not require three separate human beings. One person can fill more than one seat *so long as the seats stay distinct on paper.* This is how a family trust works across generations, and it's why governance (keeping the seats clean) matters more than anything.

---

## 2. "Is this a church in a box? Is this legal?"

Let's be plain. The 508(c)(1)(a) exemption is not something anyone invented, and it isn't a tax dodge. Here's the actual law:

> **26 USC §508(c)(1)(a):** "Exceptions. — Subsection (a) shall not apply to — (1) churches, their integrated auxiliaries, and conventions or associations of churches."

Section 508(a) requires most organizations to file Form 1023 to be recognized as tax-exempt. §508(c)(1)(a) says churches **don't have to**. The word "shall" is a command. Where the exemption applies, it is **automatic and unconditional.**

Why? Because the First Amendment requires it. Congress can't establish religion, and it can't restrict the free exercise of religion. If the IRS could decide which churches are "legitimate" and which are tax dodges, that puts the government exactly where the First Amendment forbids it. The exemption is the **boundary the Constitution draws.**

The Supreme Court said so: **Walz v. Tax Commission, 397 U.S. 664 (1970)** held that exempting churches creates *less* government entanglement than taxing them. Exemption isn't generosity; it's separation.

---

## 3. "Isn't this just avoiding taxes?"

Understand the difference between a **motive** and a **qualification**.

The Code doesn't ask why. §508(c)(1)(a) doesn't say "churches that weren't formed for tax reasons are exempt." It says the filing requirement "shall not apply" to churches — period. The IRS has no authority to judge your sincerity before granting what the law makes automatic.

Here's the trap in that question: it assumes a 508 organization exists *only* to dodge taxes. But a religious organization that holds and stewards assets for religious and family purposes — with trustees bound by fiduciary duty — is performing a religious function. Structuring to minimize tax is not the same as evading. And the First Amendment protects the **organization itself**, not just its motives.

---

## 4. "The code doesn't define 'church.' Isn't that a problem?"

The reverse is a problem — for the state, not for you.

The Internal Revenue Code deliberately doesn't define "church." The IRS uses **14 criteria** (from *American Guidance Foundation*, 1959) as *guidelines, not requirements.* No court has held an organization must meet all 14. The absence of a statutory definition means the term is *broader* than most assume — and the First Amendment prohibits the government from imposing a narrow, externally invented definition of what counts as religion.

If someone says "that's not a real church," ask: *which statute defines 'church'?* There isn't one. They're applying a personal standard, not the code.

**A detail worth knowing:** these 14 criteria weren't drawn from a steeple-and-pews church. They were shaped partly by secular structures. The IRS's own framework for identifying an ecclesiastical organization is intentionally broad — it doesn't require a congregation in pews or a denominational affiliation.

---

## 5. "Can it be just my family?"

No statute requires a minimum membership. The First Amendment doesn't require a minimum congregation. **No court** has denied church status solely because the organization is a family.

A family that gathers around shared spiritual beliefs, commits resources to those beliefs, and governs them through trustees with fiduciary duties **is** a religious organization. Structure counts — size doesn't.

---

## 6. "How do I not run into 'inurement'?"

"Inurement" is the technical scare. Here's what it actually is:

Under **§501(c)(3)** law, "inurement" means the net earnings of a tax-exempt org benefiting a private individual (a founder, officer) — a charity used as a personal piggy bank loses its exemption.

**But the §501(c)(3) inurement rules don't import into §508(c)(1)(a).** A 508 organization never applied for 501(c)(3) — it's exempt under a *different section.* Those conditions attach to 501(c)(3) recognition.

And even if a fair-minded inurement test were applied, a properly structured trust clears it:

- **Trustees hold fiduciary duties, not ownership.** They manage assets for the trust's purposes; they don't own them.
- **Reasonable compensation is not inurement.** Churches pay their pastors. Administering the trust for reasonable documented compensation is a legitimate expense.
- **The purpose test is what matters.** Is the organization operated exclusively for exempt religious purposes? A trust stewarding assets for religious and family purposes, under fiduciary trustees, is.

WingPoint trusts are drawn with purpose limitations, trustee duties, and explicit inurement prohibitions in the trust document itself — so this question has a clean answer on its face.

---

## 7. "Do I have to file Form 1023?"

No. §508(c)(1)(a) says "shall not apply." A command.

The IRS's own **Publication 1828:**
> "Churches that meet the requirements of section 501(c)(3) are automatically considered tax exempt and are not required to apply for and obtain recognition of exempt status from the IRS."

Many churches file 1023 *anyway* — not because they must, but because the determination letter gives donors a document showing their contribution is deductible. That's convenience, not a requirement. A church that never files is just as exempt by law.

---

## 8. "EIN, bank, state — who do I need for that?"

- **EIN:** File Form SS-4. For a 508(c)(1)(a) ecclesiastical organization, check **"Trust"** on the entity-type line — not "church," because 508 status is about tax exemption, not legal form. An EIN doesn't require a 501(c)(3) letter.
- **Bank account:** A 508 church can open an account with its EIN. No 501(c)(3) determination letter required. If a bank demands one, they're mistaken — the agency itself says the exemption is automatic.
- **Property tax:** Many states exempt property owned and used by religious organizations. That's a **separate application per county** — and don't confuse *federal automatic exemption* with *state applications.* Your federal exemption doesn't depend on state approval.

---

## 9. "What happens if the IRS contacts me?"

Don't panic. Don't ignore it. Don't enter a conversation alone.

**Know the shield.** Congress added **§7611** in 1984 — a high procedural bar to any church tax inquiry. For a church, the IRS must:
1. Have a high-level official reasonably believe — in writing, on the facts — the org may not qualify;
2. Give written notice explaining the concerns;
3. Offer a conference before any examination;
4. Complete the exam within **2 years**;
5. Get regional-counsel written approval before any adverse determination.

That's a far higher bar than a normal nonprofit faces — and it's why the IRS can't simply raid a church on a tax theory.

**And when the IRS talks to you — §7521 gives you rights:**
- **Right to representation:** say "I want to consult with my representative" and the IRS **must suspend** the interview;
- **Right to have counsel handle it:** with a power of attorney, counsel acts for you;
- **Right to record:** you may audio-record any in-person interview, with advance notice, at your expense;
- **Right to be informed:** the IRS must explain the process and rights before it begins.

**The posture: respond in writing, keep copies, don't volunteer, follow the process.** If the inquiry is serious, take counsel experienced in church tax matters. This is not WingPoint selling services — it's the procedural reality.

---

## 10. "This can't survive a real court challenge, can it?"

This is where the doctrine goes deeper than the tax code — and where the law is genuinely on the religious organization's side.

Since *Walz*, the Supreme Court has built a distinct **church autonomy doctrine** — a third line of First Amendment precedent, separate from free-exercise and from establishment cases. It rests on both religion clauses and protects the **internal governance of a religious organization** as a zone where the state does not intrude. Chief Justice Roberts described it in *Hosanna-Tabor* (2012):

> "Independence in matters of faith and doctrine and closely linked matters of internal government."

The doctrine protects **five areas** where a religious organization is sovereign:
1. The determination and interpretation of religious doctrine;
2. The organization's polity and governance structure (including canons and bylaws);
3. The hiring, training, supervision, and removal of clergy and leaders with religious functions;
4. The determination of membership and good standing;
5. Internal communications about the first four.

The **ministerial exception** that flows from this doctrine gives religious organizations a **categorical immunity** from certain employment suits. In *Our Lady of Guadalupe* (2019), the Court reaffirmed and expanded it: what matters is the *actual job functions*, not a fixed list of titles.

**Here's the honest consequence for your trust:** the secular authority's power over a genuinely religious organization — its doctrine, its ministers, its discipline, its internal decision-making — is genuinely circumscribed. That's settled law, and it's the real, defensible "power" of an ecclesiastical structure. When paired with §508(c)(1)(a) tax exemption and §7611 procedural shields, you have a coherent architecture.

**The warning on the side:** this power is real *because it is bounded*. Church autonomy protects what a religious organization genuinely does — doctrine, worship, ministry, internal governance. It does **not** turn a trust into a license to ignore the tax code. The courts are intensely skeptical of a 508 or church structure that is *only* a tax dodge. The authority has always been and remains: keep the religious substance real, and the protection is real. Force it as pure tax avoidance and the doctrine will not carry you — and overreach can *narrow* the doctrine for everyone.

---

## The mistake most people make

The people whose work you've read in this course didn't act overnight — Hendrickson studied for years; Champion documented for seventeen; Banister investigated for two before resigning. **Understanding comes before action.** The emotional weight of this is real. Give yourself time.

When you're ready, the path is: **Understand → Document your position → Correct your records → Structure properly → Govern well.** Steps four and five are where WingPoint's trust packages and trustee training make it real. A trust without a trained trustee is a liability, not a shield.

---

## Two things to hold onto

1. **The exemption is mandatory, automatic, and constitutional.** §508(c)(1)(a) says "shall not apply." That's a command, a boundary — not discretion.
2. **The trust gives structure to freedom.** The 508 shell is the roof; the trust is the framework beneath it — separate, governed, fiduciary, purposeful.

And the spine running under the whole thing — **claim → authority → comparison → validation → calibrated conclusion.** Every point above has a statute or a case behind it. Read the code. Verify. This isn't empowerment theater — it's the door that was always open, that most people walk past because it looks too small.
