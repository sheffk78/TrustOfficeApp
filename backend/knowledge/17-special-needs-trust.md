# Special Needs Trust

## What It Is

A Special Needs Trust (SNT) is a trust designed to hold assets for a person with disabilities without disqualifying them from means-tested government benefits like Supplemental Security Income (SSI) and Medicaid. Instead of replacing those benefits, the trust pays for supplemental needs — things the government programs do not cover — so the beneficiary's quality of life improves without losing eligibility. Because the rules differ sharply depending on whose money funds the trust and when it was established, getting the structure right from day one is critical.

## Key Governance Requirements

- The trust must be established correctly under the correct sub-type: a third-party SNT (funded by someone other than the beneficiary) or a first-party SNT (also called a d4A trust, funded with the beneficiary's own assets, such as a personal injury settlement). Third-party trusts are far more flexible; d4A trusts carry a mandatory Medicaid payback requirement at death.
- The trust must contain an explicit payback provision for first-party (d4A) trusts, stating that the state Medicaid agency will be reimbursed for all benefits paid during the beneficiary's lifetime before any remainder goes to other heirs. Third-party trusts do not require this payback.
- The sole benefit rule must be followed: every dollar spent from the trust must be for the sole benefit of the disabled beneficiary. Distributions to or for the benefit of anyone else can trigger benefit disqualification and tax issues.
- The trustee must understand exactly what counts as a supplemental (non-countable) expense versus income or a resource under SSI/Medicaid rules. Food and shelter distributions are treated as "in-kind support and maintenance" and reduce SSI dollar-for-dollar, so they require careful timing and documentation.
- The trust document should expressly authorize the trustee to consider or roll over funds into an ABLE account (up to the annual contribution limit), which can be a simpler, more flexible alternative for smaller amounts.

## Distribution Rules

- Distributions must supplement, not supplant, government benefits. The trustee should never pay for items SSI or Medicaid already covers, because doing so can reduce or eliminate those benefits.
- Cash distributions directly to the beneficiary are almost always a mistake — they count as unearned income and can suspend SSI benefits. Instead, pay vendors and service providers directly.
- Food and shelter distributions (rent, mortgage, groceries, utilities) are subject to the SSI "presumed maximum value" rule and reduce the monthly SSI payment; track these carefully and document the reasoning.
- At the beneficiary's death, a first-party (d4A) trust must repay the state Medicaid agency before any remainder distribution to heirs; a third-party trust passes the remainder to successor beneficiaries as directed in the trust document.

## Common Pitfalls

- Funding a trust with the beneficiary's own money but using third-party trust language — this creates a defective d4A trust with no payback provision, which can disqualify the beneficiary and expose the trustee to personal liability.
- Distributing cash directly to the beneficiary, which counts as income and can suspend or reduce SSI benefits for the month — sometimes for months.
- Ignoring the payback requirement at death, leaving the state unpaid and exposing the trustee to a clawback claim against remainder beneficiaries.
- Overlooking ABLE accounts as a simpler, tax-advantaged alternative for amounts under the annual contribution cap, especially when the beneficiary works and wants to save earned income without losing benefits.
