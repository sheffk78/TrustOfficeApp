# Totten Trust

## What It Is

A Totten trust is a simple bank account trust created by depositing money in a bank account in the trustee's name "as trustee for" a named beneficiary. It is one of the oldest and simplest trust forms recognized in common law. The account is payable on death to the beneficiary, meaning the beneficiary has no access to the funds while the trustee is alive, but automatically receives the balance when the trustee dies. Because of its simplicity, a Totten trust does not require a separate tax return and is easy to set up with almost any bank.

## Key Governance Requirements

- **Bank account format.** The account title must clearly indicate a trust relationship, typically phrased as "[Your Name], Trustee for [Beneficiary Name]." The specific wording matters for the trust to be recognized under state law.
- **Revocable during the trustee's lifetime.** The trustee (the depositor) retains full control and can withdraw funds, change the beneficiary, or close the account at any time before death. No notice to the beneficiary is required.
- **No separate tax return needed.** Because the trustee retains complete control and the account is revocable, the income is reported on the trustee's personal tax return (Form 1040), not on a separate fiduciary return (Form 1041). The trust is treated as a grantor trust for tax purposes.
- **Passes outside probate.** On the trustee's death, the balance transfers directly to the named beneficiary without going through probate. This is one of the key advantages over a testamentary trust.
- **State law recognition.** Most U.S. states recognize Totten trusts, but a few treat them differently. The trustee should confirm the account format is valid in the state where the account is held.
- **No formal trust agreement required.** The bank's signature card and account title serve as the trust instrument. There is no separate trust document to draft or file.

## Distribution Rules

- **Automatic transfer at death.** The beneficiary receives the entire account balance upon the trustee's death by presenting a death certificate to the bank. No court order or probate process is needed.
- **No distributions before death.** The beneficiary has no right to the funds during the trustee's lifetime. The trustee can spend the money freely; nothing is set aside or protected for the beneficiary.
- **Revocable at any time.** The trustee can change the beneficiary or revoke the trust entirely without the beneficiary's consent or knowledge.
- **Entire balance passes.** Unlike trusts with incremental or conditional distributions, a Totten trust pays the full remaining balance to the beneficiary outright.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Confusing with a payable-on-death account.** A Totten trust and a POD bank account achieve similar results but have different legal origins. Some states treat them identically; others do not. Confirm which one your bank is actually creating.
- **Forgetting to update the beneficiary.** Life changes (marriage, divorce, death of the named beneficiary) can leave the account going to someone unintended. If the named beneficiary predeceases the trustee, the account may pass to the trustee's estate through probate, defeating the purpose.
- **Assuming it covers more than the bank account.** A Totten trust only governs the specific bank account. It cannot hold real estate, securities, or other assets. Trustees sometimes mistakenly believe the Totten trust format extends to their brokerage or investment accounts.
- **Overlooking creditor access.** Because the trustee retains full control, the account is reachable by the trustee's creditors during life. It offers no asset protection, unlike some irrevocable trusts.
