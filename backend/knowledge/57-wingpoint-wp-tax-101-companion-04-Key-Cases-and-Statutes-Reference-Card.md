## Supreme Court Cases

### Brushaber v. Union Pacific Railroad Co.
**Citation:** 240 U.S. 1 (1916)

**What it established:** The 16th Amendment did **not** create a new class of direct tax. The income tax remains an excise — an indirect tax on privileged activities, not a direct tax on all earnings. The Amendment changed nothing about the constitutional requirements for direct vs. indirect taxes.

**Why it matters:** This is the single most important case in the course. If the income tax is an excise (the Court says it is), then it only applies to the objects of an excise — privileged federal activities — not to private earnings regardless of source.

**Key quote:** "Taxation on income is in its nature an excise, entitled to be enforced as such."

---

### Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co.
**Citation:** 157 U.S. 429 (1895), 158 U.S. 601 (1895 reargument)

**What it established:** A tax on income from property is a direct tax and must be apportioned. The Court struck down the 1894 income tax as unconstitutional because it taxed property income without apportionment.

**Why it matters:** This is the case that prompted the 16th Amendment. Understanding Pollock is essential to understanding what the 16th Amendment actually changed (and did not change).

---

### Flint v. Stone Tracy Co.
**Citation:** 220 U.S. 107 (1911)

**What it established:** Excise taxes are taxes on **privilege** — the privilege of doing business in a corporate form — not taxes on property itself. The tax is measured by income but imposed on the activity.

**Why it matters:** Clarifies the excise principle. The tax is on the privileged activity, with income as the measurement, not the thing being taxed.

---

### Eisner v. Macomber
**Citation:** 252 U.S. 189 (1920)

**What it established:** "Income" means gain derived from capital, labor, or both. A stock dividend that changes nothing about the shareholder's economic position is not "income." Not every receipt is income under the 16th Amendment.

**Why it matters:** The Court defines "income" more narrowly than the IRS applies it. The definition matters for what qualifies as taxable.

---

### Stanton v. Baltic Mining Co.
**Citation:** 240 U.S. 103 (1916)

**What it established:** The 16th Amendment conferred no new power of taxation. It merely prevented the application of the apportionment requirement to income taxes that were already indirect (excise) taxes.

**Why it matters:** Another reinforcement that the Amendment changed the apportionment rule, not the nature of the tax.

---

### Hodges v. United States
**Citation:** 203 U.S. 1 (1906)

**What it established:** The 14th and 15th Amendments operate **solely on state action**, not individual action. The federal government remains one of enumerated powers even after the Civil War amendments. The 10th Amendment retains its vitality.

**Why it matters:** Limits on federal power are real and recognized by the Court. The existence of amendments does not create unlimited federal authority.

**Key quote:** "Notwithstanding the adoption of the Thirteenth, Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments, the National Government still remains one of enumerated powers, and the Tenth Amendment is not shorn of its vitality."

---

### Burnet v. Brooks
**Citation:** 288 U.S. 378 (1933)

**What it established:** The two great classes of taxation (direct and indirect) include every form of tax appropriate to sovereignty. The income tax, as an excise, falls in the indirect class.

**Why it matters:** Confirms the Brushaber holding and the two-class framework for all federal taxation.

## Key Statutes

### 26 USC §7701(a)(26) — "Trade or Business"
"The term 'trade or business' includes the performance of the functions of a public office."

**Why it matters:** The narrowest and most consequential definition in the code. Tax liability throughout the IRC is repeatedly tied to "trade or business" activity. If your work is not the performance of a public office function, the statutory chain that creates liability may not reach you.

---

### 26 USC §7701(a)(14) — "Taxpayer"
"The term 'taxpayer' means any person subject to any internal revenue tax."

**Why it matters:** "Taxpayer" is not everyone. It is a person subject to a specific tax. If you are not subject to the tax (because your activities fall outside the excise scope), you are not a "taxpayer" under the code's own definition.

---

### 26 USC §7701(a)(30) — "U.S. Person"
Defines U.S. person to include: citizens, residents, domestic partnerships, domestic corporations, domestic estates and trusts, and "any other person" meeting the definition.

**Why it matters:** The complement — anyone not meeting this definition is a "foreign person." The code recognizes two categories. Operating in a non-U.S. person capacity is structurally different from operating as a U.S. person.

---

### 26 USC §3401(a) — "Wages"
Defines wages with 23 enumerated exceptions. Agricultural labor, domestic service, ministerial services, nonresident alien compensation, tips under $20/month, and more are **not** wages under this section.

**Why it matters:** If your compensation is not "wages" under §3401, no withholding obligation arises under §3402.

---

### 26 USC §3401(c) — "Employee"
Defines employee for withholding purposes. A narrower category than common law employee.

**Why it matters:** The withholding system assumes an employer-employee relationship under these specific definitions. Not all work arrangements qualify.

---

### 26 USC §3402(a) — Withholding Requirement
"Every employer making payment of wages shall deduct and withhold upon such wages a tax."

**Why it matters:** The requirement only applies to "wages" as defined in §3401. If the remuneration is not wages, there is no withholding obligation.

---

### 26 USC §3402(n) — Exemption from Withholding
No withholding required if the employee had no income tax liability last year and expects none this year.

**Why it matters:** The code itself provides a mechanism for claiming exemption from withholding. This is not a loophole — it is a statutory provision.

---

### 26 USC §3402(d) — Employer Not Liable if Employee Pays
If the employer fails to withhold but the employee pays the tax, the tax "shall not be collected from the employer."

**Why it matters:** The system has built-in safety valves. Even if withholding was required but not done, the employer is not liable if the tax is ultimately paid.

---

### 26 USC §508(c)(1)(a) — Mandatory Exception
Churches and religious organizations that meet §501(c)(3) requirements are "automatically considered tax exempt and are not required to apply for and obtain recognition of tax-exempt status from the IRS."

**Why it matters:** This is the foundation for the 508(c)(1)(a) trust structure. It is not asking permission — it is a mandatory exception already written into the code.

---

### 26 USC §7611 — Church Audit Procedures
Imposes strict procedural requirements before the IRS can audit a church. The IRS has limited authority to examine religious organizations.

**Why it matters:** A trust organized under 508(c)(1)(a) benefits from these audit protections. The government's own code limits its ability to investigate.

---

### 26 USC §676 — Revocation of Trust
A power to revoke is treated as a power of the grantor. Relevant to the revocation and re-vesting process in trust restructuring.

**Why it matters:** The code recognizes that trust powers can be revoked. This is relevant to the Freedom Law Group's revocation of election arguments.

---

### U.S. Constitution, Art. I, §9, Cl. 4 — Direct Tax Clause
"No Capitation, or other direct, Tax shall be laid, unless in Proportion to the Census or Enumeration."

**Why it matters:** The constitutional constraint on direct taxes. If the income tax is a direct tax, it must be apportioned. The Supreme Court has held it is not — it is an excise.

---

### U.S. Constitution, Art. I, §8, Cl. 1 — Taxing Power
"Congress shall have Power To lay and collect Taxes, Duties, Imposts and Excises."

**Why it matters:** The general taxing power. Excises are included. Direct taxes are subject to the separate apportionment rule.

---

### 16th Amendment
"The Congress shall have power to lay and collect taxes on incomes, from whatever source derived, without apportionment among the several States, and without regard to any census or enumeration."

**Why it matters:** What it says and what people think it says are different. The Supreme Court held this Amendment did not create a new tax — it removed the apportionment requirement from income taxes that were already indirect (excise) taxes.
