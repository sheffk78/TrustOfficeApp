# UTC Doctrine 03 — Distributions: Spending the Trust's Money

> ⚠️ **Scope note — the Code does not define your distribution standard.**
> Whatever standard your trust document names (including HEMS) is your document's term, not the Code's. The Code's rules here are about the *discretion* a trustee exercises under that standard and about what happens to the money when the trust ends. Read the distribution standard in your document.

**UTC anchor:** art. 8 — ss. 813(c), 814, 816(18), 817; art. 4 — ss. 410–417; art. 5 — ss. 501, 504, 506; art. 10 — s. 1009

## Why this matters

Two things trustees ask about constantly: how much discretion do I actually have, and what happens to the money when the trust ends. Those answers live in the Code's discretion rule and its termination rule, and this section sets them out.

## The discretion is real, and it is also constrained

Section 814 is the section that most directly answers the question a trustee asks when a beneficiary pushes.

Subsection (a) opens the opposite way most people expect. Notwithstanding the breadth of discretion granted in the trust terms — *including terms like "absolute," "sole," or "uncontrolled"* — the trustee shall exercise a discretionary power in good faith and in accordance with the trust's terms and purposes and the beneficiaries' interests.

Sweeping language in a document is not a blank check. A trustee with "absolute discretion" still acts in good faith, follows the trust's purposes, and serves the beneficiaries' interests. This is the Code's answer to the family member who says "the trust says I have total control."

Subsection (b) adds two hard limits, unless the trust's terms expressly say otherwise.

First: a person other than a settlor who is both a beneficiary and a trustee of a trust letting the trustee make discretionary distributions for personal benefit may exercise that power only in accordance with an ascertainable standard. Section 103(2) defines an ascertainable standard as one relating to an individual's health, education, support, or maintenance. A trustee-beneficiary drawing for their own support can only draw against that standard.

Second: a trustee may not use a discretionary distribution power to satisfy a legal obligation of support that the trustee personally owes another person. A trustee cannot use trust money to pay their own legal support obligations.

Subsection (c) is the workaround: a power limited or prohibited by (b) may be exercised by a majority of the remaining trustees whose own power is not limited, and if all are, the court may appoint a special fiduciary.

Subsection (d) lists three exceptions — a power held by the settlor's spouse as trustee of a trust for which a marital deduction was previously allowed; any trust during a period it may be revoked or amended by its settlor; and a trust whose contributions qualify for the annual exclusion under the Internal Revenue Code. Those are tax-driven; confirm with a CPA.

## What a distribution decision is worth on the record

Section 813(c) requires the annual report to go to the distributees or permissible distributees of trust income or principal, and to other beneficiaries who request it. That is a wider net than "the people getting money." A remainder beneficiary who asks for a report gets one.

Section 816(18) is the lending power: a trustee may make loans out of trust property, including loans to a beneficiary, on terms the trustee considers fair and reasonable, with a lien on future distributions for repayment. Section 816(19) allows pledging trust property to guarantee loans made by others to the beneficiary. Both carry conditions the trustee must be able to explain.

## What happens at the end

Section 817 is the termination-distribution rule, and it has three parts.

Subsection (a) offers a fast path. Upon termination or partial termination, the trustee *may* send the beneficiaries a proposal for distribution. The right to object terminates if the beneficiary does not object within 30 days after the proposal was sent — but only if the proposal informed the beneficiary of the right to object and of the time allowed. That condition is not a formality: a proposal that omits the objection right and the window does not close the objection period.

Subsection (b) is the mandate. Upon an event terminating or partially terminating the trust, the trustee shall proceed expeditiously to distribute the trust property to the persons entitled to it, subject to the right to retain a reasonable reserve for debts, expenses, and taxes. "Expeditiously" and "reasonable reserve" are the operative words: the reserve is for paying obligations, not for covering a fight.

Subsection (c) limits releases. A release by a beneficiary of a trustee from liability for breach of trust is invalid to the extent it was induced by improper conduct, or the beneficiary did not know their rights or the material facts relating to the breach. Section 1009 says the same from the trustee's side.

A beneficiary's signature on a release is strong protection, but not armor against a claim that the trustee obtained it improperly or kept the beneficiary in the dark.

## Modification and termination of the trust itself

The distributive terms of a trust can be changed, but only through Article 4's procedures, all of which route through a court.

Section 410 sets the baseline: a trust terminates to the extent it is revoked or expires per its terms, no purpose of the trust remains to be achieved, or the purposes have become unlawful, contrary to public policy, or impossible to achieve.

Section 411 covers modification or termination of a noncharitable irrevocable trust by consent. On petition, if the court finds the settlor and all beneficiaries consent, it shall approve even if that is inconsistent with a material purpose of the trust. The court may also approve without full consent if the trust could have been modified had all beneficiaries consented and the non-consenting beneficiary's interests would be adequately protected.

Section 412 lets the court modify or terminate where circumstances not anticipated by the settlor mean it will further the trust's purposes, so far as practicable following the settlor's probable intention, and modify administrative terms where continuation would be impracticable or wasteful.

Section 413 is cy pres, for charitable trusts only: where a charitable purpose becomes unlawful, impracticable, impossible, or wasteful, the trust does not fail, the property does not revert to the settlor, and the court may direct application consistent with the settlor's charitable purposes.

Section 414 covers the uneconomic trust — property insufficient to justify the cost of administration. The Code's text carries a bracketed dollar figure for the trustee's self-executing route; the amount varies by state. **Confirm the current figure in your state with an attorney.** The court's power to modify, terminate, remove the trustee, and appoint a different one carries no threshold at all.

Section 415 is reformation to correct mistakes: the court may reform even unambiguous terms to conform to the settlor's intention, on clear and convincing evidence of intention and of a mistake of fact or law.

Section 416 allows modification to achieve the settlor's tax objectives where the modification is not contrary to the settlor's probable intention, and may be retroactive. Section 417 allows combining or dividing trusts, after notice to the qualified beneficiaries, where the result does not impair any beneficiary's rights or adversely affect the trust's purposes.

## Creditor reach on distributions

Section 501 lets a court authorize a beneficiary's creditor or assignee to reach the beneficiary's interest, to the extent that interest is not subject to a spendthrift provision. Section 504 says a creditor may not compel a distribution subject to the trustee's discretion — even if expressed as a standard, and even if the trustee abused it — but where the trustee failed to comply with the standard or abused it, a court may order a distribution to satisfy a support judgment against the beneficiary's child, spouse, or former spouse, up to what the trustee should have distributed. Section 506 lets a creditor reach a *mandatory* distribution the trustee failed to make within a reasonable time after the designated date.

### What this means for a trustee

- Read your trust's distribution clause and identify the standard by name. The Code applies different rules to discretionary powers than to mandatory ones, and s. 814's limits apply to the discretionary ones.
- If you are also a beneficiary, note that s. 814(b)(1) limits distributions to you to an ascertainable standard unless the document expressly says otherwise.
- Before any distribution, write the reason down. A seven-part distribution record is a good shape; the Code's anchor for why it matters is that a beneficiary can only release you if they knew their rights and the material facts.
- If you are terminating a trust, use the s. 817(a) proposal route carefully — it only works if the proposal states the right to object and the 30-day window. Otherwise work from s. 817(b): proceed expeditiously, keeping a reasonable reserve.
- Never treat a signed release as the end of the matter. It can be invalidated for improper inducement or for the beneficiary not knowing their rights.
- Check every mandatory distribution against s. 506. A required distribution you did not make is reachable by a beneficiary's creditor.
- Treat loans to beneficiaries under s. 816(18) as distributions in everything but name, and document the fairness of the terms and the lien on future distributions.

### Common question we get

**"My trust says I have absolute discretion. Do I really still have to follow it?"**
Yes, and this is the most important thing in this file. Section 814(a) says that even a power described as "absolute," "sole," or "uncontrolled" must be exercised in good faith and in accordance with the trust's terms and purposes and the beneficiaries' interests. Sweeping language in a document does not switch off the duty, and it does not authorize something the document's other terms forbid.

**"Can I pay a beneficiary's legal support obligation out of the trust?"**
Not if that obligation is one you personally owe. Section 814(b)(2) prohibits using a discretionary distribution power to satisfy a legal obligation of support that the trustee personally owes another person. If the obligation belongs to someone else, the question becomes whether the trust's distribution standard covers it — a document question. The support-judgment carve-out in s. 504(c) shows the Code pays close attention to this area generally.

**"The beneficiary signed a release. Am I safe now?"**
Better protected, but not automatically safe. Under s. 817(c) and s. 1009, a release is invalid to the extent it was induced by improper conduct of the trustee, or the beneficiary did not know their rights or the material facts relating to the breach when they signed. The cleanest version is a release taken after the beneficiary has had the accounting, has had time to ask questions, and has been told in plain terms what they are giving up.
