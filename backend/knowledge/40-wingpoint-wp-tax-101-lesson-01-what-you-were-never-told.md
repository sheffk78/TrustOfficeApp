# Lesson 1: What You Were Never Told

**Course:** WP-TAX-101 — The Tax Question  
**Lesson:** 1 of 8

Pete Hendrickson filed taxes for twenty years without questioning it. Then he read §3401(a). All of it — including the twenty-three specific exceptions listed after "wages means."

He sat there for ten minutes. Not because it was complicated. Because it was simple, and nobody had ever shown it to him.

That's where this course starts: with what the law actually says versus what everybody told you it says.

## What This Course Is — And Isn't

This course shows you the structure of the tax code, the definitions it uses, the constitutional framework that shapes it, how the system obtains consent, and the practical questions that arise when you read the law for yourself. That foundation is what most people never get.

We're not your lawyer or your CPA. We're just showing you the map. What you do with it is between you, your conscience, and anyone you choose to consult.

---

## What "Wages" Actually Means

You were told "wages" means all the money you earn from working. That's what most people assume.

26 USC §3401(a) defines wages as remuneration paid by an employer to an employee for services. Then it lists **twenty-three specific exceptions** — categories of compensation explicitly excluded from the definition.

Twenty-three. Congress enumerated nearly two dozen types of compensation that don't count as "wages" under this section. If you work at a hardware store, fixing cars for private customers, or running a dental practice — the money you earn doesn't fit this definition. The exclusions exist because the definition is narrow.

Hendrickson explains this in *Cracking the Code*:

> "The term 'wages' as used in the subtitle means only those specific items of remuneration which are both paid to a specifically-defined 'employee' by a specifically-defined 'employer' and not otherwise excluded."

Read it yourself: [26 USC §3401(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/3401)

---

## What "Employee" Actually Means

You were told "employee" means anyone who works for someone. That's the common English meaning. The statutory meaning is different.

26 USC §3401(c) defines "employee" with a specific, narrow reference — pointing to federal employment as defined in Title 5 U.S.C. §2105: officers and individuals appointed to the civil service by the President, Congress, or federal authority. Not "any natural person who performs labor for compensation." Not "anyone who gets a paycheck."

The common English word and the statutory word share a spelling. They do not share a definition. Your boss at the hardware store calls you an employee. The code does not — unless you hold a federal position.

---

## What "Employer" Actually Means

26 USC §3401(d) defines "employer" to match "employee." If someone doesn't have statutory "employees," they're not a statutory "employer" — regardless of what they're called in everyday language.

This means your boss at the hardware store isn't the "employer" the code describes. The company that signs your paychecks in the private sector isn't a statutory "employer" unless it has statutory "employees" — and as you just read, most private-sector workers don't fit that definition.

The definitions lock together. "Wages" flows from "employee" which flows from "employer" which flows from "trade or business." Pull one thread and the whole fabric shifts.

---

## What "Trade or Business" Actually Means

This one catches people off guard. 26 USC §7701(a)(26) defines "trade or business" as:

> "the performance of the functions of a public office."

That's the entire definition. Not "any commercial activity." Not "whatever you do to earn a living." Not "your job." The performance of the functions of a public office.

If you don't hold a federal position, aren't employed by the federal government, and aren't exercising federal authority, this term doesn't describe what you do.

One sentence. Read it: [26 USC §7701(a)(26)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7701)

Hendrickson writes in *Cracking the Code*:

> "The term 'trade or business' means 'the performance of the functions of a public office.' It doesn't mean what most people assume it means. It means exactly what it says — and what it says is something very specific and very narrow."

---

## What "Taxpayer" Actually Means

26 USC §7701(a)(14) defines taxpayer as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax." A legal status — you fall into it or you don't based on whether a specific tax applies to you. “Person who earns money” is common usage, not the code's definition.

Most people assume they're taxpayers. The code says it depends on whether the tax applies to your specific activity. If you're not engaged in "the performance of the functions of a public office," and you're not receiving "wages" as the code defines them, then the status "taxpayer" may not attach to you for income tax purposes.

---

## The Pattern These Definitions Create

Read them together and a pattern emerges. Every definition points to federal activity. "Employee" means federal employee. "Employer" means someone with federal employees. "Wages" means compensation from federal employment. "Trade or business" means the performance of a public office. "Taxpayer" means someone subject to a tax that applies to specific activities.

The pattern isn't accidental. Statutory construction follows rules: when a statute defines a term, that definition controls the interpretation of every provision that uses it. The definitions are this specific because the tax is this limited — and that limitation exists because the founders believed some powers were beyond government's reach.

Go look at your last W-2. Box 1 says "Wages, tips, other compensation." Now look at §3401(a). Does what you do fit that definition? Not what you think you do — what you actually do, hour by hour, day by day.

## How You Were Conditioned to Believe Otherwise

Think about your first job. You were probably sixteen or eighteen. You showed up for orientation, excited, nervous. Someone from HR handed you a stack of papers — W-4, I-9, direct deposit form, employee handbook. You signed everything because that's what you do. You wanted to look competent. You didn't want to be the person who asks too many questions on day one. The W-4 said "Employee's Withholding Allowance Certificate" and you checked boxes without knowing those words had a legal meaning narrower than the English you spoke. That moment — your signature on that form — is where the system got your consent. You were sixteen. You didn't know you were making a legal election.

Nobody sat you down and said, "Here are the statutory definitions. Here's what each word means in the context of this code. Here's what you're certifying when you sign this form."

The W-4 is titled "Employee's Withholding Allowance Certificate." Under 26 C.F.R. §31.3402(p)-1, it's called a **"voluntary withholding agreement."** The word "voluntary" is in the regulation itself. Nobody tells you this when you're filling out new-hire paperwork.

Dave Champion puts it plainly in *Income Tax: Shattering the Myths*: the false narrative that everyone who earns a living owes income tax persists because Americans never read the law and blindly believe what they're told by others who have also never read the law.

The system operates on the assumption that you won't read the code. And most people don't — not because they're lazy, but because the system is designed so you never have to.

---

## The Practical Layer — How the System Gets Your Consent

Knowing the definitions is one thing. Understanding how you got inside them — even though you don't fit — is another.

The W-4 you signed on your first day of work. The Social Security Number you've used since childhood. The information returns your employer files every year. Each one is a mechanism that converts your private compensation into "wages" paid to a statutory "employee" by a statutory "employer." Not because the law requires it, but because you signed the forms without knowing what the words meant.

The system doesn't need to force you into the category. It needs you to *volunteer* into it. And most people do — not because they chose to, but because they never read the definitions on the forms they were signing.

This gap between what the code says and how most people live inside it was designed — around consent that was never fully informed.

---

## The Impersonation Problem

There's one more section you need to see — and it's not in the Internal Revenue Code at all.

**18 U.S.C. § 912** makes it a federal crime — punishable by fine and up to three years imprisonment — to falsely assume or pretend to be an officer or employee of the United States.

Read that again. *Falsely assume or pretend to be.*

Now put it alongside what you just read. If §3401(c) defines "employee" as a federal worker, and §7701(a)(14) defines "taxpayer" as someone subject to internal revenue laws — then a private citizen who files a return claiming the status of a federal employee under those statutes isn't just "playing along." They're impersonating a federal employee under the very code that criminalizes that impersonation.

You weren't trying to impersonate anyone. You were filling out a form HR handed you on your first day at a new job, using words nobody told you had narrow statutory meanings. The system doesn't need you to understand what you're signing — it just needs your signature.

Understanding the definitions is practical. If the word doesn't mean what you assumed it means, then filing as if it does is a legal act with legal consequences — and §912 is one of them.

---

## A Door That's Already Open

One more thing before you go further. The code itself carves out a specific category for people living out a religious or charitable mission. **508(c)(1)(a)** provides that certain organizations — churches and their integrated auxiliaries — are *automatically* tax-exempt without filing for recognition. No Form 1023. No 501(c)(3) application. No annual reporting requirement.

Why mention this now? Because when you start asking "am I really in the category the code describes?" the natural next question is "is there a category I *actually* belong to?" And the answer is: yes, there is.

Scripture tells us the way is narrow that leads to life. The tax code, written by flawed men, accidentally echoes this — the path out is a door that was always there, that most people walk past because it looks too small to matter.

We'll walk through it in detail in Lesson 7. For now, just know — the door exists, and the law put it there.
