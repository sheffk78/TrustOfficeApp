# UTC Doctrine 09 — The Notification Register: Every Notice, Report, and Deadline the Code Imposes

> ⚠️ **Scope note — this register is a planning tool, not a certificate of compliance.**
> Three cautions:
> 1. **State overlays are real.** Many states add registration, initial-notice, annual-accounting, and filing duties on top of the Code's baseline, and resolve the bracketed periods differently. Every state row needs local confirmation, and nothing here is the complete list.
> 2. **A complete row means an event was recorded**, not that the notice was legally sufficient.
> 3. **Do not auto-claim a deadline was met.** Rows complete by trustee action.

**UTC anchor:** art. 1 — ss. 103(13), 108(d), 109–111; art. 4 — ss. 410, 414, 417; art. 6 — ss. 602, 604; art. 7 — ss. 704, 705, 706, 707; art. 8 — ss. 813, 817; art. 10 — ss. 1005, 1013

## Why this matters

State compliance guidance covers initial, ongoing, and change notices; a transparency checklist tracks appointment, annual, responsiveness, denial, and recordkeeping items; a calendar holds the governance tasks. What is missing is one consolidated register: for each event, who must be told, what the notice must say, and where the Code says so. This section is that register.

## The building blocks

**Who counts as a recipient — s. 103(13).** A qualified beneficiary is one who is a distributee or permissible distributee of trust income or principal; would be one if the distributees' interests terminated without the trust terminating; or would be one if the trust terminated. In practice: current and remainder beneficiaries, and often the settlor while living. A contingent beneficiary whose interest has not vested may not qualify.

**How notice is given — s. 109.** Notice must be given in a manner reasonably suitable in the circumstances and reasonably calculated to result in actual receipt: first-class mail, personal delivery, delivery to a last known address, or a properly directed electronic message. It need not go to a person whose identity or location is unknown and not reasonably ascertainable.

**Who else gets it — s. 110.** Any other beneficiary who asks for notice must also receive notice whenever the Code requires notice to qualified beneficiaries.

**What the notice must contain — s. 108(d).** When a trustee changes the principal place of administration, it must give at least 30 days' notice to the qualified beneficiaries before the change takes effect, stating the reason. A beneficiary who objects in writing before that gets a judicial determination of whether the change serves the trust.

A service standard — 10 business days to answer beneficiary questions, and the objection window in the annual accounting cover letter — is a **practice standard, not a Code requirement**. Keep it separate from "promptly."

## How the register should look

**The row shape.** Event, date triggered, date due, date completed, who received it, how delivered, proof attached, notes. Proof means the artifact — the sent email, certified-mail receipt, signed release, filed report, minutes.

**The trigger, not the anniversary, starts most rows.** 1.1 runs from acceptance. 1.3 from the trustee's knowledge that the trust became irrevocable. 4.2 from the date the proposal was sent. A register scheduled by recurrence gets most Code clocks wrong.

**Three clocks per row.** A due date, a completed date, and a *deferred* date where an objection period is still open. Rows 1.3, 1.4, 2.3, 3.2, 4.3, and 4.4 all run past the notice.

**Separation of baseline from state overlay.** The Code rows above are the baseline. State additions — registration, initial-notice deadlines, annual-accounting requirements, tax filings, court-ordered accountings, and the state resolution of every bracketed period — live in a separate, labeled layer, because that is where the deadlines usually are.

**The "not a compliance certificate" banner.** In plain language, persistently: the register tracks Code-based events and is not a determination of legal compliance; state law adds requirements it may not include; a complete row means a recorded event, not a legally sufficient notice.

**No auto-completion.** Rows complete when a trustee records the event, never because a date passed.

## What this means for a trustee

- Build the register once, at acceptance, from the trust document, not a generic list. The document tells you who the qualified beneficiaries are, whether the trust names a successor, and what the notice provisions require.
- Record your qualified beneficiaries under s. 103(13). Every row in phases 1 through 4 depends on that list.
- Set the trigger date and the due date for every row. A Calendar holding only the due date loses the ability to tell whether the clock started right.
- Attach proof to every completed row. Delivery evidence separates a notice from an assertion of one.
- Keep objection and deferral windows visible. Row 4.2 is not done until the objection period has run.
- Add the state layer separately; confirm it with an attorney in that state.
- Keep the records after termination; the s. 1005 clock runs for years.

### Common question we get

**"Is this the whole list of things I have to notify people about?"**
No, and it should be said plainly. This is the Code's baseline. States add registration, initial-notice, periodic-accounting, and filing requirements on top, and some resolve the bracketed periods differently. The register tells you what the Code requires and when; your state's requirements are a separate, frequently stricter, list that has to come from an attorney there.

**"The beneficiary asked to be notified of everything. What do I do now?"**
Section 110 handles it, and it is a standing obligation rather than a one-time favor. Any other beneficiary who asks the trustee for notice must also receive notice whenever the Code requires notice to qualified beneficiaries. So the request pulls that beneficiary into the acceptance, existence, compensation-change, and place-of-administration notices, the annual report, and the termination steps.

**"What if I sent the notice but I don't know they received it?"**
That is a real and common problem, and it is why the register has a proof column. Section 109 requires a manner reasonably suitable in the circumstances and reasonably calculated to result in actual receipt: first-class mail, personal delivery, delivery to a last known address, or a properly directed electronic message. But the method alone does not create evidence. Save the delivery confirmation, certified-mail receipt, courier record, email with its delivery receipt, and the call memo. For the most consequential notices — 1.3 and 4.2 — consider a second method. Sufficiency is an attorney question.
