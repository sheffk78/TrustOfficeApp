# Qualified Terminal Interest Property (QTIP) Trust

## What It Is

A Qualified Terminal Interest Property trust, commonly called a QTIP trust, is an estate planning vehicle that qualifies assets for the federal estate tax marital deduction while allowing the grantor to control how those assets are ultimately distributed after the surviving spouse dies. The trust must give the surviving spouse a qualifying "terminal interest" — meaning all income from the property must be payable to that spouse for life, and no person other than the surviving spouse can receive any part of the principal during their lifetime. By making a QTIP election on the federal estate tax return (Form 706), the executor defers estate tax on the transferred assets until the surviving spouse's death, at which point the assets are includible in the surviving spouse's estate and taxed there. This makes the QTIP especially valuable in blended-family situations where the first spouse to die wants to provide for a current spouse during their lifetime but preserve the remaining principal for children from a prior marriage.

## Key Governance Requirements

- **QTIP election must be made on Form 706.** The executor of the first spouse's estate must affirmatively elect QTIP treatment on the federal estate tax return for the trust's assets to qualify for the marital deduction. The election is irrevocable once made and is made on a property-by-property basis, so the executor can elect QTIP for some assets and not others.

- **Surviving spouse must receive all income for life.** The trust instrument must require that all income from the QTIP property be paid to the surviving spouse at least annually for the remainder of their life. The income interest cannot be subject to any discretion that would allow the trustee to redirect income to another person.

- **No principal to anyone else during the spouse's lifetime.** No person other than the surviving spouse may receive any distribution of principal while the spouse is alive. The trustee may, however, be given discretionary power to distribute principal to or for the benefit of the surviving spouse.

- **Trustee cannot be granted power over the income.** If the trustee has the power to distribute principal to someone other than the surviving spouse, the QTIP election fails. The income right belongs exclusively to the surviving spouse, though the spouse can be given a limited power of appointment over the assets at death.

- **Proper documentation of the election and asset values.** The value of QTIP property must be accurately reported on Form 706, and the election must identify the specific property or trust. Inadequate valuation or election errors can result in disqualification and immediate estate tax exposure.

## Distribution Rules

- **Income distributions to the surviving spouse are mandatory and must be made at least annually.** The trustee has no discretion to accumulate income — all income generated by the QTIP assets must be distributed to the surviving spouse each year.

- **Principal distributions are discretionary to the surviving spouse only.** The trustee may be given authority to distribute principal for the surviving spouse's benefit (often under an ascertainable standard such as health, education, maintenance, or support), but never to or for the benefit of anyone else during the spouse's lifetime.

- **On the surviving spouse's death, remaining principal passes according to the grantor's instructions** — typically to children or other remainder beneficiaries named in the trust instrument. This is the mechanism that preserves assets for the grantor's chosen ultimate beneficiaries.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Failing to make the QTIP election on Form 706.** The marital deduction is not automatic — the executor must elect it. A missed or improperly made election can result in immediate estate tax on the trust assets at the first spouse's death, with limited relief available under certain IRS procedures.

- **Allowing principal distributions to someone other than the surviving spouse.** Even a single disqualifying distribution can void the QTIP election retroactively, pulling the assets back into the taxable estate. Trustees must understand the boundary of their distribution authority.

- **Accumulating income instead of distributing it.** Income must be paid out at least annually. A trustee who holds income in the trust, even temporarily or for investment purposes, risks violating the terminal interest requirement.

- **Poor valuation records.** Without accurate date-of-death and ongoing asset valuations, the trustee cannot properly report the QTIP property on Form 706 or the surviving spouse's later estate tax return, creating exposure to IRS adjustment and penalties.
