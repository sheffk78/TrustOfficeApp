# Land Trust

## What It Is

A Land Trust is a trust arrangement in which real property is held by a trustee in the trustee's own name, while the beneficial interest — the actual economic ownership — is retained by the beneficiary (usually the settlor). The public land records show only the trustee as titleholder, which provides a degree of anonymity for the real owner. Unlike a traditional revocable living trust, a land trust is specifically designed for holding real estate and is recognized only in a limited number of states. It is commonly used for privacy, estate planning simplification, and to allow beneficial interests to transfer without recording a deed.

## Key Governance Requirements

- The trust must be established and administered in a state that recognizes land trusts by statute or common law — most prominently Illinois, Florida, Indiana, and North Dakota. In these states, the land trust statute governs the arrangement; in other states, the structure may not be recognized or may be recharacterized as a standard trust, losing the anonymity benefits.
- Title to the property is held by the trustee in the trustee's own name. The trust agreement itself is typically unrecorded — the public record shows only the deed to the trustee — which is what provides the anonymity.
- The trust agreement must clearly separate legal title (held by the trustee) from beneficial interest (held by the beneficiary), and must specify the beneficiary's power of direction over the trustee regarding sale, refinance, or management of the property.
- The trustee's role is limited and ministerial in most land trusts: the trustee holds title and acts on the beneficiary's written direction, but does not manage the property, collect rents, or make investment decisions. Those functions remain with the beneficiary or a property manager the beneficiary designates.
- The beneficiary's power of direction should be exercised in writing, and the trustee should document each direction received and acted upon to maintain a clean chain of authority.

## Distribution Rules

- The beneficiary directs the trustee to sell, mortgage, or lease the property, and the trustee executes the documents — the beneficiary makes the real decisions; the trustee signs.
- Beneficial interest can be transferred by the beneficiary assigning their interest, often without recording a new deed. This makes gifting, estate planning, and partial sales simpler and private.
- Upon the beneficiary's death, the beneficial interest passes according to the beneficiary's designation in the trust agreement (similar to a payable-on-death designation), avoiding probate for the real property in many cases.
- Distributions of sale proceeds or rental income are directed by the beneficiary and executed by the trustee or a designated agent; the trustee is not obligated to investigate the beneficiary's instructions absent clear red flags.

## Common Pitfalls

- Assuming the land trust provides asset protection or liability shielding — it does not. The beneficiary remains personally liable for torts, debts, and obligations related to the property; the trust is an anonymity and title-holding vehicle, not a liability shield.
- Using a land trust in a state that does not recognize it (outside IL, FL, IN, ND and similar jurisdictions) — courts may recharacterize the arrangement, strip the anonymity, or create title defects.
- Failing to maintain the separation between the trustee's ministerial role and the beneficiary's decision-making power — if the trustee starts managing the property independently, the land trust structure can be challenged as a sham.
- Overlooking that beneficial interest transfers, while private, may still require disclosure on tax returns, FINCEN reports, or state real estate transfer statements — anonymity at the recorder's office does not mean anonymity from the IRS or other regulators.
