# Trust Compliance: Montana

## State Income Tax on Trusts

Montana imposes a state income tax on trusts with Montana nexus. Resident trusts and trusts with Montana-source income must file. Nonresident trusts with no Montana-source income generally have no filing obligation. Montana's graduated rates are notably lower than California's top rate of 13.3%: ordinary income is taxed at 4.7% on the first $20,500 and 5.9% above, with long-term capital gains at 3.0% and 4.1%. Montana allows a deduction for income distributed to beneficiaries, shifting the tax burden to beneficiaries who may be in lower brackets. Estimated tax payments are required if the trust expects to owe $500 or more, due quarterly on April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15.

## Filing Requirements

Montana fiduciary income tax is filed using Form FID-3, the Montana Income Tax Return for Estates and Trusts. The filing deadline is April 15 for calendar-year trusts, or the 15th day of the fourth month after the close of the fiscal year. Form FID-3 is due annually. Beginning with Tax Year 2024, resident estates and trusts must file Form FID-3 if they have a federal filing requirement or have positive Montana taxable income after considering any Montana additions to federal taxable income. An extension can be filed, which provides additional time to file but not to pay.

## Beneficiary Notice Requirements

Montana does not have a statutory requirement for trustees to provide initial notice to beneficiaries upon accepting trusteeship or when a trust becomes irrevocable. However, the Montana Uniform Trust Code (Title 72, Chapter 38, MCA) requires trustees to keep qualified beneficiaries reasonably informed about the administration of the trust and the material facts necessary for them to protect their interests. Trustees must respond to beneficiary requests for information and provide reports. While there is no mandatory initial notice deadline like California's 60-day requirement under Probate Code Â§16061.7, trustees should document all communications with beneficiaries to create a defensible record.

## Court Supervision

Court supervision of trusts in Montana is optional. A trust does not need to be registered with the court to be valid. Trustees may operate trusts in an unsupervised manner, which is the default for most revocable and irrevocable trusts. However, a trustee or beneficiary may petition the court for supervision if disputes arise or if judicial guidance is needed. Montana courts can also intervene when a trustee requests instructions or when a beneficiary seeks enforcement of their rights under the trust.

## Key State-Specific Rules

- **Spendthrift Protection:** Montana allows self-settled asset protection trusts, meaning a grantor can place assets in a trust for their own benefit and shield those assets from creditors, subject to certain limitations under MCA Title 72, Chapter 38.
- **Perpetuity Period:** Montana has abolished the rule against perpetuities for trusts created after the UTC adoption. Trusts can last indefinitely, providing flexibility for multi-generational wealth planning.
- **Community Property:** Montana is not a community property state, following the equitable distribution model for property division. This simplifies certain aspects of trust administration for married couples.
- **Trustee Duties:** Montana's UTC codifies the duties of loyalty, care, and impartiality under MCA Title 72, Chapter 38, Part 2. Trustees must administer the trust solely in the interests of the beneficiaries.
