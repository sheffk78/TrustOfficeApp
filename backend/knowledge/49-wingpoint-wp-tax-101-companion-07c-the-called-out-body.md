# The Called-Out Body: What an Ecclesiastical Organization Actually Is

**WingPoint · WP-TAX-101 companion lesson · Draft 7 (intro revision) for Jeff review**
**Builds on doctrine D-007 · August 21, 2026**

---

Most people have been taught to recognize a church by its outward signs: a building, a congregation meeting on Sunday, a denomination, a minister, perhaps a tax-exempt letter from the IRS. Those signs may be useful. But they do not answer the first question.

What is a church, in its essential sense: a building, an institution recognized by the state, or a body of people gathered around a shared religious purpose and capable of governing its own life?

The answer matters. It determines where authority rests, what "ecclesiastical" means, and why the law treats the internal affairs of a religious body differently from the affairs of an ordinary organization.

---

## Start with the Word

Sit with the word before you touch the law. "Ecclesiastical" comes from the Greek, *ekklesia*. A compound. *Ek*, out of. *Kaleo*, to call. To call out. A gathering of people called out and gathered around a purpose they agreed on together.

In its oldest use, ekklesia did not mean a building. It meant the assembly. The people. Strip it to the root and a church is exactly that: a group that associated in sacred purpose and agreed to govern itself.

That root quietly undoes the picture you started with. A church is not the structure. The building, the board, the letterhead are incidentals. The gathering in purpose is the thing.

---

## What the Word Means in Law

"Ecclesiastical," in law, describes what belongs to the church in its own internal governance, as against the civil, secular sphere. A boundary word. On one side, the church ordering its own affairs. On the other, the state doing what states do. The legal reference at Cornell puts it this way: ecclesiastical law "primarily addresses the internal governance of a church and the conduct of its clergy and members."

An ecclesiastical organization has an internal order it governs. And the state is disabled from entering that order. Not restricted. Not discouraged. Disabled. By the First Amendment.

The government cannot decide which religions are genuine. The moment it does, it is approving some and denying others, and that is precisely what the First Amendment forbids. It cannot settle your doctrine. It cannot install or remove your minister. It cannot overrule how you run your own body.

One honest distinction, so you are not tripped later. On the tax side, the state can decide whether a body meets the code's picture of a church. For exemption purposes. That is a different question from whether your faith is real. The state never gets to decide that. It only applies its own rougher test of whether, for tax purposes, a body looks like a church.

---

## The Power Does Not Sit Where the Title Sits

Go back to 1871. The Supreme Court, in *Watson v. Jones*, describing how a Presbyterian church held its property, drew a distinction that carries the whole argument. The trustees held the legal title to the church building and property. But the equitable power of management, the real control, rested with the Session. The governing body.

The Court's own words: the trustees were "without any functions in essence ecclesiastical." They held the paper. The Session ran the place, decided what the property was for, and directed it to that purpose.

Power does not sit where the title sits. It sits where the governance of purpose sits. The name on the deed is not the person running the church. The one who decides what the property is for — that is where authority lives. The title is form. The governance of purpose is substance.

A word on that old case, because people get nervous about 1871. *Watson v. Jones* is over a hundred and fifty years old, and it is still the law of the land. Not history. Not a curiosity. When the Court reached the same ground in *Kedroff* (1952) and *Serbian E. Orthodox Diocese* (1976), it kept returning to the line Watson drew. The old cases did not expire. They are how the thing is held together.

---

## What This Means if You Hold a Trust

A WingPoint trust is not a pile of paper. It is a structure you govern toward a purpose. And how the law reads that structure decides whether you are doing the clerical work, the incidental form, or the ecclesiastical work — the part the state is disabled from entering.

A trust has three seats. The one who gives. The one who holds. The one who benefits. The seats stay distinct on paper no matter who fills them. But the question that matters is not which seat you occupy. It is what capacity you act in when you occupy it.

A trustee who manages the property as a steward, toward the stated religious purpose, under defined terms, with records — that trustee is doing the governing work the First Amendment protects. A trustee who merely holds the title and lets the structure coast — that trustee is holding paper. And nothing else.

The power is in the governance of purpose. Correct execution. Real purpose. Real order. Faithful stewardship. Clear records. That is what makes the protection real.

---

## How the IRS Draws the Line

The IRS gets asked, all the time, what counts as a church. The answer has a surprise in it.

"Church" appears all through the tax code. It is never defined there. What grew up over a century of court decisions is not a definition but a list of attributes. Distinct legal existence. A recognized creed and form of worship. A definite and distinct church government. A formal code of doctrine and discipline. A distinct religious history. Members not tied to some other denomination. Ordained ministers. Established places of worship. Regular congregations. Regular religious services. Its own literature. That is the list the IRS works from.

And the surprise, straight from the IRS's own training material: most people read that list as a checklist. Like you need thirteen of fourteen to get in. You do not. The so-called fourteen points is not a test. There is no minimum number. No number at all. A fixed, numerical understanding of a church would favor the old, established institutions and work against the newer and smaller. And locking a counting-based box around "church" is the one thing the First Amendment will not let the state do.

But one attribute keeps pulling the weight. A congregation. The Tax Court, in *Church of Eternal Life and Liberty* (1986), set the working form of a church as "a coherent group of individuals and families that join together to accomplish the religious purposes of mutually held beliefs." Not brick. Not a letterhead. A coherent group that joins together. At minimum, to be a church, you assemble a body regularly in worship. The gathering is the irreducible part.

So the IRS's own working picture of a church is a group, not a building. The tax law and the doctrine are not conceptually opposed. Even Washington, when it has to say what a church is, ends up describing the gathered body. Not the name on a deed.

---

## The Protections Are Built Into the Law

Asked about protection that holds even when a church does wrong: the protections do not come from the church behaving well. They are structural, written into the code.

First protection. A valid church does not apply for its own exemption. No form, no fee, no signoff. 26 U.S.C. §508(c)(1)(a) excludes a church and its integrated auxiliaries from the general requirement that charitable organizations formally claim their exemption. The church's standing is not granted by the agency. It is asserted. Because Washington is disabled from giving an identity to an organization it had no hand in qualifying.

Second protection. A standard tax-exempt organization files Form 990 every year — the government's standing window into a body's money and its leaders. Churches do not file it. No year-over-year reporting for the IRS to read and quietly build a case. This is the same edge the documentary pointed at, and it cuts both ways: the thing that hides some church spending from view is the thing that keeps the IRS from flagging a church the way it flags ordinary charities.

Third protection, the one most worth understanding. The high bar. Under 26 U.S.C. §7611, the IRS cannot simply question a church. A senior Treasury official must form a reasonable belief, grounded in facts on the written record, that the organization may not be exempt or is running an unrelated taxable business. A hunch will not do. Then, notice describing the concern. To move from inquiry to examination, the IRS must allow fifteen days and offer the church a genuine conference to resolve it. The whole matter must finish within two years, or it drops. §7611 is the code telling the IRS, in effect: a church is not one you pick at random.

The gate is high not because the code is kind to churches, but because the First Amendment is written through it. So it does not switch off when the agency gets suspicious. It is highest exactly when abuse would make the IRS want to run straight at the door.

That is a hard truth, and it works to the benefit of the genuine. It never excuses the conduct the documentary properly exposes. The constitutional gate protects a real religious body that governs itself. It is not a license to abuse, and it does not excuse the wrong a costume is used for.

---

## Not a Loophole. A Boundary.

A fair question should surface now. Is this a way to avoid taxes? The answer carries more weight than the question sounds.

The protection does not come from clever wording. It comes from the First Amendment, and it belongs only to a body that genuinely operates as a religious organization. A shell with no documented purpose, no real governance, no actual religious function — that is paper. And paper with nothing behind it gets read right past.

A qualifying organization is exempt automatically. No application, no approval. If the IRS does inquire, a real church faces a high procedural bar.

Keep the substance real. Document it honestly. Govern it faithfully. Dress up an empty shell and you are on the wrong side of the line. That is the honest middle, and the durable one.

---

## What You Were Taught, and What Is True

The trained version goes like this. A church is a building and a denomination. It exists by permission of the state or the IRS. Your standing is whatever the government grants you. And if you never quite get permission, the feeling is you never quite exist. Legally. Really.

The working version looks different. A church is a gathered, self-governing body of believers — the assembly, not the edifice. "Ecclesiastical" is the church's own internal governance, the sphere the state cannot enter. Power is in the governance of purpose, not the title. Correct execution, real purpose, real order, faithful stewardship — that activates the protection. The First Amendment side cannot be revoked. The tax side turns on whether the body meets the code's picture, which is why keeping the substance real is not a suggestion.

See it that way and the question stops being how to get permission. It becomes how to govern what is already yours. Not a new trick. An old law, read accurately, and most people were never shown it.
