# Grantor Retained Annuity Trust (GRAT)

## What It Is

A Grantor Retained Annuity Trust, or GRAT, is an estate planning vehicle used to transfer appreciating assets to beneficiaries at a reduced gift tax cost while the grantor retains the right to receive a fixed annuity for a set term. Governed by IRC 2702, the GRAT allows the grantor to place assets into an irrevocable trust in exchange for the right to receive annual annuity payments of a fixed dollar amount for the trust term. At the end of the term, any remaining assets pass to the remainder beneficiaries (typically the grantor's children). The gift tax value of the transfer is calculated as the initial contribution minus the present value of the retained annuity, using an IRS hurdle rate (the Section 7520 rate). When the annuity is set so that the present value of the retained annuity equals the initial contribution, the gift is "zeroed out" — meaning no gift tax is owed and no lifetime exemption is consumed — yet any appreciation above the hurdle rate passes to the beneficiaries free of gift and estate tax. This makes the GRAT a powerful estate freeze technique, especially in low-interest-rate environments or with highly appreciating assets.

## Key Governance Requirements

- **Fixed annuity payment calculation and schedule.** The trust instrument must specify an annuity amount that is a fixed dollar figure (not a percentage), payable at least annually. The annuity is calculated based on the initial contribution, the Section 7520 rate at funding, and the trust term. The trustee must make each payment on schedule — missed or late payments can jeopardize the GRAT's qualification.

- **Minimum term and grantor survival requirement.** While there is no statutory minimum term, a GRAT must last at least two years in practice to be effective, and the grantor must survive the entire term for the remainder to pass to beneficiaries free of estate tax. If the grantor dies during the term, the trust's assets are included in the grantor's estate at their date-of-death value, largely defeating the estate freeze. Shorter terms reduce mortality risk but reduce the time for appreciation.

- **Grantor trust status for income tax.** A GRAT is structured as a grantor trust, meaning the grantor pays the income tax on the trust's earnings rather than the trust. This is a feature, not a defect — the grantor's payment of the trust's income tax is not an additional gift and effectively allows the trust assets to grow tax-free, enhancing the estate freeze benefit.

- **Zeroed-out GRAT structure.** To avoid gift tax and exemption consumption, the annuity is typically set so that the present value of the retained annuity equals the initial contribution, producing a zero (or near-zero) taxable gift. The trustee and advisor must verify this calculation at funding using the correct Section 7520 rate.

- **Annuity payment source and swap-out flexibility.** The trustee must pay the annuity in cash or, if the trust instrument permits, in kind by returning trust assets to the grantor at their current value. Some GRATs include a swap-out provision allowing the trustee to substitute equivalent-value assets, useful for retaining high-basis assets.

## Distribution Rules

- **Annuity payments to the grantor are mandatory and must be made on schedule** — at least annually, in the fixed amount specified in the trust instrument. The trustee cannot skip, delay, or vary the annuity amount.

- **At the end of the term, all remaining assets pass to the remainder beneficiaries** (or to a continuing trust for their benefit). No portion of the remainder is retained by the grantor.

- **If the grantor dies during the term, the trust assets are returned to the grantor's estate** at their date-of-death value, undoing the estate freeze for those assets.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Grantor dies during the term.** If the grantor does not survive the annuity term, the trust assets return to the grantor's estate and the estate freeze fails. Shorter terms reduce this risk but also reduce the time for appreciation to exceed the hurdle rate.

- **Annuity payments missed or miscalculated.** Each annuity payment must be made on time and in the correct fixed amount. Errors can jeopardize the GRAT's qualification and the zeroed-out gift tax treatment.

- **Using assets that do not appreciate above the hurdle rate.** A GRAT only transfers value if the assets appreciate more than the Section 7520 rate. Low-performing assets result in the annuity consuming the entire trust and no remainder passing to beneficiaries.

- **Failing to account for grantor trust income tax.** The grantor must pay the trust's income tax each year. If this is not planned for, the grantor's cash flow can be strained, and failing to pay can create complications for the trust's tax position.
