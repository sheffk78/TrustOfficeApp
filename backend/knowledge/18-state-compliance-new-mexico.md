# Trust Compliance: New Mexico

## State Income Tax on Trusts

New Mexico imposes a state income tax on trusts that have New Mexico nexus. Nonresident trusts with no New Mexico-source income generally have no New Mexico income tax filing obligation.

New Mexico's income tax rates for trusts are graduated, following the same rate schedule applied to individual taxpayers. The rates range from 1.7% for income not over $5,500 to 4.9% for income in excess of $16,000. These rates are relatively moderate compared to high-tax states like California. Fiduciaries should plan distributions carefully because New Mexico allows a deduction for income distributed to beneficiaries, which can shift the tax burden to beneficiaries who may be in lower brackets.

## Filing Requirements

New Mexico fiduciary income tax is filed using Form FID-1, the New Mexico Fiduciary Income Tax Return. The filing deadline is April 15 for calendar-year trusts, or the 15th day of the fourth month after the close of the fiscal year. A fiduciary must file Form FID-1 if the trust has New Mexico-source income or if the trust is a resident trust. An estate or trust that is exempt from federal income tax may still need to file Form FID-1 if it has New Mexico-source income. An extension can be filed, which provides additional time to file but not to pay.

## Beneficiary Notice Requirements

New Mexico does not have a statutory requirement for trustees to provide initial notice to beneficiaries upon accepting trusteeship or when a trust becomes irrevocable. The New Mexico Uniform Trust Code (NMSA Chapter 46A) requires trustees to keep qualified beneficiaries reasonably informed about the administration of the trust and the material facts necessary for them to protect their interests. Trustees must respond to beneficiary requests for information and provide reports. While there is no mandatory initial notice deadline, trustees should document all communications with beneficiaries to create a defensible record.

## Court Supervision

Court supervision of trusts in New Mexico is optional. A trust does not need to be registered with the court to be valid. Trustees may operate trusts in an unsupervised manner, which is the default for most revocable and irrevocable trusts. However, a trustee or beneficiary may petition the court for supervision if disputes arise or if judicial guidance is needed. New Mexico courts can also intervene when a trustee requests instructions or when a beneficiary seeks enforcement of their rights under the trust.

## Key State-Specific Rules

- **Uniform Trust Code:** New Mexico adopted the UTC as NMSA Chapter 46A, codifying the Uniform Trust Code provisions for trust creation, administration, and enforcement.
- **Spendthrift Protection:** New Mexico allows self-settled asset protection trusts, meaning a grantor can place assets in a trust for their own benefit and shield those assets from creditors, subject to certain limitations.
- **Perpetuity Period:** New Mexico has abolished the rule against perpetuities, allowing trusts to last indefinitely for multi-generational wealth planning.
- **Community Property:** New Mexico is a community property state, meaning trusts involving married couples must carefully track separate versus community property, as this affects taxation and creditor access.
- **No Inheritance Tax:** New Mexico does not impose an inheritance tax, simplifying the tax landscape for trust beneficiaries.
