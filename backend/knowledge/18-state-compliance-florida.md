# Trust Compliance: Florida

## State Income Tax on Trusts

Florida does not impose a state income tax on individuals or trusts. This is a major advantage for trusts domiciled or administered in Florida, as trust income — including investment income, capital gains, and distributions — is not subject to state income tax. Trustees of Florida trusts are not required to file a state fiduciary income tax return.

There is one important caveat: if a Florida trust has income sourced to another state (for example, rental property in a state that taxes nonresident income), the trust may have filing obligations in that other state. Trustees should evaluate whether any out-of-state income creates nexus elsewhere. Florida also has no estate tax or inheritance tax, meaning the state does not levy a tax on the transfer of assets at death beyond what the federal government requires.

The absence of state income tax makes Florida particularly attractive for irrevocable trusts designed to accumulate income, since the trust can grow without the drag of state taxation on undistributed income.

## Filing Requirements

Florida does not require a state fiduciary income tax return. Trustees file IRS Form 1041 for federal income tax purposes as usual. Florida does not have an estate tax return requirement at the state level, since Florida's estate tax was eliminated and is not currently tied to federal estate tax credits.

If the trust holds a Florida business entity, the trustee should confirm whether that entity has state-level obligations. Florida's corporate income tax applies to C corporations and certain other entities, but most pass-through entities and trusts themselves are not subject to it. Trustees should consult with a tax advisor to confirm whether any entity within the trust structure triggers a Florida filing.

## Beneficiary Notice Requirements

Florida has a specific statutory beneficiary notice requirement under Florida Statutes Section 736.0813. The trustee must provide qualified beneficiaries with a notice of the trust's existence, a copy of the trust instrument, and information about the trustee's identity and address. This notice must be given within 60 days after the trustee accepts the trusteeship, and for irrevocable trusts, the trustee must provide notice to qualified beneficiaries of the trust's existence, the identity of the settlor, and the right to request a copy of the trust instrument and an accounting.

The notice must also inform beneficiaries that the trust is irrevocable and that they have the right to receive an annual accounting. This is a mandatory requirement under the Florida Trust Code, and failure to provide notice can expose the trustee to liability and potential removal.

## Court Supervision

Florida trusts are generally administered without court supervision. The Florida Trust Code does not require trusts to be registered with the court. Trustees can operate trusts in an unsupervised manner, which is the standard practice. Court involvement is typically limited to disputes, requests for declaratory relief, or when a trustee or beneficiary petitions for court guidance on a specific matter.

Florida does have a voluntary court registration option for trusts, but this is rarely used and not required for validity or ongoing administration.

## Key State-Specific Rules

- **Asset Protection Trusts:** Florida enacted self-settled asset protection trust legislation, allowing grantors to create qualified trusts under Florida Statutes Chapter 736 that protect assets from creditors after a specified period, subject to conditions.
- **Florida Trust Code:** Florida adopted a comprehensive trust code based on the Uniform Trust Code, providing clear statutory guidance on trustee duties, beneficiary rights, and trust administration.
- **Homestead Protection:** Florida has strong homestead and creditor protection laws that interact with trust planning, particularly for primary residences.
- **No Estate Tax:** Florida does not impose a state estate or inheritance tax, simplifying post-death administration.
