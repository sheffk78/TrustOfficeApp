# Qualified Personal Residence Trust (QPRT)

## What It Is

A Qualified Personal Residence Trust, or QPRT, is an estate planning vehicle that allows a grantor to transfer a personal residence to beneficiaries at a reduced gift tax value while retaining the right to live in the home for a fixed term. Governed by IRC 2702, the QPRT is specifically limited to a "qualified residence" — defined as the grantor's principal residence or one other residence used by the grantor for personal purposes for at least 14 days per year. When the grantor funds the QPRT, the gift tax value of the transfer is the initial value of the residence minus the present value of the grantor's retained right to occupy it, calculated using the IRS Section 7520 rate and the chosen term. Because the retained interest's present value reduces the gift, the taxable gift is less than the full value of the home — this is the initial value freeze that makes the QPRT attractive. A typical term is around 10 years, though shorter or longer terms are possible. If the grantor survives the term, the residence passes to the beneficiaries (or to a continuing trust) free of estate tax, and all post-transfer appreciation is removed from the grantor's estate. If the grantor dies during the term, the residence is included in the grantor's estate at its date-of-death value, largely defeating the benefit.

## Key Governance Requirements

- **Qualified residence limitation.** The trust may hold only the grantor's principal residence or one other personal residence. If the trust holds a residence that does not meet the qualified residence definition, the QPRT fails and the retained interest is valued at zero for gift tax purposes, making the entire value of the home a taxable gift.

- **Retained right to occupy for a fixed term.** The trust instrument must give the grantor the unrestricted right to occupy the residence for the specified term. The grantor cannot be required to pay rent during the term. The term length is a strategic decision balancing mortality risk against gift tax savings — longer terms produce a larger retained interest value and a smaller taxable gift but increase the risk the grantor dies before the term ends.

- **Initial value freeze and gift tax implications at funding.** The taxable gift at funding is the residence's initial value minus the present value of the retained occupancy right. The grantor must file Form 709 to report the gift and use lifetime exemption or pay gift tax. Once the gift is made, any future appreciation passes to beneficiaries free of additional transfer tax, which is the core estate planning benefit.

- **Post-term occupancy requires fair market rent.** After the term ends, the residence belongs to the beneficiaries. If the grantor continues to live in the home, they must pay fair market rent to the beneficiaries (or to a trust for their benefit). Failing to pay rent can cause estate inclusion under IRC 2036. The rent arrangement must be documented and at arm's length.

- **Restrictions on sale and use during the term.** The QPRT can sell the residence during the term, but the proceeds must be used to purchase a replacement residence within a specific period or be distributed to the grantor. The trust may also hold a limited amount of cash for maintenance and improvements.

## Distribution Rules

- **During the term, the grantor has the exclusive right to occupy the residence** without paying rent. The trustee (often the grantor) manages the property, pays taxes and maintenance, and cannot lease or sell the home without following the trust's replacement-residence rules.

- **At the end of the term, the residence passes to the remainder beneficiaries** (or to a continuing trust for their benefit). The grantor has no further ownership interest unless they negotiate a post-term lease.

- **Post-term options include renting from the beneficiaries, moving out, or purchasing the home from the trust at fair market value.** Each option has different tax and practical consequences that should be planned before the term ends.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Grantor dies during the term.** If the grantor does not survive the term, the residence is included in the grantor's estate at its date-of-death value, undoing the estate planning benefit and wasting the gift tax paid at funding.

- **Failing to pay fair market rent after the term.** A grantor who stays in the home post-term without paying rent risks having the full value of the residence included in their estate under IRC 2036, entirely defeating the QPRT.

- **Holding a non-qualified residence.** If the property does not meet the qualified residence definition (principal residence or one other residence used at least 14 days per year), the QPRT fails and the retained interest is valued at zero, making the entire home value a taxable gift.

- **Choosing too long a term to maximize the gift tax savings.** A longer term reduces the taxable gift but increases mortality risk. A grantor who optimizes solely for tax savings may choose a term they do not survive, losing the benefit entirely.
