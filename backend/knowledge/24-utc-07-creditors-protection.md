# UTC Doctrine 07 — Creditors, Spendthrift Provisions, and Protecting the Trust

> ⚠️ **Scope note — creditor-protection rules are narrow, exception-laden, and jurisdiction-dependent.**
> Four things to keep in view:
> 1. The Code's rules are about whether a *beneficiary's* interest can be reached by *that beneficiary's* creditors. They are not a promise that trust assets are untouchable, and not a defense to a claim against the trustee personally.
> 2. The Code makes the spendthrift and creditor rules non-overridable by the trust document. A document cannot talk its way out of them.
> 3. Several sections turn on bracketed state choices and on federal law. A state statute or the tax code does the work there, not the Code.
> 4. "Spendthrift" is a term of art with a narrow definition. A trustee who says "my trust has a spendthrift clause" may not have one as the Code defines it.
> Read this section as reference-grade and jurisdiction-dependent, and keep a persistent note about state law and facts wherever it is used.

**UTC anchor:** art. 5 — ss. 501–507; art. 1 — ss. 103(16), 105(b)(5); art. 8 — s. 803; art. 10 — s. 1001

## Why this matters

Spendthrift clauses block a beneficiary from assigning or alienating a beneficial interest. What the Code adds is the part the marketing category leaves out: a spendthrift provision is valid only if it restrains both voluntary and involuntary transfer, and it is exception-laden. This section is reference-grade and jurisdiction-dependent — read it with the state-law note below.

## The vocabulary

Section 103(16) defines a **spendthrift provision** as a term of a trust which restrains both voluntary and involuntary transfer of a beneficiary's interest. Section 501 turns on the same phrase: a creditor may reach a beneficiary's interest "to the extent a beneficiary's interest is not subject to a spendthrift provision." The definition is not decorative. A clause that stops a beneficiary from selling their interest but not a court from garnishing it is not a spendthrift provision under the Code, and s. 501's route stays open.

Section 103(2) defines an **ascertainable standard** as one relating to an individual's health, education, support, or maintenance; 103(14) defines a **revocable** trust as one revocable by the settlor without the trustee's or an adverse interest holder's consent.

## Section 502: what a spendthrift provision does

**502(a):** a spendthrift provision is valid only if it restrains both voluntary and involuntary transfer of a beneficiary's interest.

**502(b):** a term providing that the interest is held subject to a "spendthrift trust," or words of similar import, is sufficient to restrain both. **502(c):** a beneficiary may not transfer an interest in violation of a valid spendthrift provision, and except as provided in Article 5, a creditor may not reach the interest or a trustee distribution before receipt.

That last clause carries the most weight. The protection is strongest *before* the money reaches the beneficiary. Once distributed, the beneficiary holds ordinary property and the protection does not travel with the cash. That is the honest answer to the question beneficiaries actually ask: "does a spendthrift clause protect me once I've been paid?"

## Section 503: the exceptions

**503(a):** "child" includes any person for whom a child-support order or judgment has been entered. **503(b):** even if the trust contains a spendthrift provision, a beneficiary's child, spouse, or former spouse with a support judgment, or a judgment creditor who provided services protecting the interest, may obtain a court order attaching present or future distributions for the beneficiary's benefit.

**503(c):** a spendthrift provision is unenforceable against a claim of this state or the United States to the extent a statute provides.

So there are three ways a spendthrift provision can be overcome: a support or maintenance judgment for the beneficiary's child, spouse, or former spouse; a judgment creditor who provided services protecting the interest; and state or federal law that overrides the clause. The third is why this section cannot be self-contained — federal tax liens, ERISA, and bankruptcy law all reach into trust administration.

## Section 504: discretionary trusts and the abuse-of-discretion carve-out

**504(b):** except as provided in (c), whether or not a trust contains a spendthrift provision, a creditor may not compel a distribution subject to the trustee's discretion, even if the discretion is expressed as a standard or abused.

**504(c):** to the extent a trustee has not complied with a distribution standard or abused a discretion, (1) a distribution may be ordered to satisfy a support judgment against the beneficiary, and (2) the court shall direct the trustee to pay an equitable amount, capped at what the trustee should have distributed absent the failure or abuse.

**504(d):** this does not limit a beneficiary's right to sue a trustee for abuse of discretion or failure to comply with a distribution standard. **504(e):** where a trustee's discretion for their own benefit is limited by an ascertainable standard, a creditor may not reach the beneficial interest except to the extent it would be subject to the claim were the beneficiary not acting as trustee.

The (c) carve-out is the one to flag. A creditor cannot generally reach a discretionary distribution, but if the trustee failed to follow the standard or abused the discretion, the court can order a distribution to satisfy a support judgment, capped at what the trustee should have distributed. A trustee who never distributed is in a weak position — the "required" amount is not zero if the standard would have compelled something.

## Section 505: the settlor's creditors

**505(a)(1):** during the settlor's lifetime, the property of a revocable trust is subject to the settlor's creditors' claims.

**505(a)(2):** with respect to an irrevocable trust, a creditor of the settlor may reach the maximum amount that can be distributed to or for the settlor's benefit. With more than one settlor, a particular settlor's creditor may not exceed that settlor's interest in the portion attributable to their contribution.

**505(a)(3):** after the settlor's death, and subject to the settlor's right to direct the source from which liabilities are paid, the property of a trust revocable at death is subject to the settlor's creditors' claims, estate costs, funeral expenses, and bracketed statutory allowances to a surviving spouse and children, to the extent probate is inadequate.

**505(b)(2):** upon the lapse, release, or waiver of the power, the holder is treated as the settlor only to the extent the value of the property affected exceeds a bracketed threshold drawn from Internal Revenue Code §§ 2041(b)(2), 2514(e), and 2503(b). **Confirm current figures with your CPA and attorney.**

The three-part structure of 505(a) in plain English: revocable and living — the settlor's creditors get in. Irrevocable — they reach only what the settlor could have received. Revocable at death — they, plus estate costs and spousal or children's allowances, get in to the extent probate is inadequate.

## Section 506: the overdue-distribution trap

**506(a):** a "mandatory distribution" is a distribution of income or principal the trustee is required to make under the trust's terms, including on termination. It does not include a distribution subject to the trustee's discretion, even if expressed as a standard or coupled with direction language.

**506(b):** whether or not a trust contains a spendthrift provision, a creditor may reach a mandatory distribution, including one on termination, if the trustee has not made it within a reasonable time after the designated date.

The reasoning is explicit: a court will order a distribution the terms require, and a creditor can capture what the trustee sat on. The fiduciary risk is double — the beneficiary gets what they were owed, and the creditor reaches it.

## Section 507: the trustee's personal obligations

**507:** trust property is not subject to the trustee's personal obligations, even if the trustee becomes insolvent or bankrupt. This is genuinely protective, and it is the precise boundary. It protects trust property from the trustee's personal creditors. It does not protect the trust from a claim that the trustee personally breached a duty — ss. 1001 and 1002 territory, answered with personal assets.

## The line the Code draws in s. 105

Section 105(b)(5) makes the effect of a spendthrift provision and the rights of certain creditors and assignees non-overridable by the trust's terms. Article 5 is a floor the document cannot undercut, and the Code's defaults in 105(a) mean silence creates no extra protection. A document silent on creditors gets the Code's defaults, not an absence of rules.

### What this means for a trustee

- Read your trust's spendthrift clause against s. 103(16): does it restrain both voluntary and involuntary transfer? If it stops only voluntary transfers, it is not a spendthrift provision under the Code, and s. 501's creditor route stays open.
- If you are advising on an asset-protection trust, involve a trust attorney in the governing state before drafting. The Code supplies the floor; the state statute, the tax rules, and the document supply everything else.
- Recognize that protection is strongest before funds reach the beneficiary. Once distributed, ordinary creditor rules apply.
- Never treat the trust as a place to put money a beneficiary's own creditor should not reach, without checking ss. 503(b) and 503(c). Support judgments, service-provider judgment creditors, and state or federal law all get past the clause.
- If the trust is revocable and the settlor is living, understand s. 505(a)(1). If it was revocable at death, s. 505(a)(3) applies to estate claims.
- Check every mandatory distribution against s. 506. s. 507 shields trust property from your creditors, not you from a breach-of-trust claim.

### Common question we get

**"My trust has a spendthrift clause. Is my family's money protected from creditors?"**
Partly, and "partly" is doing real work. Section 502(a) tells you whether your clause even qualifies: it must restrain both voluntary and involuntary transfer, and if it only stops your daughter from selling her interest, a court can still reach it under s. 501. Even a qualifying provision has exceptions — s. 503(b) lets a support-judgment creditor attach present or future distributions, and s. 503(c) makes the clause unenforceable against any claim a statute overrides. On top of that, s. 505 exposes a revocable trust to the settlor's creditors during life, and s. 506 lets a creditor reach an overdue mandatory distribution. Whether your family's specific creditor has a path in is a fact question for a trust attorney in your state.

**"Can a beneficiary give their interest back to the trust?"**
It depends on the document, and the Code tells you why the question is harder than it sounds. If the trust contains a valid spendthrift provision, s. 502(c) says a beneficiary may not transfer an interest in violation of it. That is why a beneficiary-to-trustee transition usually needs an amendment creating a transition provision, with a spendthrift exception for voluntary relinquishment. A trust with no spendthrift provision is different — the Code does not bar the transfer, and the trustee's duties to the remaining beneficiaries, including impartiality under s. 803, still apply.

**"Does protecting the trust from creditors mean the creditors can't touch me either?"**
No, and this is the distinction to hold onto. Section 507 says trust property is not subject to the trustee's personal obligations, even if the trustee becomes insolvent or bankrupt. That protects the *trust* from the *trustee's* creditors. It does not protect the trustee from a beneficiary's claim that the trustee breached a duty — ss. 1001 and 1002: a court can order the trustee to pay money or restore property, and the trustee answers with personal assets.
