# UTC Doctrine 05 — Amending, Revoking, and Modifying a Trust

> ⚠️ **Scope note — "irrevocable" is not the same as "unchangeable."**
> A trust can be modified by court order, reformed, and modified for tax objectives, and the settlor's own power to amend and revoke turns on the trust's terms. Whether a particular change can be made to a particular trust is document- and court-specific. Start with the amendment provision in your document, and treat whether something can be changed as an attorney question.

**UTC anchor:** art. 6 — ss. 601, 602, 603, 604; art. 4 — ss. 410–417

## Why this matters

"Irrevocable" is the most misread word in the trust world. A trust can be modified by court order, reformed, and modified for tax objectives, and the settlor's own power to amend and revoke turns on the trust's terms. This section lays out both sides of that picture.

## The revocable trust: settlor's power

Section 601 sets the capacity standard: the capacity required to create, amend, revoke, or add property to a revocable trust, or to direct its trustee, is the same as required to make a will — applied under the governing jurisdiction's law, not uniformly across states.

**602(a):** unless the trust's terms expressly provide that it is irrevocable, the settlor may revoke or amend it. A trust created under an instrument executed before the Code's effective date is outside this subsection. **602(b)** covers multiple settlors and community property: for community property, either spouse acting alone may revoke, but amendment requires joint action of both spouses; for other property, each settlor may revoke or amend as to the portion attributable to that settlor's contribution; and if fewer than all settlors act, the trustee shall notify the others.

**602(c) — method.** The settlor may revoke or amend (1) by substantial compliance with a method provided in the trust's terms, or (2) if the terms provide no method, or it is not expressly made exclusive, (A) by a later will or codicil referring to the trust, or (B) by any method showing clear and convincing evidence of intent.

**602(d):** on revocation, the trustee delivers the trust property as the settlor directs. **602(e) — agent authority:** a settlor's powers to revoke, amend, or distribute may be exercised by an agent under a power of attorney only as expressly authorized by the trust's terms or the power. **602(f) — conservator or guardian:** a conservator, or a guardian if none is appointed, may exercise those powers only with the approving court's approval.

**602(g):** a trustee who does not know a trust has been revoked or amended is not liable to the settlor or the settlor's successors for distributions made and actions taken on the assumption that it had not been.

Section 603 handles the settlor's powers over a revocable trust. A trustee may follow a direction contrary to the trust's terms to the extent the trust is revocable by the settlor — by the settlor alone, or by the settlor with another person. To the extent the settlor has capacity to revoke, the beneficiaries' rights are subject to the settlor's control and the trustee's duties are owed exclusively to the settlor. While a power of withdrawal may be exercised, the holder has a settlor's rights as to the property subject to it.

## Contesting validity: a deadline that runs against the trustee

**604(a):** a person may contest the validity of a trust that was revocable at the settlor's death within the earlier of (1) a bracketed period after the settlor's death, or (2) a bracketed period after the trustee sent the person a copy of the trust instrument and a notice stating the trust's existence, the trustee's name and address, and the time allowed for commencing a proceeding. The model text's bracketed values are placeholders, not law.

**604(b):** upon the settlor's death, the trustee may distribute the trust property in accordance with the trust's terms, and is not liable unless (1) the trustee knows of a pending proceeding contesting the trust's validity, or (2) a potential contestant notified the trustee of a possible proceeding and a proceeding is commenced within 60 days after the notification.

The bracketed periods are state choices, and the model text is not law in any state until enacted with those numbers resolved. **Confirm the applicable periods in your governing state with an attorney.** The jurisdiction-independent point is the shape of the safety valve: a trustee who sends the instrument plus an existence notice with the time allowed has a defined runway, and a beneficiary who does not object inside it loses the right to contest.

## The irrevocable trust: what the Code itself provides

**Section 410** is the frame. A trust terminates to the extent it is revoked or expires per its terms, no purpose of the trust remains to be achieved, or the purposes have become unlawful, contrary to public policy, or impossible to achieve. A proceeding under ss. 411–416, or a combination under s. 417, may be commenced by a trustee or a beneficiary.

**Section 411 — by consent.** On petition, if the court finds the settlor and all beneficiaries consent, the court shall approve the modification or termination even if inconsistent with a material purpose. A settlor's consent power may be exercised by an agent only as expressly authorized, or by a conservator or guardian with court approval. Without full consent, the court may approve if the trust could have been modified had all consented and the non-consenting beneficiary's interests would be adequately protected. The model text carries a bracketed subsection stating that a spendthrift provision is not presumed a material purpose — a state choice.

**Section 412 — unanticipated circumstances.** The court may modify a trust's administrative or dispositive terms, or terminate it, if because of circumstances not anticipated by the settlor, modification or termination will further the trust's purposes, and so far as practicable the modification must follow the settlor's probable intention.

**Section 413 — cy pres** (charitable trusts only): where a charitable purpose becomes unlawful, impracticable, impossible, or wasteful, the trust does not fail, the property does not revert to the settlor, and the court may direct the property to be applied consistent with the settlor's charitable purposes.

**Section 414 — uneconomic trust.** After notice to the qualified beneficiaries, a trustee may terminate a trust whose property is insufficient to justify the cost of administration — the model text carries a bracketed dollar threshold, and **that figure varies by state, so confirm it with an attorney.** The court may also modify or terminate such a trust, or remove the trustee, with no threshold.

**Section 415 — reformation to correct mistakes.** The court may reform a trust's terms, even if unambiguous, to conform them to the settlor's intention, on clear and convincing evidence of the intention and of a mistake of fact or law. This rescues a trust with a typo in a distribution clause — and it is also what a beneficiary's lawyer will cite in a dispute.

**Section 416 — settlor's tax objectives.** The court may modify a trust's terms in a manner not contrary to the settlor's probable intention to achieve the settlor's tax objectives, and may make the modification retroactive. A CPA-and-attorney conversation, not a trustee decision.

**Section 417 — combination and division.** After notice to the qualified beneficiaries, a trustee may combine two or more trusts into one, or divide one into several, if the result does not impair any beneficiary's rights or adversely affect the trust's purposes. This is one of the few Article 4 changes a trustee can make without a court.

## What the amendment guidance already gets right

The core principle is stated this way: "Irrevocable does not mean unchangeable. Most irrevocable trusts include mechanisms for modification. The trust instrument itself is the primary source of authority for what can be changed and how." Its Section 38 pattern — unanimous written trustee decision recorded in the minutes, no alteration of the trust's fundamental character, no deprivation of a vested beneficial interest without that beneficiary's written consent — is a document-level mechanism, not the UTC's consent procedure, which runs through a court under s. 411. Both are real; they are different routes. A trustee asking "can I amend this?" needs to hear, in order: what the document authorizes, what it requires procedurally, and — if the answer touches a beneficiary's rights or a material purpose — that this is court business. Its two scenarios map cleanly: adding a non-lineal beneficiary is the s. 411 track with the court as the gate, and a beneficiary becoming trustee hits a spendthrift clause, so a simple "I give it back" is blocked.

## What this means for a trustee

- Identify your trust's type in writing: revocable or irrevocable, and whether the document says so expressly.
- If the settlor of a revocable trust is alive and directing a change, s. 603(a) lets you follow a direction contrary to the document's terms. Document it in the minutes before you act.
- For a revocable trust, check the document's own amendment method first. Section 602(c)(1) makes substantial compliance with it the primary route; the will-and-codicil and clear-and-convincing-evidence routes in (c)(2) are fallbacks.
- Notice the s. 602(b)(3) duty: if fewer than all settlors revoke or amend, notify the others promptly.
- Do not distribute on a settlor's death until you understand s. 604(b)'s protection and its two exceptions — a known pending proceeding, or a notified contestant followed by a proceeding commenced within 60 days.
- For an irrevocable trust, read s. 411 and s. 415 before telling anyone the trust "can't be changed." Reformation, the consent route, and court-ordered modification all exist.
- For a charitable trust, s. 413's cy pres mechanism means the trust does not fail when its purpose becomes impracticable.

### Common question we get

**"My trust is irrevocable. So I can't change it at all?"**
No, and this is the most important correction to make. Section 411 lets the court approve a modification or termination on the settlor's and all beneficiaries' consent, and permits approval without full consent where the non-consenting beneficiary's interests would be adequately protected. Section 412 allows modification or termination for unanticipated circumstances. Section 415 allows reformation to correct a mistake, even of unambiguous terms, on clear and convincing evidence. Section 416 allows modification for tax objectives. None are self-executing, and none are a trustee's unilateral call — which is why the honest answer is "possibly, through a court," not "no."

**"The settlor wants to change the distribution standard. Can I just do it?"**
If the trust is revocable and the settlor is directing the change, s. 603(a) permits you to follow a direction contrary to the trust's terms, and 602(c) sets the method — the document's method if one is provided, otherwise a later will or codicil referring to the trust, or another method showing clear and convincing evidence of intent. If the trust is irrevocable, you cannot do it yourself; you can petition under s. 411 with the settlor's and all beneficiaries' consent, or under s. 412 for unanticipated circumstances. Either way, the amendment belongs in the minutes, with the reason and the authority.

**"The grantor died. The beneficiary says the trust is invalid. Can I just distribute and deal with it later?"**
Not without understanding s. 604. The trustee may distribute in accordance with the trust's terms, and is protected unless the trustee knows of a pending proceeding, or a potential contestant notified the trustee and a proceeding was commenced within 60 days after that notice. Sending the instrument with an existence notice stating the time allowed starts the second bracket in 604(a). Those bracket values are state choices, so the actual periods come from an attorney. And 604(c) means a beneficiary of a trust later found invalid must return what they received.
