# Lesson 5: Correcting the Record — W-2s, 1099s, K-1s, and Your Return

**Course:** WP-TAX-101 — The Tax Question  
**Lesson:** 5 of 7  

---

You've spent four lessons learning that the system doesn't work the way you thought. The definitions are narrower. The tax is an excise. The consent system pulled you in. The constitutional framework draws lines the government can't cross.

Maybe you're feeling anger — "Why didn't anyone tell me this?" Maybe relief — "It's not just me." Maybe fear — "What happens if I act on this?" Maybe all three at once. That's normal. You just learned that what you assumed about taxes may not be what the law actually says. That realization carries weight. Don't rush past it. The practical steps in this lesson will still be here after you've sat with the understanding.

This lesson gives you the practical steps. But the understanding from Lessons 1–4 gives you the framework. Without it, the practical steps are just procedures — mechanical actions without comprehension. With it, you understand *why* each step exists and *what* it means.

What you do with this information is your decision. WingPoint provides education, not specific filing advice.

One thing you need to understand up front: **your CPA won't be able to help you with this.** Most CPAs are trained in the common understanding of the tax code — the one where "wages" means all compensation, "employee" means anyone who works, and "everyone pays income tax." They're not trained in the statutory definitions you've been reading. They'll tell you to file as usual, report everything as wages, and pay what the forms say you owe. That's not because they're bad people. It's because the common understanding is what they were taught, and most of them have never read §3401(c) or §7701(a)(26) with the care you now have.

**What your CPA will cite, and why it doesn't apply the way they think:**

**§61 — "Gross income means all income from whatever source derived."** Your CPA will point to this and say it means everything you earn is taxable. But the phrase "from whatever source derived" modifies "income" — and "income" in the code isn't self-defining. It's limited by the definitions of "wages" (§3401(a)), "employee" (§3401(c)), and "trade or business" (§7701(a)(26)). If what you received doesn't fall within those definitions, it doesn't qualify as "income" under §61 in the first place. "All income from whatever source" means all income that qualifies as income — not all money that changes hands.

**§61(a) — the 15 enumerated items.** Your CPA may say, "Look, §61(a) specifically lists 'compensation for services' as gross income." It does. But "compensation for services" in the context of the code means compensation for services rendered *in a trade or business* — which, as you've learned, means the performance of the functions of a public office (§7701(a)(26)). Private-sector compensation for private labor isn't "compensation for services" as the code uses that phrase. The list in §61(a) doesn't override the definitions — it relies on them.

**The 16th Amendment.** Your CPA will say, "The 16th Amendment gives Congress the power to tax income." It does. But the Supreme Court ruled in Brushaber (1916) and Stanton v. Baltic Mining (1916) that the 16th Amendment did not create a new taxing power. The income tax was already an excise — a tax on certain activities. The 16th Amendment removed the requirement to apportion it among the states, but it didn't change what the tax applies to. As Eisner v. Macomber (1920) confirmed, "income" is not synonymous with "every receipt of money."

**Cheek v. United States (1991).** Your CPA may cite Cheek for the principle that you can't just ignore the tax code. They're right — and that's exactly why you're reading the code, not ignoring it. Cheek held that a good-faith belief that you're not required to file (even if wrong) negates willfulness. Filing a corrected return that cites the code's own definitions isn't ignoring the law — it's engaging with it directly.

**"Everyone pays income tax."** Your CPA may say this as if it's self-evident. But the code doesn't say "everyone." The code defines who the tax applies to through its terms — and those terms are narrow. The common understanding says "everyone." The code says otherwise. Read the definitions. That's what this course has been about.

The bottom line: your CPA is working from the common understanding. You're working from the code's own definitions. Those two things conflict — and the code is the law. You don't need to argue with your CPA. You need to understand the law well enough to file accurately on your own.

You'll need to do this yourself. Read the forms carefully. Understand what each line means. Zero out what doesn't fit the definitions. Document your position. The companion materials in this course give you the structure — but the work is yours. Nobody's going to hand you a pre-filled 4852 that perfectly fits your situation. You need to understand it well enough to file it yourself.

One more structural point before we get practical. §61 defines gross income as "all income from whatever source derived." The phrase "from whatever source derived" sounds like it covers everything — but it modifies "income," and "income" itself has limits. You can't have "income from whatever source derived" if what you received doesn't qualify as "income" under the code's definitions in the first place. If the underlying receipts aren't "wages" (because you're not a statutory "employee") and aren't income from a "trade or business" (because you're not performing the functions of a public office), then they may not constitute "gross income" as the code defines it. The filing requirements follow the same lines as the tax — they apply to those engaged in the taxable activity, not to everyone by default.

## Your Starting Point — The IRS Transcript

Before you can correct anything, you need to see what the IRS has on file. Your **IRS transcript** shows every information return filed about you — every W-2, every 1099, every K-1. This is your starting point.

**How to get your transcript:**

The easiest way is to set up an **ID.me account** linked to your IRS online account. Go to [irs.gov/individuals/get-transcript](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/get-transcript) and follow the ID.me verification process. Once you're in, look for the **Wage and Income Transcript** — this is the one that shows every information return filed under your SSN. The IRS offers four different transcript types for each year; you want the Wage and Income version specifically, because that's where the W-2s and 1099s live.

You can typically access the last 3–4 years of transcripts online. If you need to go back further, use **Form 4506-T** to request older transcripts by mail. Allow 5–10 calendar days for mail requests. You can also call the automated transcript service at 800-908-9946.

**What to look for:** Compare every entry on your Wage and Income transcript against what you know about your actual work situation. Every entry that characterizes your private compensation as federal "wages" or income from a "trade or business" is a record that may not accurately reflect your status.

## Form 4852 — Correcting a W-2

When an employer issues a W-2 that characterizes your compensation as "wages" under §3401, and you've determined that your compensation doesn't fall within the statutory definition (because you're not a statutory "employee" engaged in a "trade or business"), you can file a **Form 4852** — a Substitute for Form W-2.

Here's how it works in plain language: your employer's W-2 reports your compensation as "wages" with some dollar amount. You take that same W-2, file a 4852 in its place, and report the corrected amount as **zero** in the "wages" box — because your compensation doesn't meet the statutory definition of "wages" under §3401(a). You're not hiding income. You're reclassifying it. The money you received exists. The question is whether it fits the code's definition of "wages" — and if you're not a statutory "employee" engaged in a "trade or business," it doesn't.

The IRS describes Form 4852 as being for situations where "an employer or payer has issued an incorrect Form W-2." This is an official IRS form. It exists in the system for this exact purpose.

On the 4852, include a clear explanation of why you're reporting zero. Here's an example of the statement you can write in the explanation box:

> "Compensation reported on the original W-2 does not constitute 'wages' as defined in 26 USC §3401(a), as the recipient is not an 'employee' as defined in 26 USC §3401(c), and is not engaged in 'trade or business' as defined in 26 USC §7701(a)(26). Therefore, the correct amount of wages subject to federal income tax withholding is zero."

You're stating, under penalty of perjury, that the corrected information is accurate to the best of your knowledge. The IRS receives a document that contradicts the W-2 your employer filed. This creates a discrepancy the IRS must process.

**What happens when you file a Form 4852:**
- Some people receive letters asking for clarification; some don't hear back
- The IRS processes the discrepancy through existing administrative procedures
- Consistency matters more than speed — the same position on the same basis, year after year

## Correcting 1099s — Zeroing Out False Characterizations

1099 forms work the same way as W-2s — they create records that may not accurately reflect your status. The correction process is straightforward: you zero out the incorrectly characterized income and document why.

**For 1099s:** Edit the 1099 — either by crossing out the incorrectly reported amount and writing zero in its place (using white out or a PDF editor for digital copies), or by including a corrected form that reports zero for that income category. Then attach a signed jurat statement explaining why.

A **jurat statement** — a written declaration made under oath before a notary public — accompanies your filing. Your sworn statement:

> *"I declare under penalty of perjury that the following is true and correct: The income reported on Form 1099-[type] issued by [payer name], EIN [number], in the amount of $[amount], was received as compensation for private labor and does not constitute income from a 'trade or business' as defined in 26 USC §7701(a)(26), nor 'wages' as defined in 26 USC §3401(a), as I am not an 'employee' as defined in 26 USC §3401(c) nor engaged in the performance of the functions of a public office."*

You can list multiple 1099s in a single jurat — just identify each form by type, payer, EIN, and amount, then state why the characterization doesn't match the statutory definitions.

The goal: **correct the record down to what actually applies under the code's own definitions.** If the income doesn't fit the statutory definitions, the corrected return shows zero for that category.

## Filing — 1040 or 1040-X

You'll be filing either a **1040** (if you're filing for the current year for the first time) or a **1040-X** (to amend a previously filed return). The IRS typically accepts amendments for the last three years. People who have followed the corrected filing process documented at [losthorizons.com](https://losthorizons.com) have received complete refunds — including FICA — for those years as checks from the IRS.

The process is the same whether you're filing fresh or amending: include your 4852s (for W-2 corrections), your zeroed-out 1099s with jurat statements, and your corrected return showing the accurate characterization. Attach your explanation statement to the return.

## The W-4 — When You're Forced to Sign

As you saw in Lesson 3, the W-4 is described in the regulations as a "voluntary withholding agreement." The code provides for exemption from withholding under §3402(n) — if the employee had no income tax liability last year and expects none this year. The W-4 has an exemption line.

But what happens when an employer requires you to fill out a W-4 as a condition of employment? You can't start work without signing it. "Voluntary" and "required as a condition of employment" are in tension.

**"Under duress" notation:** Some researchers, including Champion, recommend adding the notation "signed under duress" or "VOLUNTARY WITHHOLDING AGREEMENT — per 26 CFR §31.3402(p)-1" to the W-4 form. The purpose isn't to refuse to sign — it's to preserve your position that the form was signed under economic compulsion, not genuine voluntary consent.

The legal theory: if you sign a form that the regulation itself calls a "voluntary withholding agreement," and you sign it only because you'll lose your job if you don't, then the consent was obtained under duress. Noting this on the form preserves your record that the consent wasn't truly voluntary.

The W-4 Decision Guide (companion material) walks through each line of the form and what the code says about it.

## The Corrected Filing Process — The Sequence

1. **Get your IRS transcript** — Set up ID.me, pull your Wage and Income Transcript, identify every information return filed about you
2. **Determine whether your compensation falls within the statutory definitions** — Based on the code, not on what your employer or HR department assumes
3. **Document your position** — Keep records of your analysis, the code sections you relied on, and your reasoning
4. **File corrected forms** — 4852 for W-2s (reporting zero for the "wages" box), zeroed-out 1099s with jurat statements, reflecting the accurate characterization
5. **File your 1040 or 1040-X** — Include all corrected forms and jurat statements with your return
6. **Be consistent** — The same position on the same basis, year after year

Consistency matters more than speed. Documentation matters more than boldness. The IRS has processes for handling discrepancies. Patience and precision serve you better than urgency and emotion.

## How the IRS Actually Responds — What to Expect

This is the part most courses skip:

**The most common response is silence.** The IRS processes millions of returns. A corrected return or Form 4852 goes into the system. Sometimes you hear nothing back. Sometimes you receive a letter asking for clarification. Sometimes the IRS proposes adjustments that you can respond to.

People expect either immediate acceptance or an army at the door. The reality is bureaucratic and slow. Your corrected return is one line in a very large system.

**What the IRS typically sends:**
- **CP notices** — Computer-generated letters proposing adjustments. Form letters, not personal audits. Generated by algorithms matching your return against what your employer reported. You have the right to respond, and most responses are handled by mail.
- **Information requests** — Letters asking for documentation or clarification of your position. Typically straightforward — the IRS wants to understand what you're claiming and why.
- **Proposed deficiencies** — Notices saying the IRS believes you owe more than you reported. You have appeal rights, and most of these get resolved through correspondence, not court.

The IRS has never sent someone to prison for filing a corrected return that cited the code accurately. The prosecutions you've heard about — Hendrickson's contempt charge, others — were for defying court orders, not for being wrong about the law. The law is what it is. Filing a corrected return that accurately reflects the code's own definitions is not a crime.

**That said, the IRS may push back.** You may receive a letter saying your filing is "frivolous." They may threaten penalties. They may use language designed to make you feel like you've done something wrong. Hundreds of people have filed corrected returns using the process described in this course, and they've received different responses — some got complete refunds with no questions asked, some got letters asking for clarification, some got pushback. What's important is that you're using your understanding to file accurately and correct your record. It's your record. The IRS has a process for handling discrepancies, and you have the right to respond.

Fear tactics work when you don't know what to expect. Now you know: CP notices, information requests, and proposed deficiencies are normal administrative responses. They're not evidence that you've done something wrong. They're evidence that the system is processing your filing. Respond to each one in writing, cite the code sections, and maintain your position consistently.

Understanding this reduces the fear — because fear thrives on the unknown. When you know what the notices look like, what they mean, and how to respond, the process becomes manageable instead of terrifying.

## The Revocation of Election

If you previously elected to be treated as a taxpayer — by filing returns, by filling out W-4s claiming a certain status — you may have the option to revoke that election.

The revocation of election is a legal process by which someone who previously consented to being treated as a taxpayer formally withdraws that consent. It's available to anyone who previously made an election they didn't fully understand — not exclusive to any one organization.

### What You Send

The revocation package typically includes:

1. **A letter of revocation** — A written statement, signed under penalty of perjury, declaring that you are revoking any and all elections previously made to be treated as a "taxpayer" under subtitle A of the Internal Revenue Code. This letter should cite the specific code sections that define the elections you're revoking (§3401(c) "employee" status, §3402(p) voluntary withholding agreement, etc.) and state clearly that you no longer consent to being classified under those definitions.

2. **A copy of your W-2(s)** — To show what was incorrectly reported and what you're correcting.

3. **Your corrected return (1040 or 1040-X)** — Showing zero in the categories where the statutory definitions don't apply, with your 4852(s) and jurat statement(s) attached.

4. **Any supporting documentation** — Copies of the code sections you're relying on, a brief explanation of your position, and any correspondence from the IRS related to your filing.

### What It Says

The revocation letter should include:
- Your full legal name and Social Security Number
- A clear statement that you are revoking all prior elections to be treated as a "taxpayer," "employee," or person engaged in a "trade or business" under the Internal Revenue Code
- Citations to the specific statutes that define those terms (§3401(a), §3401(c), §3401(d), §7701(a)(14), §7701(a)(26))
- A statement that you are filing corrected returns (4852s, zeroed 1099s) reflecting your accurate status
- Your signature, dated, under penalty of perjury

The Freedom Law Group Revocation of Election White Paper (linked in the Evidence section) provides detailed templates and language for the revocation letter.

### Where You Send It

Mail your revocation package to the IRS service center that processes returns for your state. The address depends on where you live — check IRS.gov or the instructions on Form 1040-X for the current mailing address. Send it certified mail with return receipt requested so you have proof of delivery.

### What Happens After You Send It

The IRS processes the revocation and updates your filing status. Documented outcomes show that a significant majority of people who complete the process are classified by the IRS as non-filers — meaning the IRS acknowledges they are not required to file. This can take several months. You may receive correspondence from the IRS acknowledging receipt, asking for clarification, or proposing adjustments. Respond to each one in writing, maintain your position consistently, and keep copies of everything.

Revocation connects directly to the correction process in this lesson. If you're correcting your information returns and adjusting your filing position, revocation of prior elections may be part of that sequence. It's a step you take after understanding the law (Lessons 1–4), not before.

## Transitioning Your Filing Position — Not Overnight

1. **Understand the definitions first** — Lessons 1 through 4 gave you this
2. **Document your position** — Code sections, court cases, reasoning
3. **Start with one correction** — Don't change everything at once
4. **Be consistent** — The same position, the same basis, year after year
5. **Give the system time to process** — Corrections take time. Don't panic if you don't get an immediate response.

The difference between correcting a return and filing a new one is significant. A corrected return adjusts what was previously filed. A new return starts fresh. The process you choose depends on where you are and what you've already filed.
