# Marital Deduction Trust

## What It Is

A marital deduction trust is a trust designed to hold assets passing to a surviving spouse in a way that qualifies for the unlimited marital deduction under federal estate tax law. The unlimited marital deduction allows an estate to transfer an unlimited amount of assets to a qualifying spouse free of federal estate tax, deferring the tax until the surviving spouse's death. The trust is typically funded at the first spouse's death and must meet specific IRS requirements to qualify. Proper use can significantly reduce or eliminate estate tax at the first death, but failure to meet the requirements can trigger a large, unexpected tax bill.

## Key Governance Requirements

- **Unlimited marital deduction qualification.** The trust must be structured so that the transferred assets qualify for the unlimited marital deduction under IRC Section 2056. This generally requires that the surviving spouse be the sole beneficiary during their lifetime and have qualifying income and principal rights.
- **Qualifying spouse.** The surviving spouse must be a U.S. citizen. If the spouse is not a citizen, the marital deduction is not available unless the assets pass through a Qualified Domestic Trust (QDOT), which adds further requirements.
- **Form 706 filing.** The estate must file IRS Form 706 (United States Estate Tax Return) to claim the marital deduction, even if no tax is owed. The return must be filed within nine months of death, with a possible six-month extension. The trust's qualifying structure must be documented on the return.
- **Spouse's income and principal rights.** For a QTIP (Qualified Terminable Interest Property) marital trust, the spouse must receive all income for life, and the trustee cannot distribute principal to anyone else during the spouse's lifetime. For a general power of appointment marital trust, the spouse must have the right to direct who receives the assets at death.
- **Trustee selection and independence.** The trustee must administer the trust according to its terms and cannot favor remainder beneficiaries over the surviving spouse. Independence matters if the trustee is also a remainder beneficiary.
- **Ongoing tax compliance.** The trust typically files Form 1041 annually and must distribute income to the spouse to avoid trust-level taxation. K-1s must be issued to the spouse for distributed income.

## Distribution Rules

- **Income to spouse.** In a QTIP trust, all income must be distributed to the surviving spouse at least annually. The trustee cannot accumulate income inside the trust.
- **Principal distributions.** The trust document governs whether principal may be distributed to the spouse. Many marital trusts permit principal distributions for the spouse's health, education, maintenance, or support (HEMS standard), while others restrict principal entirely.
- **Remainder at spouse's death.** When the surviving spouse dies, the remaining assets pass to the remainder beneficiaries named in the trust (often the couple's children). Those assets are then included in the surviving spouse's estate for estate tax purposes, which is the trade-off for the upfront marital deduction.
- **No distributions to others during spouse's life.** For QTIP qualification, no person other than the spouse can receive distributions during the spouse's lifetime.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Failing the citizenship test.** If the surviving spouse is not a U.S. citizen and the estate did not use a QDOT, the marital deduction is denied and the full estate tax applies at the first death.
- **Missing the Form 706 deadline.** The nine-month deadline is firm. Late-filed returns can lose the marital deduction entirely, producing a tax bill the trust may not have liquid assets to cover.
- **Distributing to non-spouse beneficiaries.** Any distribution to a child or other beneficiary during the spouse's lifetime can disqualify the QTIP election retroactively, exposing the trust to estate tax plus penalties.
- **Accumulating income.** QTIP requires that all income be distributed to the spouse annually. Allowing income to accumulate inside the trust triggers trust-level income tax at compressed rates and risks disqualification.
