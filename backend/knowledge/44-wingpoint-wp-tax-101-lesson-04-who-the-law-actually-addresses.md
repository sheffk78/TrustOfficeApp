# Lesson 4: Who the Law Actually Addresses

**Course:** WP-TAX-101 — The Tax Question  
**Lesson:** 4 of 8  

---

Everything in Lessons 1 through 3 — the constitutional framework, the narrow definitions, the withholding mechanism, the consent system — comes down to one question: **who does the code itself identify as the person subject to the tax?**

Not "who does the law affect?" Everyone is affected. The question is narrower: who does the code actually address?

---

## The Pattern — Definitions That Point in One Direction

In Lesson 1, you saw each definition individually. Now read them together and a pattern emerges:

- **"Employee"** means a federal worker — officers and individuals appointed to the civil service (5 USC §2105).
- **"Employer"** means someone with statutory "employees."
- **"Wages"** means compensation from a statutory "employee" to a statutory "employer" — with twenty-three exceptions.
- **"Trade or business"** means "the performance of the functions of a public office."
- **"Taxpayer"** means someone subject to an internal revenue tax — a legal status that attaches based on whether a specific tax applies to your activity.

Every definition points to federal activity. Employee means federal employee. Employer means someone with federal employees. Wages means compensation from federal employment. Trade or business means performing a public office. Taxpayer is a status that only attaches when a specific tax applies.

The pattern isn't accidental. Statutory construction follows rules: when a statute defines a term, that definition controls the interpretation of every provision that uses it. The definitions are this specific because the tax is this limited — and that limitation exists because the founders believed some powers were beyond government's reach.

There are two more definitions Lesson 1 didn't cover — and they widen the pattern even further.

**"United States"** — 26 USC §7701(a)(9) & (10): When used in a geographical sense, means only the District of Columbia and federal territories — not the 50 Union states as most people understand them. This territorial limitation matters because the code's jurisdiction follows this definition.

**"Individual"** — 26 C.F.R. §1.1441-1(c)(3): In the regulations, "individual" means "an alien individual who is not a citizen or national of the United States." The regulations don't include American citizens in this definition — only aliens subject to federal jurisdiction.

Seven definitions. Seven lines drawn between what people assume and what the code says. Every one narrows the scope. Every one points toward federal activity and away from private life.

---

## The Pattern in Plain Language

If you work at a private company — a hardware store, a software firm, a dental practice — and you perform private labor for private compensation, the code's definitions don't describe your situation. You're not engaged in "the performance of the functions of a public office." You're not a statutory "employee." Your compensation isn't statutory "wages."

But the code has a mechanism for bringing you inside those definitions anyway: your consent. The W-4 you signed. The Social Security Number you use. The information returns filed on your behalf. Each one is an election — a voluntary choice to be treated as if you fit the statutory definitions, even when your actual activity doesn't.

---

## The Social Security Number — Your Federal Personnel ID

Under 5 U.S.C. §552a(a)(13), everyone with a Social Security Number becomes classified as "federal personnel" — which includes anyone "entitled to receive immediate or deferred retirement benefits under any retirement program of the Government of the United States."

Read that in the statute itself. When you received your SSN, you didn't just get a number for identification — you were enrolled in a federal system. And the system uses that enrollment to create jurisdiction where it otherwise might not exist.

Look at your Social Security card. Look at any official correspondence from a government agency. Notice how the name appears. Is it the name your mother gave you, or a variation that exists only on paper? The Social Security Administration creates an entity with a name that looks like yours but is spelled in ALL CAPITAL LETTERS, along with an account number. Look at the back of your card — it says "Property of the Social Security Administration. Must be returned upon request." You're the custodian of government property, not the owner. You didn't get an ID card. You got assigned a federal account number.

When you sign the card, you're agreeing to act as the fiduciary for the card's actual owner — the federal government. This is the connection between the "two yous" — the living person and the commercial persona the system uses as a handle. The distinction between who you are and what the system calls you shows up in the cards you carry and the numbers you use.

Scripture distinguishes between your earthly citizenship and your heavenly one. Paul wrote: "Our citizenship is in heaven" (Philippians 3:20). The tax code, in its own clumsy way, makes a similar distinction — between the living person and the legal fiction created by the system. One is real. One is paper. Understanding which is which isn't just legal literacy. It's spiritual clarity.

## State Citizen vs. Federal Citizen — Two Kinds of Jurisdiction

The code distinguishes between different types of citizenship and jurisdiction. This is in the law itself, and it has real consequences for who the tax code addresses.

The **State Citizens Service Center Manual** — an internal government document — distinguishes between:
- **A state citizen** — a member of one of the several states, owed the full protection of the Constitution, operating under state jurisdiction in private affairs
- **A federal citizen** — a citizen of the United States under the 14th Amendment, a category created after the Civil War, initially for freed slaves who didn't have state citizenship, operating under federal jurisdiction

What this means in practice: if you're a state citizen engaged in private activity within your state, the federal tax code's definitions of "trade or business" (performance of the functions of a public office), "employee" (federal officer or appointee), and "wages" (remuneration for that federal employment) don't describe your activity. You're outside the scope of the tax as written.

The confusion happens because most Americans have been enrolled in both categories simultaneously — you have state citizenship by birth, but you've also been enrolled in federal programs (Social Security, Medicare, federal banking) that create a federal nexus. The question isn't which category you belong to. It's which category your *specific activity* falls under. Private labor for private compensation in a private business? That's state citizen territory. Federal office? Federal employee compensation? That's where the tax code attaches.

*Hodges v. United States* (1906): The Supreme Court held that the 14th and 15th Amendments operate on state action, not individual action. The federal government's authority over individuals is limited to its enumerated powers. A private citizen of a state, engaging in private activity within that state, operates outside the scope of federal taxation unless a specific nexus connects them.

This reinforces what the definitions already say: the federal government's authority is enumerated and limited. It doesn't cover everything by default. The code doesn't accidentally use narrow words — it uses narrow words because the authority behind it is narrow.

## Two Readings — Two Worlds

This distinction — state citizen vs. federal citizen, private activity vs. federal activity — isn't just legal. It's philosophical. It goes to the heart of what you believe about where your rights come from.

If your rights come from God — inherent, unalienable, preceding government — then the Constitution isn't granting you rights. It's recognizing rights you already have. The limits on government power (apportionment, uniformity, narrow definitions) exist because the government's authority is delegated and bounded. The tax code's narrow definitions reflect those bounds. The government can tax the privileged activities it creates. It cannot tax you for existing — not without your consent.

If your rights come from the government — granted by statute, revocable by majority vote — then the definitions are just technical language for the general principle that income is taxable. The government can redefine terms, expand scope, and adjust what's covered. Your consent doesn't matter because the government's authority is total within its domain.

These two views produce two different relationships with the code. One says: the law means what it says, and what it says is limited. The other says: the law means whatever the government says it means, and you'd better comply.

Most people don't realize they've been living inside the second view — the one where government grants rights — even if they'd claim to believe the first. How do you know which one you actually live by? Ask yourself: when you get a form from a government agency, do you feel like you're responding to an authority that owns you, or an authority that works for you? Your gut reaction is the real answer. And the tax code, read honestly, supports the first view.

The founders built a system based on that first view. The Declaration of Independence — "endowed by their Creator with certain unalienable Rights" — places the source of rights above government, not within it. The Constitution's restrictions on federal power — apportionment, uniformity, the Bill of Rights — exist because the founders believed those restrictions came from somewhere higher than the state.

You don't have to accept either view to read the code. The definitions say what they say regardless of your philosophy. But understanding why they say what they say — why the definitions are narrow, why the Constitution draws lines, why the consent system exists to bring you inside lines you don't fit — makes the pattern visible in a way that just reading the statutes alone doesn't.

## Bob's Bicycles — Why Scope Matters

If you're still thinking "but everyone pays income tax," here's a way to see why scope matters even when a tax is real:

Imagine a government that imposes a tax on the sale of bicycles on federal roads. The statute says: "A tax is imposed on the sale of bicycles."

Bob sells bicycles in his own shop, on his own property, to his own customers. He doesn't use federal roads. The tax is on "selling bicycles on federal roads" — because when you read the definitions, "sale" means "a sale conducted on the federal road system."

If you read only the statute and assumed "selling bicycles" meant all bicycle sales, you'd think Bob owes the tax. But the definitions draw the line. Bob isn't evading anything. The tax simply doesn't apply to his activity.

Replace "selling bicycles on federal roads" with "performing the functions of a public office." Replace "Bob's Bicycles" with your private-sector job. The structure is identical.

Bob isn't cheating. He's not exploiting a loophole. He's simply not doing the thing the tax describes. Jesus told the Pharisees: "You have a fine way of setting aside the commands of God in order to observe your own traditions" (Mark 7:9). The tax code's definitions are the commands. The assumption that "everyone pays" is the tradition. Bob read the command and realized the tradition didn't apply to him.

The income tax works the same way. It's a real tax on a real activity. The question is whether the activity it describes is the one you're engaged in.

---

## Where This Leaves You

The definitions are narrow. The Constitution draws lines. The consent system brings you inside lines you don't fit. The question isn't whether the law says what it says — it does. The question is whether your specific situation falls within what it describes.

Look at your last pay stub or W-2. Does the compensation you receive fit within the statutory definitions? Is your private-sector employment a "public office"? Are you a statutory "employee" appointed by federal authority?

Not what should you do about it. Just: does it fit?
