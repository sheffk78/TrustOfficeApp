# UTC Doctrine 06 — Successor Trustees, Vacancies, and Getting Out

> ⚠️ **Scope note — resignation and removal carry continuing obligations.**
> Under the Code, resigning or being removed does not discharge a trustee's liability for acts and omissions, and a resigned or removed trustee keeps the duties of a trustee and the powers necessary to protect the trust property until delivery. Resignation ends the job; it does not exit responsibility. Removal is not a verdict on wrongdoing — the Code lists several grounds for it, including unfitness, unwillingness, persistent failure to administer effectively, and substantial change of circumstances.

**UTC anchor:** art. 7 — ss. 701, 702, 703, 704, 705, 706, 707, 708, 709; art. 8 — ss. 811, 812, 813(c), 1001

## Why this matters

Trustee succession is a live risk for the solo trustee in a way distribution disputes are not — because a successor has to be able to take over, and a departing trustee has to be able to leave cleanly. This section answers those three questions in order.

## Becoming trustee: accepting, declining, and the duty that starts

**701(a):** a designated trustee accepts (1) by substantially complying with a method of acceptance provided in the trust's terms, or (2) if there is no method, or it is not expressly exclusive, by accepting delivery of the trust property, exercising powers or performing duties as trustee, or otherwise indicating acceptance.

**701(b):** a designated trustee who has not yet accepted may reject the trusteeship, and one who does not accept is not liable for acts or omissions before acceptance.

**701(c)** lets a designated trustee, without accepting, do two limited things: act to preserve the trust property, if within a reasonable time afterward a rejection is sent to the settlor (or a qualified beneficiary if the settlor is dead or lacks capacity); and inspect trust property for potential liability under other law.

The practical sequence for a new trustee: this is the moment to decide. Section 801 attaches the duties upon acceptance, and s. 813(b)(2) starts a 60-day notice clock. Refusing is a legal right, not a failure — the Trustee-101 Lesson 2 point ("declining is a legal right, not a weakness") is exactly what 701(b) provides. A trustee who starts moving money without accepting has accepted under 701(a)(2).

Section 702 covers bonds. A trustee shall give bond only if the court finds one is needed to protect the beneficiaries' interests, or the trust's terms require it and the court has not dispensed with the requirement. The court may specify amount, liabilities, and whether sureties are necessary, and may modify or terminate a bond at any time. A court will usually not require a bond from a family trustee — and never sign a personal bond without reading it.

## Co-trustees

**703(a):** cotrustees unable to reach a unanimous decision may act by majority decision. **703(b):** if a vacancy occurs in a cotrusteeship, the remaining cotrustees may act for the trust. **703(c):** a cotrustee must participate in a trustee's function unless unavailable through absence, illness, disqualification under other law, or other temporary incapacity, or the function has been delegated. **703(d):** if a cotrustee is unavailable and prompt action is necessary, the remaining cotrustee or a majority of them may act.

**703(e):** a trustee may not delegate to a cotrustee a function the settlor reasonably expected the trustees to perform jointly. Unless irrevocable, a delegation may be revoked. **703(f):** except as provided in (g), a trustee who does not join in an action of another trustee is not liable for it. **703(g):** each trustee shall exercise reasonable care to prevent a cotrustee from committing a serious breach of trust, and to compel a cotrustee to redress one. **703(h):** a dissenting trustee who joins in an action at the majority's direction, having notified any cotrustee, is not liable unless it is a serious breach.

The pairing of (f) and (g) is the heart of it: not joining is the protection, not noticing is not. A co-trustee has an affirmative duty to watch a fellow trustee and act against a serious breach. Section 1001(b)(7) lets a court remove a trustee, and (b)(5) appoint a special fiduciary.

## Vacancy: what it is and who fills it

**704(a) — when a vacancy exists.** A vacancy occurs if: (1) a designated trustee rejects the trusteeship; (2) a designated trustee cannot be identified or does not exist; (3) a trustee resigns; (4) a trustee is disqualified or removed; (5) a trustee dies; or (6) a guardian or conservator is appointed for an individual trustee.

**704(c) — noncharitable trusts.** A vacancy that must be filled is filled in priority order: (1) a person designated in the trust's terms as successor trustee; (2) a person appointed by unanimous agreement of the qualified beneficiaries; or (3) a person appointed by the court.

Two things to take away. The designated successor in the document comes first — so a solo trustee should confirm that provision exists and that the named person is alive, willing, and reachable. And unanimous beneficiary agreement is the second route, meaning beneficiary consensus is a legal mechanism, not just a courtesy. A successor clause naming no one drops the trust to route (2) or (3).

## Resignation

**705(a):** a trustee may resign (1) upon at least 30 days' notice to the qualified beneficiaries, the settlor or the settlor's agent or successor, and — if a cotrustee remains — the cotrustee, or (2) with the court's approval.

**705(c):** any liability of a resigning trustee, or of any sureties on the trustee's bond, for acts or omissions is not discharged or affected by the resignation.

That (c) is the sentence to carry most carefully. Resignation ends the job; it does not end the exposure for what happened during it. The mitigation is the record and the timing — s. 813(c) requires the final report, s. 707 expeditious delivery.

## Removal

**706(a):** the settlor, a cotrustee, or a beneficiary may request the court to remove a trustee.

**706(b):** the court may remove a trustee if (1) the trustee committed a serious breach of trust; (2) lack of cooperation among cotrustees substantially impairs administration; (3) because of unfitness, unwillingness, or persistent failure to administer effectively, removal best serves the beneficiaries' interests; or (4) there has been a substantial change of circumstances, or removal is requested by all qualified beneficiaries, and removal best serves their interests, is not inconsistent with a material purpose, and a suitable successor is available.

**706(c):** pending a final decision on a removal request, or in lieu of removal, the court may order an accounting, require performance of duties, or appoint a special fiduciary.

Ground (3) surprises families. A trustee can be removed for persistent failure to administer effectively — no breach required, no dishonesty implied. And (c) is why a dispute can get expensive before anyone is removed: the court can order accounting, performance, or a special fiduciary.

## Delivering the property on the way out

**707(a):** unless a cotrustee remains or the court otherwise orders, and until the trust property is delivered to a successor trustee or other person entitled to it, a resigned or removed trustee keeps the duties of a trustee and the powers necessary to protect the property.

**707(b):** a trustee who has resigned or been removed shall proceed expeditiously to deliver the trust property to the successor or other person entitled to it.

Two closing obligations belong on a resignation checklist: the final report under s. 813(c) and the expeditious delivery under s. 707(b). Section 812 reinforces it: a successor trustee shall take reasonable steps to compel a former trustee to deliver trust property, and to redress a breach of trust known to have been committed by one. A handover deserves documentation — inventory, statements, deeds, keys, receipt.

## What the departing trustee is owed

Section 708: if the trust's terms do not specify compensation, a trustee is entitled to compensation reasonable under the circumstances. If they do, the trustee is entitled to it as specified, but a court may adjust it if the duties are now substantially different, or the amount is unreasonably low or high. Section 709: a trustee is entitled to reimbursement out of the trust property, with interest as appropriate, for expenses properly incurred in administration, and — to prevent unjust enrichment — for expenses not properly incurred. A departing trustee who was never compensated should document what they advanced and are claiming, because s. 709(a)(2) reads against them otherwise.

### What this means for a trustee

- Confirm your trust names a successor trustee, and that the named person knows it. Section 704(c) puts the document's designation first in the fill order, and a name nobody has discussed is not a plan.
- If you are asked to serve, decide deliberately. Section 701(b) preserves the right to decline, and 701(c) lets you take protective action before sending a rejection. Once you touch the property, you have accepted under 701(a)(2).
- On acceptance, start the s. 813(b)(2) clock: notify qualified beneficiaries of the acceptance, your name, address, and telephone number, within 60 days.
- If you are resigning, give at least 30 days' notice under s. 705(a)(1) to the qualified beneficiaries, the settlor if living, and all cotrustees. Keep proof of delivery.
- Do not treat resignation as a clean break. Section 705(c) preserves liability for your acts and omissions, and s. 707(a) keeps your duties alive until the property is delivered.
- Before you hand over, produce the final report under s. 813(c) and the accounting under s. 1001(b)(4) if one has been ordered. Get a signed receipt; s. 707(b) says expeditiously.
- If you are a co-trustee, remember 703(g): you have an affirmative duty to watch for a serious breach by your co-trustee and act against it.

### Common question we get

**"I want to step down as trustee. Does that end my responsibility?"**
No — and this is the one not to get wrong. Section 705(c) says liability for your acts and omissions is not discharged or affected by resignation. Section 707(a) keeps your duties and protective powers alive until the property is delivered to a successor or other person entitled to it. Resignation ends the job. What stays on your side is the record you built and the clean handover you documented: the final report, the accounting, and a signed receipt.

**"The trust document names a successor. Am I done worrying?"**
Not entirely, but you have done the highest-value thing. Section 704(c) fills a noncharitable vacancy in priority order: the person designated in the trust terms first, then a person appointed by unanimous agreement of the qualified beneficiaries, then the court. The designation only works if it names someone who exists, is willing, and is reachable. If it names a deceased person or someone who will not serve, you have dropped to route two or three, which is slower and more expensive. Check the designation, and tell the named person.

**"Can I be removed just for being bad at this?"**
Yes. Section 706(b)(3) lets the court remove a trustee where, because of unfitness, unwillingness, or persistent failure to administer the trust effectively, removal best serves the beneficiaries' interests. No breach and no dishonesty are required. That is a real risk for a trustee who simply stops engaging, and the mitigation is the same as everything else here: keep the reviews, keep the records, keep showing up. If you cannot continue, resign properly under s. 705.
