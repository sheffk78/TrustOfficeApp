# Asset Protection Trust

## What It Is

An Asset Protection Trust (APT) is a trust designed to shield assets from the settlor's future creditors while still allowing the settlor to benefit from those assets. This distinguishes it from a traditional spendthrift trust, which protects beneficiaries but not the settlor. APTs come in two main forms: a Domestic Asset Protection Trust (DAPT), established under the laws of a U.S. state that has enacted self-settled trust legislation, and a Foreign Asset Protection Trust (FAPT), established in an offshore jurisdiction like the Cook Islands. Both structures attempt to do what common law traditionally prohibited — letting a settlor shield their own assets from their own creditors — by relying on specific statutory frameworks.

## Key Governance Requirements

- The trust must be established and administered in a DAPT jurisdiction (Alaska, Delaware, Nevada, South Dakota, and a handful of others) or an FAPT jurisdiction. The choice of law must be explicit in the trust document, and the trustee, administration, and assets should align with that jurisdiction's statutory requirements (for example, a Nevada DAPT generally requires a Nevada trustee and at least some Nevada-based administration).
- The settlor must part with sufficient control that the assets are no longer "reachable" — most DAPT statutes require an independent trustee or a qualified trustee resident in the jurisdiction, and many require the settlor to give up the unrestricted right to revoke.
- The 10-year lookback period for bankruptcy applies: under federal bankruptcy law (11 U.S.C. § 548(e)), transfers to a self-settled trust within 10 years before the bankruptcy filing can be avoided. Outside bankruptcy, state-law fraudulent transfer rules apply, and the lookback is shorter but still significant.
- Fraudulent transfer rules prohibit transferring assets into the APT with the intent to hinder, delay, or defraud existing creditors, or when the settlor is insolvent at the time of transfer. A transfer that renders the settlor insolvent is presumptively fraudulent even without bad intent, so the settlor should retain sufficient assets and obtain solvency affidavits.
- The trust must typically include some distribution standard (HEMS — health, education, maintenance, or support — is common) that gives the independent trustee discretion to distribute to the settlor without making the assets automatically reachable.

## Distribution Rules

- The independent trustee has discretion to distribute income or principal to the settlor under the trust's distribution standard (typically HEMS), but the settlor cannot demand distributions as a matter of right — if they could, creditors could too.
- Distributions to beneficiaries other than the settlor follow the trust terms and should be documented with the trustee's discretionary reasoning.
- If the settlor is under creditor attack, the trustee may need to suspend distributions to the settlor and redirect to other beneficiaries, which the trust document should expressly authorize.
- Any distribution to the settlor, once received, becomes reachable by the settlor's creditors — the protection lives inside the trust, not in the settlor's pocket.

## Common Pitfalls

- Funding the trust when already facing creditor claims or when insolvent — this is a fraudulent transfer that courts will unwind, defeating the trust and potentially exposing the settlor to sanctions.
- Keeping too much control (serving as sole trustee, retaining unrestricted power to revoke, or directing distributions) — courts may disregard the trust as the settlor's alter ego and allow creditors to reach the assets.
- Failing to maintain a qualified trustee in the DAPT jurisdiction or to meet annual reporting requirements, which can cause the trust to lose the statutory creditor shield.
- Assuming an FAPT provides absolute protection — while offshore jurisdictions do not enforce U.S. judgments, a U.S. court can still hold the settlor in contempt for refusing to repatriate assets, and enforcement risk in bankruptcy remains real.
