# UTC Doctrine 01 — What the Uniform Trust Code Covers, and the Words It Uses

> ⚠️ **Scope note — the governing law is a fact you have to check.**
> The Code's own rules turn on where a trust is administered, and most states adopt the Uniform Trust Code in their own words, in part, or not at all. Check your trust document and the law of the state named in it before assuming any section here applies to your trust. Which law governs is an attorney question in the governing state.

**UTC anchor:** art. 1 — ss. 101, 102, 103, 104, 105, 106, 107, 108, 109, 110, 111, 113; art. 10 — ss. 1001–1013

## Why this matters

A trustee who reads the Code cold hits walls of defined terms before reaching a single duty. "Qualified beneficiary." "Terms of a trust." "Ascertainable standard." The words look like obstacles. They are not — they are the Code telling you exactly which facts the rest of the statute will be measured against. This section pulls the Article 1 vocabulary apart and puts it in a solo trustee's terms, so that when a duty, a deadline, or a notice requirement lands later, the nouns already make sense.

## The Code's own scope

Section 101 names the Act. Section 102 defines what it reaches: express trusts, charitable or noncharitable, and trusts created by statute, judgment, or decree that must be administered like an express trust. Notice what is *not* in that list. An implied trust, a constructive trust, and a trust so informal it was never declared do not get the Code's full machinery of duties and reports. This is why the very first thing to establish is whether the arrangement in front of you is a trust at all.

Section 402 sets the creation requirements, and they are worth reading as a checklist: the settlor has capacity, shows intent to create a trust, the trust has a definite beneficiary (or is charitable, for an animal, or for a noncharitable purpose), the trustee has duties to perform, and the same person is not both sole trustee and sole beneficiary. That last rule is the reason a trust cannot be a device for a person to leave assets to themselves with a fiduciary in name only.

## The words that carry the most weight

Section 103 is the definitions section, and five terms do most of the work in the rest of the Code.

**"Beneficiary"** covers a person with a present or future beneficial interest, vested or contingent, and also a person who, in a capacity other than trustee, holds a power of appointment over trust property. The second half matters: a person with a power of appointment is treated as a beneficiary for Code purposes even though they receive nothing today.

**"Qualified beneficiary"** is the recipient of almost every notice and report duty in the Code. The test is forward-looking: the person must be a distributee or permissible distributee of income or principal, would be one if current distributees' interests terminated, or would be one if the trust terminated. In practice this usually means current beneficiaries, remainder beneficiaries, and in some trusts the settlor while living. A person with a contingent interest that has not vested may not qualify.

**"Terms of a trust"** means the manifestation of the settlor's intent, expressed in the instrument or established by other evidence admissible in a court, as that intent may be amended by a trustee or other person in accordance with law, by court order, or by a nonjudicial settlement agreement. Note the last two. "The terms of the trust" is not a fixed piece of paper. It can move.

**"Trustee"** includes original, additional, and successor trustees, and cotrustees. So when a duty says "a trustee," it reaches every person who has ever held the office in that capacity, and the rules about delivering property on the way out apply to the outgoing one.

**"Revocable"** means revocable by the settlor without the consent of the trustee or a person holding an adverse interest. That definition is doing quiet work: a trust the settlor can revoke only with the trustee's agreement is not revocable under this definition, and the consequences for creditor rights and beneficiary expectations are substantial.

**"Spendthrift provision"** is defined as a term restraining both voluntary and involuntary transfer of a beneficiary's interest. That is a narrower definition than most trustees assume. A clause stopping the beneficiary from selling their interest but not stopping a court from garnishing it is not a spendthrift provision under the Code.

## The default rule, and the exceptions to it

Section 105 is the single most consequential section for a solo trustee. Its default: unless the trust document says otherwise, the Code governs the trustee's duties and powers, the relations among trustees, and the rights and interests of beneficiaries.

The exceptions are where the Code refuses to be overridden. The terms of a trust do **not** prevail over: the requirements for creating a trust; the duty to act in good faith and in accordance with the trust's terms and purposes and the beneficiaries' interests; the requirement that a trust be for the beneficiaries' benefit with a lawful, possible purpose; the court's power to modify or terminate; the effect of spendthrift provisions and certain creditors' rights; the court's power over bonds; the court's power to adjust unreasonably low or high trustee compensation; the effect of exculpatory terms; the rights of non-trustees and non-beneficiaries under ss. 1010 through 1013; and limitation periods for court actions.

The practical translation: a settlor cannot write a clause that says "trustee owes no duty of loyalty." A settlor also cannot write a clause that says "trustee is protected from every claim." Read your trust document for what it grants; read the Code for what it cannot take away.

Section 106 confirms that the common law of trusts and principles of equity still fill the gaps the Code does not address.

## Knowledge, governing law, and where the trust is run

Section 104 defines "knowledge" in three ways: actual knowledge, having received a notice, or having reason to know from the facts and circumstances. It also addresses organizational trustees, who have notice only of what a responsible employee received or would have received with reasonable diligence. This matters when a trust is administered through a bank or a corporate trustee rather than a person.

Section 107 picks the governing law: the law designated in the trust terms, unless that designation runs against a strong public policy of the jurisdiction with the most significant relationship to the matter; otherwise, the law of the jurisdiction with the most significant relationship to the matter. Section 108 then asks a different question — the *principal place of administration*, meaning where the trust is actually run. A trustee has a continuing duty to administer the trust at a place appropriate to its purposes, its administration, and the beneficiaries' interests, and may move that place, with notice to qualified beneficiaries in advance of the move and a stated window for them to object.

Two different concepts, two different answers. A trust can be governed by one state's law and administered in another.

Section 109 sets the method of notice: reasonably suitable under the circumstances and likely to result in receipt, including first-class mail, personal delivery, delivery to a last known address, or a properly directed electronic message. Notice need not be given to a person whose identity or location is unknown and not reasonably ascertainable. A beneficiary may waive notice.

Section 110 extends the reach of the qualified-beneficiary notice rules: any other beneficiary who asks the trustee for notice must also receive notice whenever the Code requires notice to qualified beneficiaries. Section 111 allows interested persons to resolve trust matters by nonjudicial settlement agreement — interpretation of terms, approval of a report, resignation or appointment of a trustee, compensation, a transfer of the principal place of administration, and trustee liability — provided the agreement does not violate a material purpose of the trust and could have been approved by a court.

Section 113 addresses a trustee's insurable interest in the life of an individual insured under a policy the trust owns.

### What this means for a trustee

- Before anything else, confirm the trust is an express trust with a definite beneficiary, and that the same person is not sole trustee and sole beneficiary.
- Learn your document's designation of governing law, and separately work out where the trust is actually administered. If those differ, both matter.
- Read the document for what it grants you. Read the Code for what it will not let the document take away — the good-faith duty, the beneficiary-benefit requirement, and the court's modification power are the big three.
- When a deadline in this pack refers to a "qualified beneficiary," check whether the person in question is one under s. 103 before assuming a notice obligation applies.
- Keep a record of notices you send, when you sent them, and how — s. 109's method rules and s. 108(d)'s transfer notice are both record-driven.

### Common question we get

**"Does the Code's definition of qualified beneficiary mean my remainder beneficiary never gets anything?"**
It means the opposite. A remainder beneficiary typically *is* a qualified beneficiary, because the test asks whether the person would be a distributee if current interests ended or if the trust terminated. That is why notice and reporting duties reach beneficiaries who have received nothing yet. It also means a contingent beneficiary whose interest has not vested may fall outside the definition — and outside the notice rules that attach to it.

**"My trust says it is governed by my home state, but I moved. Which law applies?"**
Two separate answers live in Article 1. The governing law is the one your document designates, unless that designation conflicts with a strong public policy of the state with the most significant relationship to the matter. The principal place of administration is where the trust is actually run, and s. 108 lets a trustee move it to a place appropriate to the trust's purposes and the beneficiaries' interests — with advance notice to qualified beneficiaries and a stated window for objection. This is the question to take to an attorney in the governing state.

**"Can I rely on what the trust document says, even if it seems to contradict the Code?"**
Not entirely. Section 105 makes the document the default source of the trustee's duties and powers, but the document cannot override the Code's core protections: the good-faith duty, the requirement that the trust benefit its beneficiaries and have a lawful and possible purpose, the spendthrift and creditor rules, and the limits on exculpation. Section 1006 separately protects a trustee who acts in reasonable reliance on the document's terms — but that is a defense to be established, not permission to skip the check.
