# Charitable Lead Trust (CLT)

## What It Is
A charitable lead trust (CLT) is an irrevocable trust that pays income to one or more qualified charities for a specified term of years (or for someone's life), with the remainder passing to non-charitable beneficiaries — typically the grantor's family members. It is essentially the mirror image of a charitable remainder trust: the charity gets the income stream, and the family gets the remainder. CLTs are used by individuals who want to support a charity over a sustained period while transferring appreciating assets to family at a reduced transfer-tax cost. There are two primary structures: the charitable lead annuity trust (CLAT), which pays a fixed dollar amount annually to charity, and the charitable lead unitrust (CLUT), which pays a fixed percentage of the trust's annually revalued assets. CLTs can be structured as either grantor trusts (the grantor gets an immediate income tax deduction) or non-grantor trusts (no immediate income tax deduction, but the trust gets an income tax deduction for amounts paid to charity).

## Key Governance Requirements
- **File Form 5227 or Form 1041, depending on structure.** Non-grantor CLTs file Form 5227, similar to CRTs. Grantor CLTs file Form 1041 and a related Schedule, with income and deductions flowing to the grantor's personal return. The trustee must understand which structure applies and file accordingly.
- **Fixed annual payments to charity.** The CLT must make the stated annual payment to the qualified charity each year of the trust term. CLATs pay a fixed dollar amount; CLUTs pay a fixed percentage of annual value. Missing a payment can cause the trust to fail its qualified status.
- **Charity must be a qualified organization.** Payments must go to organizations described in IRC Section 170(c). The trustee must verify the charity's qualified status and obtain written acknowledgment (Contemporaneous Written Acknowledgment for contributions of $250 or more).
- **Grantor CLT income tax deduction.** If the trust is structured as a grantor CLT, the grantor receives an immediate income tax deduction (subject to adjusted gross income percentage limitations and carryforward rules) for the present value of the charitable income interest at the time the trust is funded. The trustee and grantor's CPA must coordinate to claim this deduction properly.
- **Non-grantor CLT receives a charitable deduction for amounts paid.** A non-grantor CLT deducts the amount paid to charity each year on its own return. Excess deductions may carry forward. The trustee must track and apply these correctly.
- **Term must be definite.** The trust term is typically a fixed number of years. The trustee must track the term countdown and plan for the remainder distribution at term end.

## Distribution Rules
- **Charity receives income during the trust term.** In a CLAT, the charity receives the same fixed dollar amount each year. In a CLUT, the charity receives a percentage of the trust's annually revalued assets. Payments must be made on schedule.
- **Family receives the remainder at term end.** When the trust term ends, the remaining principal passes to the non-charitable remainder beneficiaries — typically the grantor's children or other family members. The trustee must prepare for this distribution.
- **No minimum or maximum payout percentage for CLTs.** Unlike CRTs, there is no statutory minimum payout percentage for CLTs. However, the payout amount affects the charitable deduction and the transfer-tax value of the remainder interest.
- **Remainder beneficiaries cannot include the grantor (for non-grantor CLTs).** In a non-grantor CLT, the grantor generally cannot be a remainder beneficiary, or the transfer-tax benefits may be compromised. The trustee must understand who the remainder beneficiaries are and ensure the structure is respected.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Missing annual payments to charity.** The trustee must make the stated payment to the qualified charity each year. If the trust earns less than the annuity amount (in a CLAT), principal must be invaded to satisfy the payment. Failing to make the payment jeopardizes the trust's qualified status.
- **Filing the wrong tax form.** Grantor CLTs and non-grantor CLTs have different filing requirements. A grantor CLT files Form 1041 with grantor information attached, while a non-grantor CLT files Form 5227. Filing the wrong form leads to penalties and possible loss of the charitable deduction.
- **Failing to verify charity status.** Charities can lose their qualified status. The trustee must verify the charity's status (via IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search) before each payment. A payment to a non-qualified organization is not a valid charitable distribution and can trigger adverse tax consequences.
- **Misunderstanding the income tax deduction.** Grantor CLTs provide an immediate income tax deduction for the present value of the charitable income interest, but this deduction is subject to AGI limitations (generally 30% or 60% of AGI depending on the type of charity and asset contributed) and carryforward rules (typically 5 years). The grantor and CPA must coordinate to use the deduction efficiently before it expires.
- **No plan for the remainder distribution.** When the trust term ends, the entire remaining principal must be distributed to the family beneficiaries. Trustees who have not planned for this transition can face delays, beneficiary disputes, or tax complications. The Governance Calendar should flag the term-end date well in advance.
