# Lesson 3: The Withholding Illusion

## How Withholding Makes It Feel Automatic

Money disappears from your paycheck before you see it. Federal income tax. State income tax. FICA. The number on your stub is already reduced. You never held that money. You never decided how much to send.

That feeling — automatic, involuntary, like the money was never really yours — is the product of the withholding system. How withholding works and how the law works are two different things.

And because you never held it, you never felt the weight of the decision. The government didn't have to convince you to part with $400 from a $2,000 check. They just made sure you never saw the full $2,000 in the first place. This is the genius of withholding: it severs the connection between your labor and the tax. You don't experience it as a payment. You experience it as a smaller paycheck. And a smaller paycheck feels like reality, not like a choice you made.

Joe Banister, the former IRS Criminal Investigation Division Special Agent, spent two years researching after asking his supervisors to show him the law that required most Americans to pay income tax. They couldn't give him an answer that satisfied his own standards as a criminal investigator. He resigned. We'll return to what he found. First, let's look at the code sections that make withholding work — and the ones that reveal its limits.

---

## What the Code Says About Withholding

### §3402(a) — Withholding applies only to "wages" as defined

The employer shall deduct and withhold income tax from "wages" — as defined in §3401, with its twenty-three exceptions. Not from all compensation. Not from every dollar earned by every worker.

If the compensation you receive doesn't fall within the statutory definition of "wages" (from Lesson 1: remuneration to a statutory "employee" from a statutory "employer" engaged in a "trade or business"), the withholding authority in §3402(a) doesn't attach to it.

### §3402(d) — If the employee pays, the employer isn't liable

Why would this provision exist if withholding were mandatory for every dollar earned by every worker? If withholding were absolute, the employer would always be liable. But §3402(d) says the employer isn't liable if the employee pays the tax directly — because the tax can be paid directly, and withholding is just one mechanism for collecting it.

### §3402(n) — Exemption from withholding

The code even provides for exemption from withholding entirely — §3402(n). If you had no income tax liability last year and expect none this year, the W-4 has an exemption line. The system has a built-in mechanism for people who fall outside the default. The exemption isn't hidden — it's in the form, in the code, available to anyone who reads it.

## The W-4 — What You're Actually Signing

In Lesson 2, you encountered the legal maxim "he who consents cannot receive an injury." The W-4 is where most people gave that consent — without knowing it. Now let's look at exactly what this form is and what the regulations say about it.

The W-4 is an "Employee's Withholding Allowance Certificate" under §3402(f). A form that tells your employer how much to withhold based on what you claim about your status — neither a contract nor a confession.

But here's what most people never learn: under 26 C.F.R. §31.3402(p)-1, the W-4 is described as a **"voluntary withholding agreement."** The regulation itself calls it voluntary. Your employer can pay you without withholding anything if you don't sign one — they're only required to withhold from statutory "wages" paid to statutory "employees."

When you sign a W-4, you're making an "election" — electing to be treated as a statutory "employee" for withholding purposes. You're voluntarily entering a withholding agreement that treats your private compensation as if it were federal "wages." The regulation itself calls it voluntary. The word is in the text.

When you fill out a W-4 on your first day at a new job — because HR told you to, while you're thinking about the job and not the legal implications of each line — you're making declarations about your status under terms you weren't told have narrow statutory meanings.

## Information Returns — How the System Creates "Evidence"

Information returns — W-2s, 1099s, 1042-S forms — are documents that third parties file with the IRS claiming you received money or engaged in federal activities. These forms create what the law calls **"prima facie evidence"** — meaning they're presumed to be true unless you prove otherwise. The term comes from Latin: "at first sight." The forms are treated as true on their face.

Here's the problem: your employer files a W-2 that reports your private earnings as "wages" subject to federal income tax. But you now know from Lesson 1 that "wages" only means payments to federal "employees" for federal employment services. If you're working at a private company in the private sector, that W-2 may be creating false evidence about your legal status — a record that says you're something the law says you're not.

When your employer files that W-2, they're telling the IRS that you're engaged in a "trade or business" — which means "the performance of the functions of a public office." They're claiming you're a federal employee when you probably aren't. And most employers have no idea they're doing this — they're just filling out the forms their CPA told them to fill out, using the common-English meanings of the words, not the statutory ones.

The same happens with 1099 forms. If you do contract work, the business that paid you files a 1099 claiming you received income from federal activities — creating presumptive evidence that you're engaged in federal employment or federal franchises, even when your work is entirely private and non-federal.

These information returns become the foundation for IRS enforcement actions. The IRS uses them as "evidence" that you owe taxes, even though the forms themselves may be inaccurate when applied to your situation. And most people never realize they have the right to challenge these information returns or correct them when they're wrong.

The law at 26 U.S.C. §7434 makes it a crime to file false information returns — but the IRS rarely enforces this against the businesses that file them incorrectly.

You have the right to request corrected information returns from your employer. If they refuse, you have the right to file your own documentation with the IRS explaining why the original return is inaccurate. Lesson 6 will walk through exactly how. For now: the forms that say you owe are not the final word. You have recourse.

This is how the system manufactures "taxpayers" out of people who may not fit the statutory definition. Third parties create records about you, and then the government uses those records to claim you owe taxes. It's evidence creation without your knowledge or consent.

## The CTC Suppression Story — Why It Matters

In 2003, Pete Hendrickson published *Cracking the Code*, arguing — based on the statutory definitions you've been reading — that most private-sector compensation doesn't fall within the code's definition of "wages" because most private-sector workers aren't engaged in the "trade or business" the code defines.

What happened next tells you more about the system than about Pete.

**First, the government tried to enjoin the book** — claiming it promoted an "abusive tax shelter." The DOJ filed suits in multiple courts. Every one of them was dismissed, with the DOJ moving for dismissal of its own cases. The arguments in CtC couldn't be refuted on their merits, so the government tried to suppress them instead.

**Then came a disinformation campaign** — IRS-friendly websites smeared Pete personally, and the IRS placed CtC's filing method on its "Dirty Dozen" list by deliberately mischaracterizing what the book actually says. The book doesn't argue that "wages aren't income" or that "only government workers pay taxes" — the government's own filings later admitted this, after initially claiming otherwise.

**Then came a civil lawsuit** — *United States v. Peter and Doreen Hendrickson* — alleging that the complete refunds the Hendricksons received were "mistakes." But the government had been surveilling Pete since before the book was even published. The IRS had summonsed his payroll records before issuing the refunds. The DOJ's own documentation showed these weren't mistakes at all — they were deliberate acknowledgments that the filings were correct.

**Finally, they prosecuted Pete criminally** — not for being wrong about the law, but for contempt of court. The court had ordered Pete to repudiate his own sworn testimony and declare things he didn't believe. He refused. He was convicted of contempt, not of tax fraud. The jury was instructed that they could not consider whether the court order was lawful — only whether Pete had violated it.

Pete served his time. He's out. He's still writing. The book is still in print. And here's the most important part:

**The government kept issuing refunds the entire time.** While prosecuting Pete for making the arguments that produced those refunds, federal and state agencies continued acknowledging the accuracy of CtC-educated filings by returning every penny withheld — Social Security and Medicare included — to thousands of Americans. The government's own actions contradicted its prosecution.

**Bad arguments get rebutted.** They get debunked in court. They get addressed on their merits. **The government didn't rebut Pete's arguments — it tried to silence them.** That's the tell.

This isn't the first time truth-tellers have been persecuted for saying what the powerful didn't want heard. The prophets were imprisoned. The apostles were beaten. The message was never "be quiet for your own safety." It was "speak the truth, and let the consequences be what they may." Pete Hendrickson isn't a prophet. But he did what the prophets did: he said what was true, and he paid a price for it. The question for you isn't whether you'll be persecuted. It's whether you'll let the threat of persecution stop you from reading what's written.

The system went to extraordinary lengths — fabricating claims, mischaracterizing the book, issuing unlawful court orders — because the law itself is on your side. If the law didn't say what it says, they could have just shown that in court. They didn't. They chose suppression over rebuttal. And despite all of it, the refunds kept coming. The law works when you understand it and follow it correctly. The refunds are documented in government-issued checks.

What Pete's story teaches isn't "this will happen to you." It's the opposite: **the system is so threatened by the truth that it broke its own rules to suppress it.** If the truth weren't true, none of that would have been necessary.
