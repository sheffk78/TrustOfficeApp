# Lesson 8: When You Need Help — Finding the Right Counsel

**Course:** WP-DEFEND — Standing Strong: Defending Your Trust and Your Position  
**Lesson:** 8 of 10  

---

You can do a lot on your own. Read the statutes. File your corrected returns. Keep your records. Respond to notices. Organize your binder.

But there will come a point where you need help from someone who has been down this road before — someone who has filed the responses, sat across from an IRS agent, walked into a courtroom, and presented this position to a reviewing authority.

And here's the thing most people miss: **seeking counsel isn't a personal shortcoming — it's a trustee decision.** A good trustee knows when to seek competent counsel. It's a fiduciary duty, not a failure. A trustee who represents the trust in a complex proceeding without counsel isn't being brave — they're being reckless with the trust's interests. Seeking counsel for complex matters IS the trustee's job.

When to make that call, how to find the right person, and how to walk into their office ready.

---

## The CPA Who Doesn't Understand You Is Not Your Failure — It's a Filter

If you walk into a typical CPA's office and explain that you've filed a corrected return zeroing out your wages because you're not a statutory "employee" under §3401(c), the most likely response is confusion, dismissal, or a polite version of "you can't do that."

That CPA was trained in the common understanding of the tax code — the one where "wages" means all compensation and "employee" means anyone who works. They've never read §3401(c) with the care you have. They've never encountered a client who operates through a private irrevocable trust and files from a position that challenges the assumptions their profession is built on.

That's a filter. You just learned that this particular professional isn't the one you need — which is useful information. You're not looking for someone who agrees with everything. You're looking for someone with specific experience with specific structures. This is a search problem, not a loneliness problem.

The same applies to most general practice attorneys. They may be excellent at what they do — but what they do isn't what you need. Move on quickly and look for the right fit.

The code's definitions are narrow. Your records are clean. Your filings are consistent. And the burden is on them. When you walk into a professional's office with that foundation, you're not asking them to believe in your philosophy — you're asking them to help you navigate a process. That's a very different conversation, and most competent professionals can engage with it.

---

## The Difference Between Legal Counsel, Tax Professionals, and Advocates

Not all help is the same. Understanding the difference will save you time, money, and frustration.

**Legal counsel** is an attorney who can represent you in court, file motions on your behalf, and give you formal legal advice. You need legal counsel when: you're facing a Tax Court proceeding, you've received a Notice of Deficiency, you're being audited and the scope has expanded beyond a simple correspondence audit, or you're involved in any legal proceeding where your filing position or trust structure is directly at issue.

**A tax professional** — a CPA, an enrolled agent (a practitioner licensed by the IRS to represent taxpayers before the IRS, distinct from a CPA who focuses on accounting and tax preparation), or a tax preparer — understands the tax code and can help you file correctly. You need a tax professional when: you need help preparing your corrected return, you want someone to review your filing before you submit it, or you need someone who can communicate with the IRS on your behalf. The ones who understand your position are out there — and they're worth finding.

**An advocate** is someone who understands your position, can help you communicate it clearly, and can guide you through the process — but who may not be licensed to practice law or give formal legal advice. Advocates can help you draft responses, organize your records, understand your notices, and prepare for proceedings. They are not a substitute for legal counsel when legal counsel is needed, but they can be invaluable for the day-to-day work of maintaining your position.

You may need all three at different points. You may need none of them for routine matters. The key is knowing when each is appropriate.

## When to Handle It Yourself vs. When to Bring in Help

**Handle it yourself when:**

- You receive a CP2000 or similar notice proposing an adjustment, and you understand what it's asking and how to respond.
- You need to file a corrected return or 4852 and you've done it before (or you've studied the process carefully and feel confident).
- You're organizing your trust binder, writing minutes, or maintaining your records.
- You're handling routine correspondence — responding to a straightforward question from the IRS, your bank, or a local agency.

**Bring in help when:**

- You receive a Notice of Deficiency (90-day letter). This is a formal determination that escalates your matter to Tax Court if you don't respond. Get counsel.
- You're facing an in-person audit or a field audit. Correspondence audits you can handle yourself. In-person audits benefit from professional representation.
- Your matter has escalated beyond a simple notice — you're facing a levy, a lien, or a court proceeding.
- You're unsure of your position. If you can't articulate why your corrected return is correct, if you're not confident in your response, or if the notice you've received is complex enough that you don't fully understand it, get help. It's better to pay for an hour of competent advice than to file a response you don't understand.

**The rule of thumb:** If the matter is procedural and you understand the process, handle it yourself. If the matter is legal, involves a formal proceeding, or involves amounts or consequences that could materially affect the trust, bring in help. Remember — as a trustee, seeking counsel for complex matters isn't optional. It's part of the job.

## How to Vet Counsel: Questions to Ask and Red Flags to Watch For

When you're looking for someone to help you with your trust structure, your filing position, or a challenge you're facing, vet them carefully. This space has no shortage of people who claim expertise they don't have, who charge for services they can't deliver, or who combine genuine knowledge with dangerous misinformation.

**Five questions to ask:**

1. **"Have you worked with private trust structures before?"** Not just trusts in general — irrevocable trusts, revocable trusts, and estate trusts are common. Private irrevocable ecclesiastical trusts operating outside the statutory framework are not. You need someone who has experience with this specific type of structure.

2. **"Have you helped clients file corrected returns or 4852s?"** The theory is one thing. The practice is another. Someone who has actually prepared and filed these documents knows what the IRS does with them, what responses typically look like, and what the process entails. Someone who has only read about it doesn't.

3. **"What is your approach when a client receives a CP2000 or Notice of Deficiency?"** The answer should involve reviewing the notice, preparing a documented response, and following the established process. If the answer involves sending "notices of understanding," filing "affidavits of truth," or telling you that you don't have to respond, find someone else.

4. **"Can you explain the difference between a statutory employee under §3401(c) and a common-law employee?"** If they can't articulate this difference — the central distinction in your position — they don't understand what you're doing well enough to help you.

5. **"What are the limitations of your practice?"** A good professional will tell you what they can't do as readily as what they can. Someone who claims they can handle everything — IRS notices, court representation, trust structuring, and your investment portfolio — is probably not excellent at any of them.

**Three red flags to watch for:**

- **"You don't have to pay any taxes" / "File this document and they'll leave you alone."** These are two sides of the same coin: the promise of a friction-free path. No legitimate professional will promise zero taxes forever or sell you a single magic filing. Your position is built on consistent filings, clean records, and documented responses over time. Anyone selling a shortcut is selling a fantasy.

- **"The IRS is a private corporation / the government is illegitimate / courts have no jurisdiction."** These are sovereign citizen talking points. They have no basis in law, they will not help you in any proceeding, and anyone who leads with them does not understand the legal system you're actually operating in.

- **"I can guarantee the outcome" / discouraging you from reading the law yourself.** No one can guarantee the result of a tax proceeding or court case. And a good professional wants you to understand your own position — they welcome questions, explain their reasoning, and show you the statutes and cases. Someone who says "it's complicated — let me handle it" and discourages you from reading the law is not someone you want representing your trust.

## First-Meeting Prep Checklist

When you find a professional, don't walk in empty-handed. Bring:

- **Trust binder** (or the relevant sections — declaration, minutes, trust instrument)
- **Your corrected returns** for the years in question
- **All IRS correspondence** — every notice, every letter — and your responses
- **Your correspondence log** with dates, methods, and confirmation numbers
- **A written summary** of your position and the specific issue you need help with

That last one matters most. A professional can read 20 pages of records, or they can read your one-page summary and then confirm it against the records. The summary gets you in the door faster and makes the first meeting productive instead of exploratory.

## What to Expect Cost-Wise

An initial consultation typically runs $150–$400. Ongoing representation depends on complexity. An enrolled agent handling a CP2000 response might charge $500–$2,000. Tax Court representation varies widely. Ask upfront about fees and billing structure. A good professional will discuss costs before you commit. If they won't tell you what it costs before you agree to pay, that's information too.

## A Win Story

A trustee received a Notice of Deficiency for $34,000. Through the WingPoint community, they connected with an enrolled agent who had filed over 40 corrected returns. When the trustee sat down across from the agent and opened their binder — three years of consistent filings, clean minutes, every response sent certified mail — the agent paused and said something like: "This makes my job a lot easier." The agent reviewed the records, prepared the Tax Court petition, and the matter was settled for a fraction of the proposed assessment. What could have been months of back-and-forth resolved in weeks, because the foundation was already laid. The key: the trustee's records made the professional's job easier — and made the resolution faster than it had any right to be.

That's the pattern. You do the work. You keep the records. You file consistently. When you need help, you walk in prepared — and the professional you hire can hit the ground running because you've already built the foundation.

## Your Quick Reference

- **Handle it yourself when:** Simple notices, routine correspondence, organized-records audits. You know your position better than anyone; low-complexity situations don't require paid help.
- **Get a tax professional when:** You need help preparing or reviewing a corrected return, or someone to communicate with the IRS on your behalf. Enrolled agents (IRS-licensed practitioners) specialize in this.
- **Get legal counsel when:** Tax Court proceedings, Notices of Deficiency, expanded audits, any proceeding where your filing position or trust structure is directly at issue.
- **Vetting questions:** "Have you represented clients in Tax Court?" "Are you familiar with corrected returns and Form 4852?" "Can you work with a client who has a specific statutory position?" If they can't answer yes to all three, keep looking.
- **Red flags:** Anyone who promises a specific outcome, charges a percentage of your refund, or tells you not to file. Walk away.

## Your Next Move

Search your state's bar association directory for tax attorneys who list IRS disputes or trust law as a focus. You don't need to call anyone — just know who's there.
