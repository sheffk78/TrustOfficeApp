# Lesson 5: When the World Pushes Back — Standing as a Trustee

**Course:** WP-DEFEND — Standing Strong: Defending Your Trust and Your Position  
**Lesson:** 5 of 10  

---

You've learned how to read IRS notices, how to respond, and what the escalation process looks like. But the IRS isn't the only institution you'll face — and for many people, it's not even the first one.

Before you ever receive a letter from the IRS, you're more likely to face pushback from the people and institutions you interact with every day: your employer, your bank, local government offices, and law enforcement — including at the border or the airport.

These encounters are smaller than an IRS audit. They don't come with official letterhead. But they can be more disorienting — because they're personal, they're immediate, and they don't follow a clear process the way an IRS notice does.

You've now seen the challenges you'll face — IRS notices, employer pushback, bank confusion, local government questions, law enforcement encounters, tickets, and court proceedings. Every single one asks the same thing: "show me." And in every one, the code's definitions are narrow, your filings are consistent, and the burden is on them to show your reading is wrong.

---

## A Win Before We Begin

A trustee in Colorado walked into a credit union — the first time they'd ever tried to open a trust account anywhere — with a Certification of Trust (a summary document showing the trust's name, the trustee's authority, and the trust's EIN — recognized by most states as sufficient for banking purposes) and an EIN letter. Their hands were steady on the folder, but the nerves were real. The teller had never seen a private irrevocable trust before and asked for a personal guarantor. The trustee calmly explained: "I'm the trustee acting in fiduciary capacity — meaning I'm administering the trust's assets on behalf of the trust, not in my personal capacity. The trust is the account holder. Here's the EIN letter confirming the trust is a recognized entity." The branch manager was called, reviewed the documents, and simply nodded. It took 20 minutes longer than a personal account — but it opened. The relief was specific and quiet: the documents had done exactly what they were supposed to do.

That's what this lesson is about. Presenting clean documents in your proper capacity and letting the paper do what paper does: establish a record that stands on its own.

Carry that win with you into every scenario that follows.

## The Spiritual Foundation: You Are Not Called to Be Passive

Before we get into the practical scenarios, there's something that needs to be said clearly — because the way most people understand "turn the other cheek" will work against you in this process.

The common reading of Matthew 5 is that Jesus told his followers to be passive — to accept insult, to give up what's demanded, to go along with whatever authority imposes. "Turn the other cheek" gets treated as a command to lie down. "Give him your cloak too" gets treated as a command to give more than what's taken. "Go with him two miles" gets treated as a command to volunteer for extra service.

That reading misses what was actually happening in first-century Roman-occupied Judea — and it misses what Jesus was teaching.

**Turning the other cheek.** In Roman law, a backhand slap to the right cheek was how a superior struck a subordinate — a master hit a slave, a Roman hit a Jew. But you could only backhand the right cheek. To strike the left cheek, you'd have to use your fist — and in Roman law, a fist strike was between equals. When Jesus said "turn to him the other also," he wasn't saying "take another beating." He was saying: *force the person hitting you to either treat you as an equal or acknowledge they're hitting a subordinate.* The person who turns the other cheek seizes the initiative and exposes the power dynamic: "If you're going to hit me, hit me as an equal. Or stop."

**Giving your cloak as well.** Under Jewish law, a creditor could take a debtor's outer garment as collateral, but had to return it by sunset — it was the person's only protection against the cold (Exodus 22:26-27). The inner garment (tunic) was not collateral. When Jesus says "let him have your cloak as well," he's describing someone who gives up both layers and stands naked in the streets. In that culture, the shame fell on the person who caused the nakedness, not the person who was naked. By giving up both garments, the debtor exposes the creditor's predation and forces the community to see what's happening. It's creative confrontation that reveals injustice.

**Going the second mile.** Roman soldiers could legally compel a civilian to carry their pack for one mile — but only one. Carrying it further was a violation of Roman military law, and the soldier could be punished. When Jesus says "go with him two miles," he's not saying "be extra helpful." He's saying: take the soldier past the legal limit and put him in violation of his own law. The civilian seizes the initiative — the soldier now has to beg for his pack back before he gets in trouble. It's not servitude. It's subversion through generosity.

**What this means for you.** Jesus was not teaching passivity. He was teaching his followers to stand in their authority — to seize the initiative, to expose unjust power dynamics, and to act in ways that revealed the truth without becoming the aggressor. This is exactly what you do when you file a 4852. You're turning the other cheek — standing in your position and saying: "If you want to assess tax on this income, you'll have to do it as a proceeding between two parties filing legitimate documents." You're giving your cloak — providing more documentation than they asked for, because the full record reveals the truth. You're going the second mile — responding with certified mail, on time, with citations, because the completeness of your response exposes the incompleteness of theirs.

You are a trustee acting in fiduciary capacity. You are called to operate in peace — not in fear, not in anger, and not in passivity. Peace is not the absence of conflict. Peace is the presence of authority exercised rightly. Christ did not teach his followers to shrink. He taught them to stand — and to stand in a way that exposed the truth without becoming the thing they opposed.

That is the posture you carry into every encounter in this lesson.

## When Your Employer Pushes Back

You've determined, based on your study of the statutes, that your compensation doesn't meet the definition of "wages" under §3401(a) because the trust directs your labor and you are not a statutory "employee" engaged in a "trade or business." You've submitted a W-4 claiming exempt status, or you've submitted a corrected W-4 reflecting your position.

And your employer says: "I can't accept this. Our payroll system requires me to withhold. Talk to HR."

Or: "I've never seen this before. I need to check with our legal department."

Or: "We're not going to change how we process your pay. If you have a problem, take it up with the IRS."

**What to do as a trustee:**

Your employer isn't your adversary — they're running a payroll system designed for statutory employees, following guidance from their CPA and legal counsel to withhold from all compensation. Their caution isn't hostility; it's just the system working as designed. The correction happens at the filing level, not the payroll level.

**1. Submit your documentation in writing.** If you haven't already, submit your W-4 or corrected W-4 in writing, with a signed statement explaining your position. Cite the specific statutes. Keep a copy. Send it certified mail or hand-deliver it with a signed receipt. This creates a record that you submitted the document — whether the employer acts on it or not.

**2. If they refuse, document the refusal.** If your employer declines to honor your W-4 position, send a written notice (certified mail) stating that you submitted the W-4, that the employer has declined to honor it, and that the withholding from your compensation is therefore imposed — not elected. This document matters because it creates a record that the withholding was not your election. You are not powerless here: the paper trail itself is your defense.

**3. File a Form 4852 at tax time.** If your employer issues a W-2 that mischaracterizes your compensation as wages, you file a Form 4852 — a substitute W-2 — reporting the correct amount. This is the process covered in Course 4. You don't argue with your employer about the W-2. You correct the record. This is where your position is strongest: at the filing level, your documents speak, and the burden shifts to the IRS to show why your reading is wrong.

**4. Don't make it personal.** Your employer's payroll system, their CPA, and their legal department are all telling them to withhold. They are not going to override that advice because you asked them to. That's not a failure on your part, and it's not a personal attack on theirs. The correction happens at the filing level, not the payroll level. Your job as trustee is to document your position, file correctly, and let the system process the discrepancy.

**What not to do:**

- Don't cite case law at your employer or lecture them on definitions — they're not the audience for your position. The IRS is.
- Don't refuse to show up for work. Your labor relationship and your tax position are separate.
- Don't sign anything under protest or add "without prejudice" — these have no legal effect and undermine your position.

**Why your position is strong:** You don't need your employer to agree with you. The real correction happens at filing time, on your terms. Their refusal to honor your W-4 is itself a record — and that record supports you.

## When a Bank Pushes Back

You walk into a bank to open a trust account. You have your Certification of Trust, your EIN letter, and your identification. The teller looks at your documents and says: "I've never seen a trust like this." Or: "We need the Social Security number of the trust owner." Or: "Our system requires a personal guarantor." Or: "We can't open this type of account."

**What to understand:**

Bank employees are trained on a narrow set of account types: personal accounts, business accounts, and maybe a statutory trust account or two. A private irrevocable ecclesiastical trust is not something they encounter regularly. They're confused, not rejecting you. And when bank employees are confused, their default is to follow the script and ask for more documentation.

**What to do as a trustee:**

**1. Come prepared.** Bring your Certification of Trust, your IRS EIN assignment letter, and your identification. The Certification of Trust is a summary document that most states recognize as sufficient for banking purposes — it shows the trust's name, the trustee's authority, and the trust's EIN without disclosing the full Declaration.

**2. If they ask for an SSN, clarify the trust's status.** The trust has an EIN, not an SSN. The trust is a separate entity. The bank should be using the trust's EIN for the account. If the employee is confused, ask to speak with someone who handles business or trust accounts — they are more likely to understand the distinction.

**3. If they ask for a personal guarantor, clarify your capacity.** You are opening this account as a trustee, acting in fiduciary capacity on behalf of the trust. You are not personally guaranteeing the account. If the bank requires a personal guarantor for all accounts, find a different bank. There are banks that will open trust accounts without a personal guarantee.

**4. If one branch won't do it, try another.** Bank policies vary. Branch managers vary. Some banks and credit unions are more trust-friendly than others. Small local banks and credit unions are often more willing to work with trust documents than large national banks. This is not a battle you need to win at the first institution you walk into.

**What not to do (unique to this scenario):**

- Don't over-explain. The bank employee does not need to understand your trust's ecclesiastical status or the statutory definition of "employee." They need to see a trust document, an EIN, and identification. Provide what's necessary. Stop when the account is open.

**Why your position is strong:** The IRS already recognized your trust by issuing an EIN. That letter is a federal document confirming your trust is a recognized entity. You're not asking the bank to make a legal determination — you're asking them to open an account for an entity the federal government has already acknowledged. That's a straightforward request, and eventually you'll find someone who sees it that way.

## When Law Enforcement or Border Officials Question Your Documents

This is the scenario that creates the most anxiety — and it's the one where the distinction between passive compliance and standing in your authority matters most.

A traffic stop, a roadside check, a border crossing, a TSA checkpoint, or any encounter with law enforcement where the officer asks questions about your identity, your documents, or your vehicle that go beyond the scope of the stop.

**What to understand:**

Law enforcement officers are trained to identify inconsistencies. If your documents don't match what they expect — if your vehicle is registered to a trust instead of a person, if your license address doesn't match your registration, if you present a trust document instead of a standard registration — they will ask questions. Those questions are not accusations. They are the officer doing their job.

The same applies at airports and border crossings. TSA is checking identification, not tax status. Border agents have broader authority to ask questions but are still working from a set of procedures. Your documents are unusual in their experience — that's all.

**What not to do (unique to this scenario):**

- Don't escalate a routine stop into a constitutional debate. The officer is not evaluating your legal position. Save it for court.
- Don't refuse to identify yourself or present documents. This is not the venue for testing those arguments.

**What to do as a trustee:**

**1. Answer the questions you're asked.** If an officer asks "Is this vehicle registered to you?" the correct answer is "It's registered to my trust, and I'm the trustee." You don't need to give a lecture. You don't need to cite statutes. You answer the question, present the documentation, and remain calm.

**2. Have your documents organized.** If you're driving a vehicle registered to a trust, keep the registration, insurance, and trust documentation (or a Certification of Trust) in the vehicle. When an officer asks for registration and insurance, hand them the documents. If the name on the registration is a trust name, say "I'm the trustee." That's it.

**3. For travel encounters, keep it brief.** If your trust documents are in your carry-on and questioned at TSA, explain: "These are trust documents. I'm a trustee." If border agents ask questions, answer honestly and briefly. If you're carrying significant cash, declare it — the reporting rules apply regardless of your trust structure. Present documents when asked. Explain your capacity. Be prepared, be patient, and be brief.

**4. Don't argue on the side of the road or at the checkpoint.** A traffic stop is not a courtroom. A border checkpoint is not a legal proceeding. The officer is not a judge. This is not the time or place to explain your position on statutory definitions or trust law. Answer the questions, present the documents, and cooperate with the scope of the stop.

**5. If the encounter escalates, remain calm and seek counsel.** If you are detained, if your vehicle is searched, if you are arrested, do not resist, do not argue, and do not volunteer information beyond what is asked. Say: "I'd like to speak with counsel before answering further questions." This is your right. Use it.

**6. Know the difference between a stop and a proceeding.** A traffic stop is about identification, registration, and the reason for the stop. A court appearance — for a ticket, a citation, or a summons — is a legal proceeding where your position can be presented with documentation. Don't confuse the two. The roadside is for compliance. The courtroom is for your position.

**A note on advanced techniques.** You may encounter people in this space who teach that you can travel without a driver's license, that you don't need vehicle registration, or that you can refuse to identify yourself to law enforcement. This course does not cover those techniques. We teach you how to operate as a trustee within the existing system — presenting your documents, answering questions, and standing in your authority without turning a routine encounter into a confrontation.

**Why your position is strong:** Your documents are real, your trust is recognized, and your answers are simple. "I'm the trustee" is a fact, not a theory. The more calmly and clearly you state it, the less reason the officer has to extend the encounter. Most stops where the driver is calm, organized, and cooperative end the same way — with the officer handing your documents back and moving on.

## When a Local Government Agency Pushes Back

You're dealing with your county assessor, your city clerk, your DMV, or another local office, and they're asking questions about your trust that they've never been asked before. They may say: "We can't accept a trust as the owner." Or: "The trust needs a registered agent." Or: "This document doesn't match our standard forms."

**What to understand:** Local government employees follow procedures all day — your documents don't fit those procedures, but that's a procedural mismatch, not a legal judgment against you.

**What to do as a trustee:**

**1. Be patient.** These encounters take longer than you want. Your trust is unusual in their experience. Give them time.

**2. Provide what they ask for.** If they need a specific document, provide it. Fill out their forms in fiduciary capacity. You're operating within their process, using your trust's documentation.

**3. If they say they can't accept a document, ask what they need instead.** "I understand this is unusual. What documentation would you need in order to process this?" You're finding a path through their process — and the path almost always exists.

**4. If they absolutely refuse, come back with more documentation or find a different path.** Sometimes the DMV will accept a specific form. Sometimes you need to talk to a supervisor. Sometimes you come back another day and get a different clerk. Practical problems, practical solutions.

**What not to do (unique to this scenario):**

- Don't argue that the clerk is wrong about what they need. Ask what they need instead — you're solving a procedural problem, not winning a debate.

**Why your position is strong:** Local agencies process trusts every day; your trust is less common but still a recognized structure, and there is almost always a procedural lane for it.

## The Pattern Across All Encounters

Whether you're dealing with an employer, a bank, a local office, a police officer, or a border agent, the pattern is the same:

1. **Their confusion is not your crisis — and it's not an attack.** Every person you encounter is working from procedures that weren't designed for your situation. Treat procedural problems as procedural problems, not legal judgments.

2. **You present, you don't argue.** Your documents and filings speak. The appropriate venue for your position is a formal proceeding — not a hallway, not a roadside, not a teller's window.

3. **Your trustee identity changes the conversation.** When you walk in as a trustee acting in fiduciary capacity — someone whose code definitions are narrow, whose filings are consistent, and who knows the burden is on the other side — you are not a person making a personal request. "I'm the trustee" is a complete answer to most questions about why your documents look different.

## Your Quick Reference

- **Employer pushback:** Submit W-4 in writing (certified mail). If refused, document the refusal. File 4852 at tax time. Don't argue — correct the record.
- **Bank pushback:** Bring Certification of Trust + EIN letter. Ask for someone who handles trust accounts. Try another branch if needed.
- **Law enforcement:** Answer questions asked. Present documents. Don't argue jurisdiction. Say "I'm the trustee." If escalating: "I'd like to speak with counsel."
- **Local government:** Provide what they ask for. Ask what document format they need. Be patient — procedural mismatch, not legal judgment.
- **In every scenario:** Present, don't argue. The burden is on them.

## Your Next Move

Gather your trust documents — Certification of Trust, EIN letter, and any correspondence — into a single binder or folder. You'll need them organized for the scenarios ahead.
