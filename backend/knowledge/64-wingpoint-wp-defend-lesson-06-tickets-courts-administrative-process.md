# Lesson 6: Tickets, Courts, and the Administrative Process

**Course:** WP-DEFEND — Standing Strong: Defending Your Trust and Your Position  
**Lesson:** 6 of 10  

---

At some point, you'll receive a traffic ticket, a code violation, a citation, or a summons. These encounters feel heavier than a bank teller's confusion — they carry the weight of a government proceeding.

When you appear in court or at a hearing as a trustee, you are acting in fiduciary capacity — meaning you represent the trust, not yourself. The trust is the party. You are its authorized representative. This distinction matters because it changes what documents you present, what capacity you sign in, and how you identify yourself.

---

## You Are a Trustee, Not the Party

This is the most important distinction in this lesson: when you appear in court or at a hearing as a trustee, you are acting in a **fiduciary capacity on behalf of a separate legal entity**. You are not appearing as yourself. The trust is the party. You are the authorized representative.

A trust is recognized as a separate legal entity under common law and under the IRS's own classification system — it has its own EIN, files its own tax returns, and holds property in its own name. When you step into a hearing room, you are stepping in as the person authorized to speak and act for that entity.

This changes what you say, how you identify yourself, and what documents you present.

**As an individual:** "I'm John Smith. I'm here about the ticket."

**As a trustee:** "I'm John Smith, trustee of [Trust Name]. I'm appearing in my fiduciary capacity on behalf of the trust, which is the registered owner of this vehicle."

These are fundamentally different statements. The first makes you the party. The second makes you the representative of the party. The first exposes your individual position. The second anchors the trust's position — and the trust's liability is limited to trust assets.

When you identify yourself as a trustee acting in fiduciary capacity:
- You present the trust's documents, not your personal documents
- You speak on behalf of the trust entity
- The hearing officer deals with the trust as the involved party
- Your personal position remains separate

Trustees handle these matters all the time. The system has a process for this. You are not the first.

---

## Most Tickets Are About the Ticket

A speeding ticket is about speeding. A parking citation is about parking. A code violation is about the code that was violated. These are not attacks on your trust structure or your filing position. They are administrative matters that apply to everyone — including you.

Handle the matter at hand. Pay the ticket, contest it through the normal process, or correct the violation. Don't turn a speeding ticket into a status confrontation.

**When your trust structure becomes relevant:** when the question is about ownership, capacity, or jurisdiction — not when the question is about whether you were speeding.

The situations where trust-related questions are most likely to arise:

- **Vehicle registration and insurance.** Your vehicle is registered to a trust. A traffic stop, accident, or citation means the registration and insurance are in the trust's name — and that prompts questions.
- **Property ownership disputes.** Trust-held real property, questions about title, ownership, or liability.
- **Business licensing and permitting.** The trust operates a business; you're the trustee presenting on its behalf.
- **Court appearances related to your filing position.** If your corrected return or 4852 leads to a Tax Court proceeding. (Finding counsel for these matters is covered in Lesson 8; relevant case law in Lesson 9.)

## The Hearing: A Walkthrough

You've received a citation. You've decided to contest the portion that involves the trust — say, a registration question on a trust-owned vehicle. You've filed your response by the deadline. Now you're going to the hearing.

**Before you go:** Gather your documents. Registration in the trust's name, insurance card listing the trust as insured, Certification of Trust. Put them in a folder. Bring the citation itself.

**Walking in:** You arrive at the municipal building or courthouse listed on your notice. Park. Walk in. There's usually a security checkpoint — go through it like anyone else. Find the courtroom or hearing room. There may be a calendar posted outside the door with the day's cases listed. Find yours. Take a seat in the gallery.

**Who's in the room:** A hearing officer or judge sits at the bench. A clerk is nearby, managing the docket. A prosecutor or city attorney may be present. Other people with matters that day sit in the gallery. It looks like a small, efficient office — not a dramatic courtroom scene.

**When your name is called:** Walk to the front. The hearing officer will ask you to identify yourself. This is where the trustee distinction matters:

"I'm [Name], trustee of [Trust Name]. I'm appearing in my fiduciary capacity on behalf of the trust, which is the registered owner of this vehicle."

**What the hearing officer asks:** The officer will ask about the specific citation — the registration, the violation, the circumstances. Answer the specific questions. Present the specific documents when asked.

"Your registration is expired?"  
"It was current at the time of the citation. Here's the current registration and the Certification of Trust."

The officer reviews the documents. They confirm the registration is current. They note the trust ownership. They move on.

**How long it takes:** Five to ten minutes. Most hearings are short. The officer is working through a docket. They want the relevant facts and the relevant documents. They are not there to interrogate your trust structure.

**What "resolution" means:** The officer makes a determination on the citation. Dismissed, reduced, or upheld. If dismissed, you walk out. If upheld, you pay the fine or you appeal. That's it.

**If you disagree with the outcome:** You can appeal. One adverse ruling is not the end of your position — it's a data point. Learn from it, adjust, and continue operating from the correct standing with better documentation.

The administrative process is a process — it has steps, deadlines, and forums. Follow the steps. Meet the deadlines. Present your position in the appropriate forum. That forum is the hearing, not the roadside, not the clerk's window, not a phone call.

## A Trustee Walked In and Walked Out

A trustee in Texas received a citation for expired registration and speeding on a trust-owned vehicle. Both charges were on one ticket.

The hearing room was small, lit by fluorescent panels, with a dozen other people sitting on hard benches waiting for their cases to be called. When the trustee's name came up, they walked to the front, presented the current registration, current insurance, and Certification of Trust. When the hearing officer asked about ownership, the trustee said: "I'm the trustee of [Trust Name]. The vehicle is registered to the trust. I have the current documents."

The officer reviewed them, confirmed the registration was now current, and dismissed the registration charge. The whole thing took maybe five minutes. The speeding portion was paid. The trust structure was never questioned — it was simply documented and accepted. The trustee walked out with the same calm they walked in with.

This is how it goes. Trustees handle these matters routinely. The hearing officer isn't looking for a fight. They're looking for documents that resolve the question.

## Handling a Ticket When Your Trust Is Involved

You're driving a vehicle registered to your trust. You receive a traffic ticket.

**If it's a straightforward traffic violation and you were at fault:** pay the ticket or attend traffic school. This is a traffic matter, not a trust matter. Handle it like anyone else would.

**If the citation involves your trust structure** — registration issues, questions about who owns the vehicle, insurance in the trust's name — then the trust documentation becomes relevant. Present your trust registration, insurance, and Certification of Trust at the hearing.

**If you choose to contest:** most jurisdictions allow you to contest by written declaration first. This is often easier because you can lay out your position clearly and completely without the pressure of a live hearing. If you lose the written declaration, you can typically request a court hearing.

**At a hearing involving a trust-registered vehicle:** bring the registration, insurance, and Certification of Trust. Present yourself as the trustee acting in fiduciary capacity. Answer the specific questions the hearing officer asks. Nothing more.

---

## What Not to Do

**Do not ignore tickets, citations, or summons.** Unpaid tickets become warrants. Missed court dates become failures to appear. These create real legal problems that are harder to resolve than the original matter.

**Do not use sovereign citizen tactics.** No "notices of understanding," no "affidavits of truth," no "fee schedules." These have no legal effect, they antagonize the court, and they undermine your credibility when you need it most. Your position is supported by the actual code. You don't need pseudo-legal documents. You need real ones.

**Do not argue jurisdiction in traffic court.** If you were speeding, you were speeding. Arguing that the court has no jurisdiction over you because you're a trustee operating in private capacity ensures the judge sees you as someone who doesn't understand the proceeding they're in.

That's the list. Three things. Don't ignore. Don't use pseudo-legal filings. Don't argue jurisdiction where it doesn't apply. Everything else flows from the trustee posture: calm, documented, lawful, brief.

## When Family Members Are Involved

Your trust owns vehicles. Your beneficiaries drive them. Your spouse runs errands in a car registered to the trust. Your teenager gets pulled over on the way home. These aren't hypothetical — they're daily life for a trust-operating family.

### Your Spouse Driving a Trust Vehicle

Your spouse is a beneficiary of the trust. They're driving to the grocery store in a car the trust owns. They get pulled over for a broken taillight. The officer runs the plates and the registration comes back to a trust, not a person.

**What your spouse should say:**

"This vehicle belongs to [Trust Name]. I'm a beneficiary of the trust. The registration and insurance are in the trust's name. I have the documents in the glovebox."

That's it. The officer asked who owns the car. The trust owns the car. Your spouse is a beneficiary who has permission to drive it.

**What to have in the vehicle:** Registration in the trust's name. Insurance card listing the trust as the insured and naming authorized drivers. A copy of the Certification of Trust (not the full Declaration — the Certification is a summary document that most states accept for this purpose). Keep these together in the glovebox or center console.

### Your Teenage Driver and a Trust Vehicle

Your 17-year-old is driving a trust-owned car. They get pulled over. They're already nervous — they're 17, they just got their license, and they're dealing with a police officer.

Teach your teenager this script before they ever drive the car alone:

1. **Hand over your driver's license.** You are a licensed driver operating a vehicle. That's what matters at a traffic stop.
2. **When the officer asks about the registration, say:** "This car belongs to [Trust Name]. My [parent/sibling/etc.] is the trustee. The registration and insurance are in the trust's name. They're in the glovebox."
3. **Answer what's asked. Nothing more.** The officer doesn't need to know what kind of trust it is, why it exists, or who the beneficiaries are. They need to know that the car is registered, insured, and that the person driving it has permission.
4. **If the officer presses about ownership or the trust, your teenager should say:** "I'd need to have the trustee contact you. I don't have the full details, but the registration and insurance are current and in the car."

That last response is powerful in its simplicity. A teenager saying "I'd need to have the trustee contact you" is neither evasive nor confrontational. It's accurate. And it shuts down further questioning about the trust's structure because the teenager genuinely doesn't have more details — and shouldn't be expected to.

### If a Family Member Receives a Citation in a Trust Vehicle

Handle it the same way you'd handle any citation — but present the trust documentation at the hearing.

The citation is issued to the driver for the violation (speeding, broken taillight, expired registration check). The trust's involvement is limited to the vehicle's ownership and registration. These are separate matters:

- **The traffic violation** is the driver's responsibility. If your teenager was speeding, the ticket is for speeding. Pay it or contest it on the merits.
- **The registration question** is the trust's matter. If the citation includes an issue with the registration, that's where your Certification of Trust and trust registration documents come in.

At a hearing: "I'm [Name], trustee of [Trust Name]. The vehicle is registered to the trust. I'm here in my fiduciary capacity on behalf of the trust. Here is the current registration, insurance, and Certification of Trust." Answer the specific questions asked. Present the specific documents requested. Stop.

### Preparing Your Family

Prepare your family members before they ever encounter a situation. Not with a legal briefing — with simple, practical guidance:

- **Everyone who drives a trust vehicle** should know the trust's name, should know to say "this vehicle belongs to [Trust Name]," and should know where the registration, insurance, and Certification of Trust are kept in the vehicle.
- **Everyone who drives a trust vehicle** should know not to volunteer information about the trust's structure, its ecclesiastical status, or the difference between private capacity and public capacity. The roadside is not the forum.
- **Everyone who drives a trust vehicle** should know that if an officer presses beyond "who owns the car," the response is: "I'd need to have the trustee contact you."

These are not complex instructions. They're the same kind of practical guidance you'd give anyone about how to handle a traffic stop — with the addition of "the car belongs to a trust, here are the documents."

## If a Matter Escalates

If a matter escalates to a formal proceeding where your filing position is at issue, present your position with documentation, consider counsel, and follow the court's rules. Don't improvise. Don't file documents you found on the internet. Don't cite cases you haven't read. If you don't know the rules, find someone who does. Lesson 8 covers how to find the right counsel.

For advanced techniques — operating without a driver's license, refusing government contracts, opting out of specific administrative systems — see Lesson 5's discussion. Those carry higher risk and are not covered in this course.

## Your Quick Reference

- **CP notices:** Identify the type (CP2000 = underreported income, CP504 = balance due, CP521 = installment plan). Read the notice. Respond by the deadline.
- **Tax Court:** You don't need to go to trial to get a result. Most Tax Court cases settle. The petition itself shifts the burden to the IRS.
- **Collection Due Process:** Your right to a hearing before the IRS levies or liens. Request it within 30 days. It buys time and forces the IRS to show its hand.
- **Administrative record:** Every response, every notice, every phone call — logged, dated, certified mail. The paper trail is your position made tangible.
- **Fiduciary capacity:** You represent the trust, not yourself. Sign as "John Smith, Trustee" — never just "John Smith."

## Your Next Move

Pull your most recent IRS notice (or find one online) and identify which type it is using the categories above. Practice reading it as a trustee — what's it asking, what's the deadline, what's your response path?
