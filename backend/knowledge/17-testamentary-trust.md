# Testamentary Trust

## What It Is

A testamentary trust is a trust created through a person's will and funded at death. Unlike a living trust, which takes effect during the grantor's lifetime, a testamentary trust only comes into existence when the will is probated and the assets are distributed into the trust. Because it is established by will, it must go through the probate process and typically operates under continuing court supervision, depending on state law.

## Key Governance Requirements

- **Created by will and activated at death.** The trust's terms are written into the grantor's last will and testament. The will must be admitted to probate before the trust can be established and funded.
- **Court supervision.** In most jurisdictions, testamentary trusts remain under the jurisdiction of the probate court. The trustee may need to file annual accountings with the court and seek court approval for certain actions, such as selling trust property or modifying distribution plans.
- **Funded through probate.** Assets pass through the probate estate before reaching the trust. This means probate fees, delays, and public record apply. Assets held outside probate (joint accounts, life insurance with named beneficiaries, retirement accounts) do not automatically fund the testamentary trust unless the beneficiary designation directs them there.
- **Trustee appointment.** The will names the trustee. If the named trustee cannot or will not serve, the probate court appoints a successor, which can introduce delay and unintended outcomes.
- **Ongoing fiduciary duties.** The trustee owes beneficiaries the duties of loyalty, prudence, impartiality, and accounting, the same as any trustee, but with the added layer of court reporting.
- **State-specific probate rules.** The trust is governed by the laws of the state where the will is probated, not necessarily where the trustee or beneficiaries live.

## Distribution Rules

- **Distributions follow the will's terms.** The will specifies how and when trust assets are distributed to beneficiaries. The trustee has no authority to deviate from those written instructions.
- **Court approval may be required.** For discretionary distributions, early distributions, or distributions that change the proportions among beneficiaries, the trustee may need to petition the probate court.
- **Mandatory versus discretionary.** Some wills mandate specific distributions (for example, "pay my daughter $2,000 per month until age 25") while others leave discretion to the trustee. The trustee must understand which is which.
- **Final distribution and termination.** When the trust's purpose is fulfilled or the specified termination date arrives, the trustee accounts to the court, distributes remaining assets, and the trust is closed.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Assuming the trust avoids probate.** It does not. A testamentary trust is created by a will, so probate is required. Trustees sometimes confuse this with a revocable living trust, which can avoid probate.
- **Missing court accounting deadlines.** Many jurisdictions require annual accountings to the probate court. Failing to file can result in surcharge, removal, or personal liability for the trustee.
- **Funding gaps.** Assets with beneficiary designations (life insurance, retirement accounts) do not automatically pass into the testamentary trust unless the designation names the trust. A common error is assuming everything flows into the trust automatically.
- **Acting without court approval.** Trustees accustomed to living trusts may make decisions that require probate court sign-off in a testamentary trust context, exposing themselves to personal liability.
