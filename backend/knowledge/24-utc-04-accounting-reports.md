# UTC Doctrine 04 — Accounting, Records, and Reporting to Beneficiaries

> ⚠️ **Scope note — records show your reasoning; they do not erase exposure.**
> The Code's remedies section lets courts order a trustee to account, compel performance, remove the trustee, reduce compensation, and require the trustee to pay money. Records *demonstrate* your decisions and make them *easier to produce*. They do not eliminate exposure, and nothing here says they do.

**UTC anchor:** art. 8 — ss. 804, 809, 810–816; art. 10 — ss. 1001–1007, 1009, 1012, 1013; art. 1 — s. 103

## Why this matters

A quarterly review, a minutes framework, a defensibility score, and a beneficiary communication template set all rest on the Code's accounting and records requirements. This section sets out the statutory backbone, including requirements that are easy to miss.

## Records: what the Code actually demands

Section 810 has three operative subsections and a fourth that carves out an exception.

**810(a):** a trustee shall keep adequate records of the administration of the trust.

**810(b):** a trustee shall keep trust property separate from the trustee's own property.

**810(c):** except as otherwise provided in (d), a trustee shall cause the trust property to be designated so that the trust's interest, to the extent feasible, appears in records maintained by a party other than a trustee or beneficiary.

**810(d):** if the trustee maintains records clearly indicating the respective interests, the property of two or more separate trusts may be invested as a whole.

The practical standard in (c) is the one most solo trustees have never been told: the trust's interest has to show up in *someone else's* records. A trust ledger in the trustee's own spreadsheet, a property list in a notebook, a deed in a personal file — none of these satisfy (c). The bank account, the brokerage position, the recorded deed.

Subsection (b) is absolute: the duty *not* to mingle trust assets with the trustee's own. The Trustee-101 separation checklist teaches six separation rules, and this is the statutory anchor for the one that matters most.

## Informing and reporting

Section 813 most directly reshapes what a trustee's beneficiary-communication workflow should look like.

**813(a) — the standing duty.** A trustee shall keep the qualified beneficiaries reasonably informed about the administration of the trust and of the material facts necessary for them to protect their interests. Unless unreasonable, a trustee shall promptly respond to a beneficiary's request for information about the administration.

**813(b)(1) — the instrument.** On a beneficiary's request, a trustee shall promptly furnish a copy of the trust instrument.

**813(b)(2) — acceptance notice.** Within 60 days after accepting a trusteeship, a trustee shall notify the qualified beneficiaries of the acceptance and of the trustee's name, address, and telephone number. The Trustee-101 template set has an "Appointment Notification Letter" tagged "Sent within 60 days of becoming trustee."

**813(b)(3) — existence notice.** Within 60 days after the trustee learns of the creation of an irrevocable trust, or of a formerly revocable trust becoming irrevocable, the trustee shall notify the qualified beneficiaries of the trust's existence, the settlor(s)' identity, the right to request the instrument, and the right to a report.

**813(b)(4) — compensation change notice.** The trustee shall notify the qualified beneficiaries in advance of any change in the method or rate of the trustee's compensation. Advance, not after.

**813(c) — the report.** At least annually and at termination, a trustee shall send the distributees or permissible distributees of trust income or principal — and any other beneficiary who requests it — a report of the trust property, liabilities, receipts, and disbursements, the source and amount of the trustee's compensation, and a listing of the assets and, if feasible, their market values. On a vacancy, unless a cotrustee remains, the former trustee sends it.

**813(d) — waiver.** A beneficiary may waive the right to a report and withdraw the waiver as to future reports.

## The reporting clock and the litigation clock are different

This is the piece most product workflows miss.



Section 1005 sets the limitation period for a beneficiary's breach-of-trust claim. Subsection (a): a beneficiary may not commence a proceeding more than **one year** after being sent a report that adequately disclosed a potential claim and informed them of the time allowed. Subsection (b) says a report qualifies if it gives enough information that the beneficiary knows of the claim or should have inquired into it. Subsection (c) is the fallback: **five years** from the first of the trustee's removal, resignation, or death, the termination of the beneficiary's interest, or the termination of the trust.

A report that discloses a potential claim starts a one-year clock and must tell the beneficiary how much time they have. A report silent on a known problem does not start that clock, leaving the five-year outer limit. The model text carries bracketed state choices. **Confirm the limitation period in your state with an attorney.**

"Accounting sent within 60 days of fiscal year-end" and "All beneficiary questions answered within 10 business days" are service standards — good practice, but not the Code's numbers, except where the Code says "promptly" and "within 60 days" in 813(b).

## Money damages, and the duties that produce them

Section 1001 lists what a court may do about a breach of trust: compel performance, enjoin a breach, compel redress by money or restoration, order the trustee to account, appoint a special fiduciary to take possession of the trust property, suspend or remove the trustee, reduce or deny compensation, and — subject to s. 1012 — void a trustee's act, impose a lien or constructive trust, or trace trust property wrongfully disposed.

Section 1002 sets the measure: a breaching trustee is liable for the greater of the amount required to restore the trust property and distributions to what they would have been, or the profit the trustee made. Between co-trustees there is contribution, except where a trustee was substantially more at fault, the breach was in bad faith or with reckless indifference, or the trustee received a benefit from the breach.

Section 1003 is favorable and often misunderstood: a trustee is accountable for any profit arising from administration, even absent a breach; but absent a breach, a trustee is not liable for a loss or depreciation in trust property, or for not having made a profit. Trustees are not insurers of market losses.

## Defenses that come from good records

Five sections give a trustee a defense that depends on what the records show.

**1006 — reliance on the instrument.** A trustee who acts in reasonable reliance on the trust instrument's terms is not liable for a beneficiary breach-of-trust claim to the extent the breach resulted from the reliance. "I did what the document said" is a legal argument, not an excuse.

**1007 — the event.** If the happening of an event — marriage, divorce, educational requirements, death — affects the administration or distribution of a trust, a trustee who used reasonable care to ascertain it is not liable for a loss from not knowing it. The duty is to ascertain, not to be omniscient.

**1009 — beneficiary consent, release, or ratification.** A trustee is not liable if the beneficiary consented to the breach, released the trustee, or ratified the transaction, unless it was induced by improper conduct, or the beneficiary did not know their rights or the material facts.

**1012 — protection of persons dealing with the trustee.** A person other than a beneficiary who in good faith assists a trustee, or in good faith and for value deals with one, without knowledge that the trustee is improperly exercising the trustee's powers, is protected and need not inquire into them.

**1013 — certification of trust.** A trustee may furnish a certification of trust instead of the full instrument to a person other than a beneficiary, containing the trust's existence and execution date, the settlor's identity, the acting trustee's identity and address, the trustee's powers, the trust's revocability and who holds a power to revoke, the authority of cotrustees to sign, and the trust's taxpayer identification number. It must state that the trust has not been revoked, modified, or amended so as to make its representations incorrect.

## The quarterly review, in statutory terms

A quarterly review covers balances, transactions, investment performance, distributions, beneficiary status, tax and compliance, Schedule A, and documentation in minutes. Against the Code: s. 810(a) on records, s. 809 on control of trust property, s. 813(a) on staying informed, s. 804 on prudence, and s. 813(c) on the annual report.

### What this means for a trustee

- Audit your asset records against s. 810(c): for each asset, is the trust's interest visible in a third party's records — bank, brokerage, county recorder, vehicle title? Fix the ones only in your own files.

- Treat s. 813(b) as four separate, dated obligations — acceptance, existence, instrument on request, compensation change — and log the date each was satisfied.

- Include in every annual report: trust property, liabilities, receipts, disbursements, the source and amount of your compensation, a listing of the trust assets, and market values if feasible. A report that omits compensation misses a required element.

- Send a final report at termination. Section 813(c) requires it.

- If you know of a potential claim, decide deliberately whether to disclose it in the report. A report that discloses it starts a one-year clock under s. 1005 and must state the time allowed; one that omits it leaves the longer fallback running.

- Keep your reasoning contemporaneous. Sections 1006 and 1007 are only available to a trustee whose records show what was relied on and what was ascertained.

- Use the certification of trust for third parties instead of handing over the instrument. It is faster, protects the dispositive terms, and s. 1013 tells you what to include.

### Common question we get

**"Do I have to send a report to a beneficiary who has never received a distribution?"**
Usually yes, and it depends on which kind of beneficiary they are. Section 813(c) sends the report to distributees or permissible distributees of trust income or principal — current and remainder beneficiaries — and to others who request it. Section 813(b)(3) also requires an existence notice to qualified beneficiaries. Identify your qualified beneficiaries under s. 103 and keep a dated record of who got what.

**"If I have good records, can I still be personally liable for a loss?"**
Yes, and this is the point to get right. Good records establish what you did and why; they are not a shield. Section 1001 lists the remedies a court may impose, and s. 1002 measures damages against the loss caused or the profit made. What records do is answer the questions a court would ask: what did the terms require, what did you consider, when did you decide, what did you rely on. Section 1006 makes reliance on the instrument a defense, and s. 1007 protects a trustee who used reasonable care to ascertain a triggering event — a defensible position, not immunity.

**"Do I have to tell beneficiaries my own compensation?"**
Yes. Section 813(c) requires the report to include the source and amount of the trustee's compensation, and s. 813(b)(4) requires advance notice of any change in the method or rate. Section 708 governs what compensation is reasonable: if the trust is silent, the trustee is entitled to reasonable compensation; if the trust specifies it, the trustee is entitled to it as specified, though a court may adjust it if the duties are now substantially different from those contemplated at creation, or the amount is unreasonably low or high.
