# Trust Compliance: Washington

## State Income Tax on Trusts

Washington State does not impose a state income tax on individuals or trusts. This is a significant advantage for trusts domiciled or administered in Washington, as trust income — including investment income, capital gains, dividends, and accumulated earnings — is not subject to state-level income taxation. Trustees of Washington trusts are not required to file a state fiduciary income tax return for income tax purposes.

However, Washington does impose an estate tax, which is separate from federal estate tax and applies to estates and certain trust distributions above a specific threshold. The Washington estate tax exemption is set at approximately $2.193 million (as of 2024, adjusted annually), which is significantly lower than the federal estate tax exemption. This means that estates and trusts that are not subject to federal estate tax may still face Washington estate tax. The Washington estate tax rates are progressive, ranging from 10% to 20% on amounts above the exemption.

Trustees should be aware that while ongoing trust income is not taxed by Washington, distributions from trusts or transfers at death that exceed the Washington estate tax threshold can trigger significant state-level tax liability. This requires careful planning, especially for larger trusts and estates.

## Filing Requirements

Washington does not require a state fiduciary income tax return because there is no state income tax. Trustees file IRS Form 1041 for federal income tax purposes as usual. However, for estate tax purposes, Washington requires the filing of the Washington Estate Tax Return (Form 64) if the decedent's estate exceeds the Washington estate tax exemption threshold.

Form 64 is due within nine months of the date of death, though an extension of time to file can be requested. Payment of any estate tax owed is due by the original nine-month deadline, even if an extension to file is granted. Trustees and executors managing trusts that distribute assets upon a decedent's death should coordinate with Washington tax counsel to ensure Form 64 is filed correctly and timely.

For ongoing trust administration, no state-level income tax filings are required. Trustees should confirm whether any business entities held within the trust have Washington state obligations, such as the Washington Business and Occupation (B&O) tax, which applies to certain business activities.

## Beneficiary Notice Requirements

Washington's trust code, which was significantly modernized in 2011 and subsequent years, requires trustees to keep beneficiaries reasonably informed about the trust and its administration. Under the Washington Uniform Trust Code, codified in Chapter 11.98 of the Revised Code of Washington, the trustee must provide beneficiaries with information about the trust, including a copy of the trust instrument upon request and regular accountings.

The trustee must notify qualified beneficiaries of the trust's existence, the identity of the trustee, and the beneficiary's right to request a copy of the trust instrument and an accounting. This notice should be given within a reasonable time after the trustee accepts the trusteeship or the trust becomes irrevocable. Washington's trust law modernization brought the state closer to the Uniform Trust Code, providing clearer rules on trustee duties and beneficiary rights while maintaining flexibility for trust design.

## Court Supervision

Washington trusts are generally administered without court supervision. The Washington Uniform Trust Code does not require trusts to be registered with the court to be valid or to operate. Trustees can administer trusts independently of court oversight, which is the standard practice. Court involvement is typically limited to disputes, requests for declaratory relief, or when a trustee or beneficiary petitions the court for guidance.

Washington's trust law modernization clarified the rules for unsupervised administration and provided additional tools for trustees to manage trusts efficiently. This reduces administrative costs while maintaining protections for beneficiaries through the trustee's fiduciary duties and the right to petition the court if concerns arise.

## Key State-Specific Rules

- **No State Income Tax but Estate Tax Applies:** Washington has no income tax but imposes a state estate tax with a lower exemption than federal, requiring separate estate planning for larger trusts.
- **Trust Law Modernization:** Washington modernized its trust code to align with the Uniform Trust Code, providing clearer rules on trustee duties, beneficiary rights, and trust administration.
- **Community Property:** Washington is a community property state, meaning trusts involving married couples must carefully track separate versus community property, as this affects taxation, creditor access, and distribution rights.
- **B&O Tax on Business Entities:** If the trust holds business entities operating in Washington, the Business and Occupation tax may apply to those entities, requiring entity-level compliance.
