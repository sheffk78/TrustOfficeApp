# Trust Compliance: Texas

## State Income Tax on Trusts

Texas does not impose a state income tax on individuals or trusts. This is one of the most significant advantages for trusts administered in Texas, as trust income is not subject to state-level income taxation regardless of the amount or source (provided the income is not sourced to another state that taxes it). Trustees of Texas trusts do not need to file a state fiduciary income tax return for Texas purposes.

However, Texas does impose a franchise tax on certain business entities. If a trust operates as or through a business entity that is subject to the Texas franchise tax — for example, a Texas LLC or corporation held within the trust — that entity may owe franchise tax. The Texas franchise tax is a margins tax calculated on revenue minus either cost of goods sold or compensation. The tax applies to entities with annualized total revenue above $1,230,000 (as of recent thresholds), with rates ranging from 0.375% to 1% depending on entity type. Purely passive trusts holding investments and not operating a business generally are not subject to franchise tax directly.

## Filing Requirements

Because Texas has no state income tax, there is no state fiduciary income tax return to file. If the trust holds business entities subject to franchise tax, those entities file Texas franchise tax returns using Form 05-158 (Long Form) or Form 05-164 (EZ Computation Report), due May 15 each year. Trustees should confirm whether any entity within the trust structure has Texas franchise tax obligations.

For federal purposes, trusts file IRS Form 1041 as usual. Texas residency does not change federal filing obligations. Trustees should ensure they maintain federal compliance even though state-level tax filing is simplified.

## Beneficiary Notice Requirements

Texas does not have a specific statutory beneficiary notice requirement equivalent to California's Probate Code 16061.7 or Florida's F.S. 736.0813. The Texas Trust Code does require trustees to keep beneficiaries reasonably informed about the trust and its administration, and to provide an annual accounting upon request. Under Texas Trust Code Section 113.051, the trustee has a duty to inform and account to beneficiaries, but there is no mandatory statutory notice triggered by a specific event such as trust becoming irrevocable or change of trustee.

Trustees should still provide regular accountings and communications to beneficiaries as a best practice, even though a formal statutory notice is not required. Failure to keep beneficiaries informed can lead to claims of breach of fiduciary duty.

## Court Supervision

Unsupervised trust administration is the norm in Texas. Trusts are not required to be registered with a court to be valid or to operate. The Texas Trust Code allows trustees to administer trusts entirely outside of court supervision. Court involvement typically occurs only when a beneficiary or trustee petitions the court due to a dispute, request for interpretation, or other contested matter.

This makes Texas an efficient jurisdiction for trust administration, as ongoing court filings, annual reports to the court, and court oversight are not part of routine compliance. Trustees should maintain meticulous records internally, however, since the absence of court supervision places greater responsibility on the trustee to self-document and self-regulate.

## Key State-Specific Rules

- **No State Income Tax:** Texas trusts avoid state income tax entirely, making it attractive for wealth preservation and income accumulation.
- **Franchise Tax Exposure:** Business entities held within trusts may owe franchise tax, so trustees must monitor entity-level obligations.
- **Community Property:** Texas is a community property state, requiring careful classification of separate versus community property in trust planning.
- **Unsupervised Administration:** The default trust administration model is unsupervised, which reduces administrative costs but increases the importance of internal compliance discipline.
