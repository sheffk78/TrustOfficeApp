# First Time in the Trust — The Questions People Ask When Getting Into an Ecclesiastical Trust

**WingPoint · Draft for review · August 21, 2026**
**Purpose:** A WingPoint-branded walkthrough for someone encountering an ecclesiastical (508(c)(1)(a)) trust for the first time. Real questions, answered plainly, grounded in the code — not in fear, and not in hype.

---

## Read this first

This document answers the questions people ask when they first learn about a WingPoint ecclesiastical trust. It is **education, not legal advice**. Every claim here traces to a statute, a court case, or the IRS's own publication — and each is cited so you can verify it yourself. We do not ask you to trust us. We ask you to read the code.

The trust concept has two parts that travel together:
1. **The 508(c)(1)(a) religious organization** — the tax-exempt shell, automatic by law. This is the *roof* over the whole structure.
2. **The private, irrevocable, ecclesiastical trust** — the *structure* under that roof, created by contract under common law. This is the "one person, three seats" trust you administer.

If you're new, you'll meet both. Here's what people ask first.

---

## Q1. What exactly is an "ecclesiastical trust"?

An ecclesiastical trust is a trust organized around sincerely held religious beliefs and purposes — built to hold and steward assets in service of those beliefs. "Ecclesiastical" means of the church. In WingPoint's framework, the trust layers:

- **A religious-purpose organization** (the 508(c)(1)(a) shell — see Q2), and
- **A private, irrevocable trust** ("created by contract under common law, not by statute), holding the assets and administering stewardship.

The trust is its own legal person. It has its own name, its own EIN, its own bank accounts, its own contracts. The trustee signs as fiduciary — not as the individual who happened to create it.

---

## Q2. Is this "a church in a box"? Is it legal?

Let's be plain. The 508(c)(1)(a) exemption isn't something WingPoint invented, and it isn't a tax dodge. Here's the actual law:

**§508(c)(1)(a) of the Internal Revenue Code:**
> "Exceptions. — Subsection (a) shall not apply to — (1) churches, their integrated auxiliaries, and conventions or associations of churches."

Section 508(a) is the rule that *most* organizations must file **Form 1023** to be recognized as tax-exempt. §508(c)(1)(a) says churches *don't have to*. The word "shall" is a command. The exemption, where it applies, is automatic and unconditional.

Why? Because the Constitution requires it. The **First Amendment** says Congress can't establish or restrict religion. If the IRS could decide which churches are "legitimate" and which are tax dodges, that would put the government in exactly the position the First Amendment forbids. The exemption is the boundary the Constitution draws.

`Walz v. Tax Commission, 397 U.S. 664 (1970)` — the Supreme Court held that exempting churches creates **less** government entanglement than taxing them. The government stays out of church finances *because* the First Amendment prefers it.

So: legal? Yes — it's as old as the First Amendment, and the Supreme Court has said so. It is not a loophole; it's a door the government cannot close.

---

## Q3. "Okay, but isn't this just avoiding taxes?"

Understand the difference between a *motive* and a *qualification*.

The Code doesn't ask why. §508(c)(1)(a) doesn't say "churches that weren't formed for tax reasons are exempt." It says the filing requirement "shall not apply" to churches — period. The IRS has no lawful authority to judge your sincerity before granting what the law makes automatic.

Here's the trap in that question: it assumes a 508 organization exists only to dodge taxes. But a religious organization that holds and administers assets for religious and family stewardship purposes — with trustees bound by fiduciary duty — is performing a religious function. Structuring to minimize tax is not the same as evading. And the First Amendment protects the *organization itself* — not just its motives.

---

## 4. "The code doesn't define 'church.' Isn't that a problem?"

The reverse is a problem. The fact that the Code doesn't define "church" is *pro-State-protective*.

The IRS uses **14 criteria** (from *American Guidance Foundation*, 1959) to assess church status. They are **guidelines, not requirements.** The Code deliberately doesn't define "church," and the First Amendment prohibits the government from imposing a narrow, externally invented definition of what counts as religion.

If someone tells you "that's not a real church," ask them: *which statute defines "church"?* There isn't one. They're applying a personal standard, not the Code.

---

## 4. "Can it be just my family? How many members do we need?"

No statute requires a minimum membership. The First Amendment doesn't require a minimum congregation size. The IRS has never successfully denied church status solely because the organization is a family.

A family that gathers around shared spiritual beliefs, commits resources to those beliefs, and governs them through trustees with fiduciary duties **is** operating a religious organization. The structure counts — its size does not.

---

## 5. "How does the money side not run into 'inurement'?"

"Inurement" is the technical scare — it sounds the most intimidating. Here's what it actually is:

Under **§501(c)(3)** law, "inurement" means the net earnings of a tax-exempt organization benefiting a private individual (a founder, officer). A charity used as a personal piggy bank loses its exemption.

But here's the key point: **the §501(c)(3) inurement rules don't import into §508(c)(1)(a)**. A 508 organization never applied for 501(c)(3) — it's exempt under a *different* section. Those inurement conditions attach to 501(c)(3) recognition, not 508 organizations.

And even if a fair-minded inurement test were apply, a properly structured trust clears it:
- **Trustees hold fiduciary duties, not ownership.** They manage assets for the trust's religious purposes; they don't own them.
- **Reasonable compensation is not inurement.** Churches pay their pastors. Administering a trust for reasonable compensation is a legitimate expense.
- **The purpose test is what matters.** Is the organization operated exclusively for exempt religious purposes? A trust that stewards assets for religious and family purposes, under fiduciary trustees, is.

WingPoint trusts are drawn with corporate purpose limitations, trustee duties, and explicit inurement prohibitions in the trust document itself — precisely so this question has a clean answer.

---

## 6. "Do I have to file Form 1023?"

No. §508(c)(1)(a) says the filing requirement — **shall not apply**." A command.

The IRS's own **Publication 1828** confirms:
> "Churches that meet the requirements of section 501(c)(3) are automatically considered tax-exempt and are not required to apply for and obtain recognition of exempt status from the IRS."

Many churches file 1023 *anyway* — not because they must, but because the determination letter gives donors a document showing their contribution is deductible. That's convenience, not a requirement. A church that never files is just as exempt by law.

---

## 7. "What about the EIN, the bank, the state — who do I need for that?"

- **EIN:** File Form SS-4. On the entity-type question, a 508(c)(1)(a) ecclesiastical organization checks **"Trust (TIN of grantor)"** on the relevant line — not "church" — because 508 status is about tax exemption, not legal form. Getting an EIN doesn't require a 501(c)(3) determination letter.
- **Bank account:** A 508 church can open an account with its EIN. You don't need a 501(c)(3) letter. If a bank says otherwise, they're mistaken — the agency itself says the exemption is automatic. Ask them to show you where the statutory requirement is.
- **Property tax:** Many states exempt property owned and used by religious organizations. That's a *separate* application per county — do not confuse the *federal automatic exemption* with *state applications*. Your federal exemption doesn't depend on state approval.

---

## 8. "What happens if the IRS contacts me?"

Don't panic — and don't ignore them. And don't enter a conversation alone.

Know the shield. Congress added **§7611** in 1984, and it raised the bar for the IRS to investigate *at all*. For a church tax inquiry, the IRS must:
1. Have a high-level official reasonably believe — in writing, on the facts — that the org may not qualify;
2. Give written notice explaining the concerns and subject;
3. Offer a conference before any exam;
4. Complete the exam within **2 years**;
5. Get regional-counsel written approval before any adverse finding.

That's a much higher bar than a normal nonprofit faces. It exists so the state can't use the tax code to raid a church.

**And if the IRS talks to you — you have rights under §7521:**
- **Right to representation:** state you want counsel and the IRS **must suspend** the interview;
- **Right to have counsel handle it:** with a power of attorney, they handle it;
- **Right to record:** you may audio-record any in-person interview, with advance notice, at your expense;
- **Right to be informed:** the IRS must explain the process and rights before starting.

**The practical posture: respond in writing, keep copies, don't volunteer, follow the process. If the inquiry progresses, take counsel experienced in church tax matters.** This is not WingPoint selling services — it's the procedural reality.

---

## 9. "If it were this simple, why doesn't everyone do it?"

Most people never read the Code. They operate on what they were told, not on what the statutes say. The fact that most people don't know these definitions isn't evidence the definitions are wrong — it's evidence they're obscure.

Everyone doesn't read the Code. Everyone doesn't know the consent system, or the automatic exemption, or the §7611 shield. The complexity isn't in the law — it's in **unlearning what you were taught the law said.**

---

## 10. "Is this too good to be true? Won't the IRS just win?"

The IRS loses in Tax Courts regularly — that's a fact. When you say "the IRS never loses," you mean "the IRS seems too powerful to challenge." That's a *feeling*, not a fact. §7611 gives churches real procedural protection and §7521 gives you the right to end an interview.

And there's this — the law made the exemption to be misused *because* the First Amendment forces it. The government cannot insert itself into deciding which churches are real, because doing so *is* the Establishment Clause failing. That's not a vulnerability a well-funded agency can just patch away — it's a checked constitutional line Congress and the courts have reinforced, not weakened, since 1969.

---

## The two things to hold onto before you go deeper

1. **The exemption is mandatory, automatic, and constitutional.** The Code says **"shall not apply"** to churches. That's not discretion — it's a boundary.
2. **The trust gives structure to freedom.** The 508 shell is the roof; the trust is the framework beneath it — separate, governed, fiduciary, purposeful.

And remember the spine that runs through all of it: **claim → authority → comparison → validate → calibrated conclusion.** Every point above has a statute or a case behind it. Read the Code. Verify. This is empowerment, not advocacy theater. This is the door that was always open — most people just walk past because it looks too small.
