# Irrevocable Trust (General)

## What It Is

An irrevocable trust is a trust that, once established and funded, cannot be modified, amended, or revoked by the grantor without the consent of the beneficiaries or a court order. Unlike a revocable living trust—where the grantor retains control and can change terms freely—an irrevocable trust removes the assets from the grantor's estate and the grantor's control. This permanence is the trade-off for the benefits: potential estate tax reduction, asset protection, and (in some cases) income tax advantages. Because the grantor no longer controls the assets, the trust is a separate taxable entity and must file its own tax return.

## Key Governance Requirements

- **Irrevocability.** The grantor cannot unilaterally change the trust's terms, remove assets, or dissolve the trust. Any modification requires either beneficiary consent (under IRC Section 641 or state law mechanisms like decanting) or a court order. Some trusts include limited power-of-appointment or trust-protector provisions that allow narrow modifications, but the default is permanence.
- **Separate tax filing (Form 1041).** The trust files its own income tax return, IRS Form 1041, each calendar year. The trust has its own taxpayer identification number (EIN). Income retained in the trust is taxed at compressed trust tax rates, which reach the top federal bracket at very low income thresholds—often under $15,000. Income distributed to beneficiaries is deducted on the trust's return and reported to beneficiaries on Schedule K-1.
- **Trustee independence.** The trustee administers the trust for the beneficiaries, not the grantor. If the grantor serves as trustee, the trust's asset protection and tax benefits may be undermined. An independent trustee—someone not subordinate to the grantor—preserves the trust's integrity. Some trusts use a directed trustee structure or a trust protector to balance independence with the grantor's intent.
- **Beneficiary rights.** Beneficiaries have the right to an accounting, information about trust assets, and distributions as provided in the trust document. They can petition a court to remove a trustee who breaches fiduciary duty. The trustee must communicate with beneficiaries and provide regular reports.
- **Modification limitations.** Depending on state law, modification may be possible through decanting (moving trust assets into a new trust with modified terms), judicial modification, or nonjudicial settlement agreements. The trustee must understand which mechanisms are available under the governing state's law and follow the correct procedure.
- **Fiduciary duties.** The trustee owes beneficiaries the duties of loyalty, prudence, impartiality, and accounting. Self-dealing is prohibited. Investments must follow the Prudent Investor Rule.

## Distribution Rules

- **Terms govern distributions.** The trust document specifies mandatory distributions (such as "pay all income to my spouse annually") and discretionary distributions (such as "distribute principal for beneficiaries' health and education"). The trustee must follow these instructions precisely.
- **HEMS standard.** Many discretionary trusts use the HEMS standard—Health, Education, Maintenance, Support—as the distribution benchmark. This gives the trustee guidance and flexibility while keeping distributions non-taxable gifts to the beneficiary.
- **Income distribution and K-1s.** Income distributed to beneficiaries is reported on Schedule K-1 and taxed on the beneficiary's personal return. Income accumulated in the trust is taxed at trust rates. The trustee must decide each year whether to distribute or accumulate, considering both tax efficiency and beneficiary needs.
- **Termination.** The trust terminates according to its terms—often at a specified date, upon a beneficiary's death, or when assets are fully distributed. The trustee must account, file a final Form 1041, and distribute remaining assets.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Grantor retaining too much control.** If the grantor serves as trustee, controls distributions to themselves, or retains reversionary interests, the trust's asset protection and estate tax benefits may be voided. The IRS can treat the trust as a grantor trust, pulling the assets back into the grantor's estate.
- **Missing Form 1041 deadlines.** Trust tax returns are due April 15 (calendar-year trusts), not the personal return deadline. Late filings carry penalties, and compressed trust tax rates make even small amounts costly.
- **Failing to issue K-1s.** If income is distributed but K-1s are not issued, beneficiaries cannot report the income correctly and the trust loses the distribution deduction, resulting in double taxation.
- **Self-dealing.** A trustee who lends trust funds to themselves, buys assets from the trust, or uses trust property for personal benefit commits a breach of fiduciary duty, regardless of intent. The consequences include surcharge, removal, and personal liability.
