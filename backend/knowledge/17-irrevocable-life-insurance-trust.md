# Irrevocable Life Insurance Trust (ILIT)

## What It Is
An irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT) is an irrevocable trust designed to hold one or more life insurance policies on the grantor's life (or a spouse's life) so that the death benefit is excluded from the grantor's taxable estate. It is used by individuals whose estates may exceed the federal estate tax exemption, or who want to ensure liquidity for estate taxes, business buyouts, or legacy planning. Because the trust — not the grantor — owns the policy and is the beneficiary, the death benefit passes outside the taxable estate. The trust is irrevocable from the moment it is signed; it cannot be amended or revoked.

## Key Governance Requirements
- **Obtain an Employer Identification Number (EIN).** The ILIT is a separate taxpayer. The trustee must obtain an EIN from the IRS and use it for all policy and account registrations — never the grantor's Social Security number.
- **File Form 1041 if the trust has taxable income.** While life insurance death benefits are income-tax-free, the trust may earn income from interest on the policy cash value, dividends, or invested premiums. A simple ILIT with no income may not need to file, but the trustee must monitor this annually.
- **Crummey power notice requirements.** When the grantor contributes premium money to the trust, the trustee must notify each beneficiary who holds a Crummey withdrawal right within a specific window — generally 30 days — giving them the opportunity to withdraw their share of the contribution. Failure to provide proper notice can cause the contribution to be a taxable gift rather than qualifying for the annual gift tax exclusion (currently $19,000 per recipient in 2025).
- **No estate tax return needed for the trust itself, but Form 706 may be required.** The ILIT's death benefit is outside the gross estate, but the trustee or executor should coordinate with the estate's preparer to confirm proper reporting, especially regarding the three-year rule for policies transferred within three years of death.
- **Maintain strict separation of trust assets.** The trustee must never commingle ILIT assets with personal funds or grantor funds. Premium payments must come from the trust account, and the trust must own the policy from inception or via a proper transfer-for-value.

## Distribution Rules
- **During the grantor's life**, the primary "distribution" is the payment of life insurance premiums. The trustee uses contributed funds to pay premiums after giving Crummey notices.
- **At the grantor's death**, the death benefit is paid to the ILIT. The trustee then distributes according to the trust terms — commonly to the grantor's spouse and/or children, either outright or in continuing trust.
- **Distributions to beneficiaries are typically discretionary.** The trustee usually has broad discretion (often HEMS or sole discretion) to distribute trust principal and income for beneficiaries' benefit.
- **The grantor cannot be a beneficiary.** If the grantor retains any beneficial interest, the death benefit is pulled back into the taxable estate. The trustee must ensure no indirect benefit flows to the grantor (e.g., loaning trust funds to the grantor).

## Common Pitfalls
- **Missing or late Crummey notices.** This is the single most common ILIT error. If the trustee does not give each powerholder notice within the required window (typically 30 days), the contribution may not qualify for the annual gift tax exclusion, creating a taxable gift that may require a gift tax return (Form 709).
- **The grantor pays premiums directly.** If the grantor writes a check to the insurance company instead of contributing to the trust and letting the trustee pay, the policy may be treated as owned by the grantor, pulling the death benefit back into the taxable estate. All premiums must flow through the trust account.
- **Transferring an existing policy triggers the three-year rule.** If the grantor transfers an existing policy to the ILIT and dies within three years, the death benefit is included in the gross estate. The trustee should note the transfer date and track the three-year window.
- **Forgetting to file Form 709.** Even when Crummey notices are properly given, contributions that exceed the annual exclusion amount per beneficiary (after applying withdrawal rights) require a gift tax return. Trustees and grantors frequently overlook this filing.
- **No records of notice.** A verbal or informal notice is not sufficient. The trustee must maintain written notice and preferably a beneficiary acknowledgment or proof of mailing. Without documentation, the exclusion can be challenged years later.
