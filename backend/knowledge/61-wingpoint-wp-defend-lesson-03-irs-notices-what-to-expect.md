# Lesson 3: IRS Notices — What to Expect and How to Handle Them

**Course:** WP-DEFEND — Standing Strong: Defending Your Trust and Your Position  
**Lesson:** 3 of 10  

---

You filed a corrected return. Or you filed a Form 4852. Or you zeroed out a W-2 that mischaracterized your compensation. And now there's a letter from the IRS in your mailbox.

You know from Lesson 2 why your position has power. Now let's talk about what actually shows up in your mailbox, what it means, and what to do about it.

---

## The Notice You're Most Likely to Receive

If you filed a corrected return or a Form 4852, the notice you'll most likely receive is a **CP2000 — Notice of Proposed Adjustment**.

This is not a bill. It is not a penalty. It is not a finding that you're wrong. It's the IRS saying: "We received information that doesn't match what you reported on your return. Here's what we think the difference is, and here's what we propose to adjust."

If you filed a 4852 that zeroed out wages, you *created* a discrepancy on purpose. The CP2000 is the IRS's automated system flagging that discrepancy. It is the expected, predictable result of what you did.

Most CP2000s resolve at the first response. When you send a clear, documented response with your 4852 and your cited position, you're giving the examiner something they can work with. The IRS processes millions of notices a year. A clean, documented response gives them a reason to close the file.

**What to do with a CP2000:**

- **Read it carefully.** Note the notice number (top right corner), what the IRS is proposing, the deadline for responding, and which specific items they're questioning.
- **Respond in writing within the deadline.** Phone calls don't create a record you control. Letters do.
- **Respond on point.** Answer what they asked — nothing more, nothing less. If they're questioning line 1 of your 1040, respond about line 1. Don't write a dissertation about the entire tax code.
- **Respond with documentation.** Include a copy of your 4852. Cite the specific statute. Show your calculation. The IRS processes paper — give them paper that supports your position.
- **Send it certified mail with return receipt.** Keep the receipt. Keep a copy of what you sent. Date everything.

That's the core of it. Most people who file corrected returns receive a CP2000, respond with documentation, and the matter resolves. That's the typical path.

---

## The W-2 Trap — The Most Common Mistake

You may receive a notice asking you to provide your W-2s. This feels straightforward — they're asking for documents, you have documents, so you send them. **Don't.**

The IRS already has your original W-2. That's the document that started this whole process. Your employer filed it, the IRS received it, and their system matched it against your return and found a discrepancy. By filing a Form 4852, you declared that the original W-2 was incorrect and that the 4852 is the correct substitute.

If you send the original W-2, you just contradicted your own correction. You handed them evidence against your position.

**The correct response:** Treat the original W-2 as null and void. The 4852 is now the controlling document. If the IRS asks for W-2s, respond by providing a copy of your Form 4852 and a brief statement that the 4852 was filed because the original W-2 contained an error, and that the 4852 is the correct and complete statement of your compensation. Do not send the old W-2.

---

## 60-Day Letters — Procedural, Not Escalation

After you respond to a CP2000, you may receive a notice telling you the IRS needs an additional 60 days to review your response. These are common and they are not escalations — they are delays. The IRS is overloaded. File it, note the date, and keep waiting.

---

## Never Sign the Examination Agreement Form

Before or alongside a formal CP2000, you may receive a letter that includes an agreement form — a line for you to sign agreeing to the IRS's proposed adjustment. This is not a requirement. It is an option, and it is a trap.

If you sign the agreement form, you are waiving your right to challenge the adjustment. The agreement form is not an acknowledgment that you received the notice — it is a waiver of your rights. If it says "I agree" or "I consent to the proposed changes," and you don't agree, don't sign it. Respond in writing with your documented position instead.

---

## How to Respond to Any Notice

The rules for responding to any IRS notice are the same:

**Respond in writing.** Phone calls don't create a record you control. The person on the phone is not authorized to make decisions about your case. If you must call, follow up in writing immediately.

**Respond on point.** Answer the question that was asked. Don't write a 20-page response when three paragraphs will do.

**Respond with documentation.** Include your 4852. Cite the specific statute. Show your calculation.

**Respond within the deadline.** If the notice says respond within 30 days, respond within 30 days. Missing a deadline doesn't make you wrong, but it allows the IRS to proceed to the next step without your input.

**Respond with certified mail.** Send everything via USPS certified mail with return receipt requested. Keep the receipt. Keep a copy of what you sent. Date everything.

**Do not volunteer information you weren't asked for.** The IRS asked a specific question. Answer it. Every additional piece of information you provide is another thing that can be misunderstood, misinterpreted, or used to create a new question.

## The Typical Path

Here's what the most common experience looks like for someone who files a corrected return or 4852:

1. **You file.** You submit your corrected return or 4852, documenting your position and citing the relevant statutes.

2. **6–18 months later:** You receive a CP2000 proposing an adjustment. This is expected. It's the system doing what the system does — matching records and flagging discrepancies. It is not a sign that you did something wrong.

3. **You respond.** You provide your documentation, cite your position, and send it certified mail.

4. **The IRS may send a 60-day letter.** This just means they need more time. File it, note the date, and keep waiting.

5. **3–12 months after your response:** The IRS reviews what you sent. They may accept it, ask for more information, or issue a determination.

If they accept your response — which happens often — the matter closes. This is where most people's experience ends.

If they disagree, the matter can continue — and that's where Lesson 4 picks up: what happens when the IRS pushes back, what your rights are, and how to make them prove their position.

---

## If a Response Is Required vs. When Silence Is Appropriate

Most notices require a response. The IRS is telling you something and giving you a chance to disagree. If you don't respond, they assume you agree and proceed accordingly. Silence is consent.

The one exception: 60-day continuation letters. If the letter says "we need an additional 60 days to review your case" and it doesn't ask you for anything, you don't need to respond. Just file it and wait.

**The general rule: If a notice asks you a question or proposes an action, respond to it. Briefly, accurately, on point, and in writing.**
