# Revocable Living Trust

## What It Is
A revocable living trust (RLT) is a trust created during the grantor's lifetime that the grantor can amend, restate, or revoke entirely at any point while they are alive. It is the most common estate planning vehicle in the United States, used by individuals and couples who want to avoid probate, maintain privacy, and provide for seamless asset management if they become incapacitated. The grantor typically serves as their own trustee during life, naming a successor trustee to step in at death or incapacity.

## Key Governance Requirements
- **No separate tax return during grantor's life.** A revocable living trust uses the grantor's Social Security number. All income flows through to the grantor's personal Form 1040. No Form 1041 is required until the trust becomes irrevocable.
- **Fiduciary duties apply to successor trustees.** Once a successor trustee takes over (at death or incapacity), they owe full fiduciary duties: loyalty, prudence, impartiality, and accounting. The fact that the trust was "informal" during the grantor's life does not relax these duties afterward.
- **Recordkeeping should begin immediately.** Even while the grantor is alive and serving as trustee, maintaining records of trust assets, transactions, and amendments makes the eventual transition far cleaner. A well-maintained Schedule A (trust asset schedule) is critical.
- **State law governs administration.** Most states have adopted some version of the Uniform Trust Code. Trustees must follow the specific state's statutes on notices to beneficiaries, accountings, and trustee compensation.
- **No probate filing required.** Unlike a will, the trust does not get filed with the probate court at death, which is the primary advantage. However, this also means there is no court supervision — the successor trustee must self-administer correctly.
- **Estate tax is NOT avoided.** Assets in a revocable living trust are included in the grantor's taxable estate for federal estate tax purposes (Form 706) and state estate/inheritance tax where applicable.

## Distribution Rules
- **During the grantor's life**, distributions are typically made to or for the benefit of the grantor at their sole discretion. Since the grantor is usually the trustee and beneficiary, this is effectively unrestricted access.
- **After death**, distributions follow the terms set in the trust agreement. Common structures include outright distributions to named beneficiaries, distributions to sub-trusts (such as marital trusts, bypass/credit shelter trusts, or minor's trusts), or staggered age-based distributions (e.g., one-third at 25, half at 30, remainder at 35).
- **HEMS standard is common.** Many RLTs authorize the successor trustee to distribute principal for a beneficiary's health, education, maintenance, and support in reasonable comfort. This gives flexibility while providing a fiduciary standard.
- **Discretionary distributions** to beneficiaries must be made in accordance with the fiduciary duty of impartiality — a successor trustee cannot play favorites among beneficiaries.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Thinking the trust avoids all taxes.** It avoids probate, not estate tax. Grantors frequently confuse probate avoidance with tax avoidance. The assets are still in the taxable estate.
- **Never funding the trust.** A signed but unfunded trust is essentially useless at death. Assets titled in the grantor's individual name still go through probate. The successor trustee must verify that re-titling was completed.
- **Failing to transition properly at death.** The trust becomes irrevocable at the grantor's death. Successor trustees often do not realize this triggers new duties: obtaining a tax ID (EIN), filing Form 1041, giving statutory notices to beneficiaries, and providing accountings.
- **No records of amendments.** Successor trustees frequently discover multiple amendments, codicils, or restatements with no clear record of which controls. Without a complete document trail, administration can stall or lead to litigation. Maintaining all versions in the Vault prevents this.
