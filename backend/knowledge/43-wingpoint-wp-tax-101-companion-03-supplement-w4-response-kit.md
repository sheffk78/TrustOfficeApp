# W-4 Response Kit — Supplemental Materials for Lesson 3

**Course:** WP-TAX-101 — The Tax Question  
**Document Type:** Educational templates — not legal advice  
**Purpose:** To provide template letters for individuals who, after studying the statutory definitions and withholding mechanics in Lesson 3, have concluded that they may not fall within the definitions that trigger mandatory withholding

## Document 1: Letter to Employer — Notification of Exempt Status and Withholding Position

**When to use:** When you have determined, based on your own study of the statutes and regulations, that your compensation does not constitute "wages" as defined in 26 USC §3401(a) because you are not a statutory "employee" as defined in §3401(c), and you wish to inform your employer of your position and request that they not withhold from your compensation.

**Key statutory basis:**
- 26 USC §3401(a) — "wages" defined with 23 exceptions
- 26 USC §3401(c) — "employee" defined as federal officer/person elected to office
- 26 USC §3401(d) — "employer" defined in relation to statutory "employee"
- 26 USC §3402(a) — withholding applies only to "wages" as defined
- 26 USC §3402(d) — if employee pays the tax, employer is not liable for withholding
- 26 USC §3402(n) — exemption from withholding available
- 26 C.F.R. §31.3402(p)-1 — W-4 is a "voluntary withholding agreement"

### [TEMPLATE BEGINS]

**[Your Name]**
**[Your Address]**
**[Date]**

**[Employer Name / HR Department]**
**[Employer Address]**

**RE: Notification Regarding Withholding Status and W-4 Position**

Dear [Employer Name / HR Contact]:

I am writing to inform you of my position regarding the Employee's Withholding Allowance Certificate (Form W-4) and the withholding of federal income tax from my compensation.

I have undertaken a careful study of the Internal Revenue Code and its implementing regulations. Based on that study, I have concluded that my compensation does not fall within the statutory definition of "wages" as that term is defined in 26 USC §3401(a), because I do not hold a position that qualifies me as a statutory "employee" under 26 USC §3401(c).

I understand this may be unfamiliar, so I want to explain my position clearly and provide the relevant statutory citations for your review.

**The Statutory Definitions**

The Internal Revenue Code defines "wages," "employee," and "employer" more narrowly than common usage suggests:

- **26 USC §3401(a)** defines "wages" as remuneration paid to a statutory "employee" by a statutory "employer" for employment services — and then lists twenty-three specific exceptions to even that definition.

- **26 USC §3401(c)** defines "employee" as including "an officer, employee, or elected official of the United States, a State, or any political subdivision thereof, or the District of Columbia, or any agency or instrumentality of any one or more of the foregoing." This definition points to federal and government employment — not private-sector employment generally.

- **26 USC §3401(d)** defines "employer" in relation to the statutory "employee." If the person receiving compensation is not a statutory "employee" under §3401(c), then the person paying that compensation is not a statutory "employer" under §3401(d).

**What This Means for Withholding**

26 USC §3402(a) authorizes withholding only from "wages" as defined in §3401(a). If the compensation paid to me does not constitute "wages" within the statutory meaning — because I am not a statutory "employee" engaged in a "trade or business" as defined in 26 USC §7701(a)(26) — then the withholding authority in §3402(a) does not attach to my compensation.

**Employer Liability — 26 USC §3402(d)**

I want to address any concern you may have about employer liability. 26 USC §3402(d) provides:

> "If the employee pays the tax on wages, the employer is not liable for withholding — even if withholding was required and not done."

This means that even in a scenario where withholding might have been required, if the person liable for the tax pays it directly, the employer bears no liability for the failure to withhold. The statute itself contains this protection for employers.

**My Position Regarding the W-4**

26 C.F.R. §31.3402(p)-1 describes the W-4 as a "voluntary withholding agreement." The regulation uses the word "voluntary." I am not voluntarily entering into a withholding agreement that treats my private compensation as statutory "wages" when, based on my study of the code, I have concluded that it does not meet the statutory definition.

**What I Am Requesting**

1. That you refrain from withholding federal income tax from my compensation, on the basis that my compensation does not constitute "wages" as defined in 26 USC §3401(a).

2. That you refrain from filing a Form W-2 reporting my compensation as "wages" subject to federal income tax withholding, on the same basis.

3. If your legal counsel or compliance team determines that withholding and W-2 filing are required regardless of my position, I will provide a signed Form W-4 claiming exemption under 26 USC §3402(n), as I had no federal income tax liability last year and expect none this year.

**My Commitment**

This matter is between me and the Internal Revenue Service. I am not asking you to take any position on the law. I am informing you of my position and requesting that you not withhold from my compensation when the statutory basis for that withholding does not, in my understanding, apply. If the IRS contacts you regarding my withholding status, I will handle that correspondence directly.

I am happy to provide any additional information you need and to discuss this matter with your legal or compliance team.

Respectfully,

[Your Name]

## Document 2: Letter to IRS — Response to Lock-In Letter Notice or Withholding Inquiry

**When to use:** When the IRS has sent you (or your employer) a notice proposing a "lock-in" letter (also called a "withholding determination" under §3402(f)(2)(B)) or a notice stating that your withholding is insufficient. This letter asserts your position, cites the relevant statutes, and requests that the IRS respond with specificity.

**Key statutory and regulatory basis:**
- 26 USC §3401(a) — "wages" defined with exceptions
- 26 USC §3401(c) — "employee" defined
- 26 USC §3401(d) — "employer" defined
- 26 USC §3402(n) — exemption from withholding
- 26 C.F.R. §31.3402(p)-1 — voluntary withholding agreement
- 26 C.F.R. §31.3402(f)(5) — employer not required to withhold if exemption claim is in effect
- 26 USC §7434 — liability for fraudulent information returns
- 26 USC §6201 — assessment authority
- 26 USC §6212 — notice of deficiency requirements
- 26 USC §7491 — burden of proof shifts to IRS in court proceedings

⚠️ **IMPORTANT CAUTION:** A lock-in letter is a serious matter. The IRS uses lock-in letters to mandate specific withholding amounts and restrict your ability to change your W-4. If you receive a lock-in letter, you should consult with qualified legal counsel before responding. This template is provided for educational purposes only — it is not a substitute for legal representation.

**[Your Name]**
**[Your Address]**
**[Date]**

**Internal Revenue Service**
**[Appropriate Address — see notice for where to respond]**

**RE: Response to [Notice Number / Letter ID] — Proposed Withholding Determination / Lock-In Letter**

**[Your SSN or ITIN, if you choose to provide it — see note below]**

Dear Sir or Madam:

I am writing in response to [Notice Number / Letter ID], dated [date], in which the Internal Revenue Service [proposes to issue a lock-in letter / has issued a lock-in letter / states that my withholding is insufficient].

I have undertaken a careful study of the Internal Revenue Code and its implementing regulations. Based on that study, I have concluded that my compensation does not fall within the statutory definition of "wages" as that term is defined in 26 USC §3401(a), because I do not hold a position that qualifies me as a statutory "employee" under 26 USC §3401(c).

I respectfully request that the Service provide a specific response to the following statutory and regulatory points before proceeding with any withholding determination.

**I. The Statutory Definitions Do Not Describe My Activity**

- **26 USC §3401(c)** defines "employee" as including "an officer, employee, or elected official of the United States, a State, or any political subdivision thereof, or the District of Columbia, or any agency or instrumentality of any one or more of the foregoing." I am a private citizen engaged in private employment in the private sector. I do not hold a federal position, I am not appointed to federal office, and I am not engaged in the performance of the functions of a public office as defined in 26 USC §7701(a)(26).

- **26 USC §3401(a)** defines "wages" as remuneration for services rendered by a statutory "employee" to a statutory "employer" — with twenty-three enumerated exceptions. My private-sector compensation does not meet this definition.

- **26 USC §3401(d)** defines "employer" in relation to the statutory "employee." Because I am not a statutory "employee," my employer is not a statutory "employer" under this provision.

**II. The Withholding Authority Under §3402(a) Does Not Attach to My Compensation**

26 USC §3402(a) authorizes withholding only from "wages" as defined in §3401(a). If my compensation is not "wages" within the statutory meaning, the withholding authority in §3402(a) does not apply.

**III. The W-4 Is a Voluntary Withholding Agreement**

26 C.F.R. §31.3402(p)-1 describes the W-4 as a "voluntary withholding agreement." I am not voluntarily entering into a withholding agreement that treats my private compensation as statutory "wages."

**IV. Exemption From Withholding Under §3402(n)**

26 USC §3402(n) provides for exemption from withholding for individuals who had no federal income tax liability in the preceding year and expect none in the current year. If the Service determines that withholding is required despite my position, I am prepared to claim exemption under this provision.

**V. Information Returns May Not Accurately Reflect My Status**

Any W-2 or 1099 filed on my behalf reporting my private compensation as "wages" or "nonemployee compensation" from federal activities may constitute an inaccurate information return. 26 USC §7434 makes it unlawful to file fraudulent information returns. I am not stating that any specific return is fraudulent — I am noting that the accuracy of such returns depends on whether the compensation meets the statutory definitions, and I have reason to believe it may not.

**VI. Request for Specific Response**

I respectfully request that the Service respond to each of the following with specific statutory and regulatory citations:

1. **Under which specific statutory provision does the Service claim authority to withhold from my compensation?** Please cite the statute and explain how each element of the definition is satisfied.

2. **How does the Service determine that my private-sector employment constitutes "the performance of the functions of a public office" under 26 USC §7701(a)(26)?**

3. **How does the Service determine that I am a statutory "employee" under 26 USC §3401(c)?** Please specify which category of federal employment I fall within.

4. **If the Service claims that my compensation constitutes "wages" under §3401(a), please identify which of the twenty-three exceptions, if any, apply, and explain why the remaining definition encompasses my private-sector compensation.**

5. **Under what authority does the Service propose to override the voluntary nature of the withholding agreement described in 26 C.F.R. §31.3402(p)-1?**

I am not refusing to comply with any lawful requirement. I am requesting that the Service demonstrate, with specific statutory citations, how the withholding requirements apply to my specific circumstances given the definitions as written in the code.

Under 26 USC §7491, if this matter proceeds to judicial proceedings, the burden of proof is on the Service to demonstrate that its position is correct.

**VII. Reservation of Rights**

This letter is written without prejudice to any of my rights under the Constitution and laws of the United States. Nothing in this letter shall be construed as a waiver of any right, privilege, or protection, including but not limited to rights under the First, Fourth, Fifth, and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution.

Nothing in this letter constitutes an admission that I am a "taxpayer" as defined in 26 USC §7701(a)(14), that my compensation constitutes "wages" as defined in 26 USC §3401(a), or that I am engaged in a "trade or business" as defined in 26 USC §7701(a)(26).

I look forward to the Service's specific response to the questions posed above.

Respectfully,

[Your Name]

## Document 3: W-4 Exempt Filing Guide — If You Must File a W-4

**When to use:** When your employer requires you to file a W-4 regardless of your position, and you need to file one claiming exemption under §3402(n) while preserving your legal position.

**Background:** 26 USC §3402(n) provides that an employee who had no federal income tax liability in the preceding year and expects none in the current year may claim exemption from withholding. This is written into the code. The W-4 form has a line for this exemption.

If your employer insists on a W-4 and will not accept your notification that withholding does not apply to your compensation, filing a W-4 claiming exemption under §3402(n) preserves your position while keeping you in compliance with the employer's requirement.

**How to file W-4 exempt:**

1. **Line 1(a)–(b):** Write your name and Social Security Number (if you choose to provide one).

2. **Line 1(c):** If you are claiming exemption, write "Exempt" on this line.

3. **The exemption statement on the W-4:** The form requires you to certify that you had no federal income tax liability last year and expect none this year. Read this statement carefully. Based on your study of the statutes, if you have concluded that your compensation does not constitute "wages" within the statutory meaning and that you therefore had no federal income tax liability, this certification may be consistent with your position.

4. **Attach a statement of your position (optional but recommended):** Consider attaching a brief statement such as:

> "I claim exemption from withholding under 26 USC §3402(n) on the basis that I had no federal income tax liability for the preceding tax year and expect none for the current tax year. This claim is made without prejudice to my position that my compensation does not constitute 'wages' as defined in 26 USC §3401(a) and that I am not a statutory 'employee' as defined in 26 USC §3401(c)."

5. **Keep a copy.** Retain a signed and dated copy of everything you submit.

**Important:** Filing a W-4 claiming exemption under §3402(n) does not constitute an admission that you are a statutory "employee" or that your compensation constitutes statutory "wages." The exemption provision exists in the code for people who have no liability. Claiming it is exercising a right the code provides.

**Lock-in letter warning:** If you claim exemption on your W-4, the IRS may send your employer a lock-in letter that mandates a specific withholding amount and restricts your ability to change your W-4. If this happens, use Document 2 above and consult with qualified legal counsel.
