# Trust Compliance: Delaware

## State Income Tax on Trusts

Delaware does not impose a state income tax on non-resident trusts. This is a key advantage for trusts established in Delaware where neither the grantor nor the beneficiaries are Delaware residents. Non-resident trusts — meaning trusts where the grantor was not a Delaware resident at the time the trust became irrevocable — are not subject to Delaware income tax on income accumulated within the trust, even if a Delaware trustee administers the trust.

For resident trusts, where the grantor was a Delaware domiciliary at the time the trust became irrevocable, Delaware does impose income tax. The Delaware fiduciary income tax rates range from approximately 2.2% to 6.6%, depending on taxable income. However, many Delaware trusts are structured as non-resident trusts to take advantage of the absence of state income tax, making Delaware attractive for out-of-state grantors seeking a favorable trust jurisdiction.

A critical Delaware rule: accumulated income in a non-resident trust is not taxed by Delaware, but income that is distributed to a beneficiary who is a Delaware resident may be subject to Delaware income tax at the beneficiary level. Trustees should coordinate with tax advisors to understand how distributions affect beneficiary tax obligations.

## Filing Requirements

For non-resident trusts, Delaware does not require a state fiduciary income tax return. Trustees file IRS Form 1041 for federal income tax purposes as usual. For resident trusts, Delaware Form 400 (Fiduciary Income Tax Return) is required, due on April 15 for calendar-year trusts or the 15th day of the fourth month after the fiscal year-end.

Estimated tax payments may be required for resident trusts that expect to owe Delaware tax. Trustees of resident trusts should use Delaware Form 400-ES for quarterly estimated payments, due on April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15. Non-resident trusts with no Delaware-source income have no Delaware filing obligation.

## Beneficiary Notice Requirements

Delaware's trust code, codified in Title 12 of the Delaware Code, requires trustees to keep beneficiaries reasonably informed about the trust and its administration. Under the Delaware Trust Act, the trustee must provide beneficiaries with information about the trust, including a copy of the trust instrument upon request. The trustee also has a duty to provide accountings, but Delaware allows significant flexibility — the trust instrument can modify or waive the duty to account and inform beneficiaries, subject to certain protections for beneficiaries.

Delaware's flexibility in beneficiary notice and accounting requirements is a feature that attracts grantors who value privacy and control. However, trustees should be cautious when relying on waivers, as they can increase the risk of disputes. Best practice is to maintain internal records and provide at least minimal communication to beneficiaries, even when the trust instrument allows for waivers.

## Court Supervision

Delaware trusts are generally administered without court supervision. Trusts are not required to be registered with the Court of Chancery or any other court to be valid or to operate. The Delaware Trust Act allows trustees to administer trusts independently of court oversight, which is the standard practice.

Delaware's Court of Chancery is a unique and highly respected equity court that handles trust disputes and provides guidance on trust matters. While routine trust administration does not require court involvement, the availability of the Court of Chancery for trust disputes is a significant advantage. The court is known for its expertise in trust and corporate law, providing predictability and sophisticated judicial oversight when needed. Trustees or beneficiaries can petition the Court of Chancery for guidance, accountings, or resolution of disputes, but this is voluntary and not part of routine compliance.

## Key State-Specific Rules

- **No Income Tax on Non-Resident Trusts:** Delaware's absence of income tax on non-resident trusts makes it a premier jurisdiction for out-of-state grantors seeking tax efficiency.
- **110-Year Perpetuity for Personal Property:** Delaware allows personal property trusts to last for 110 years, enabling multi-generational dynasty planning for personal property.
- **Court of Chancery:** Delaware's Court of Chancery provides expert judicial oversight for trust disputes, offering predictability and sophisticated trust law jurisprudence.
- **Directed Trustee Provisions:** Delaware has comprehensive directed trustee statutes, allowing division of trustee responsibilities among investment, distribution, and administrative trustees for specialized management.
