## The IRS's Most Common Arguments — And Why They Don't Apply

When the IRS or anyone else challenges a 508(c)(1)(a) organization, they typically use one or more of these arguments. Knowing them in advance removes the fear — because each one has a specific legal answer.

### Argument 1: "You're just using this to avoid taxes."

This is the most common attack. The implication is that a 508(c)(1)(a) trust is a tax dodge dressed up as a church.

**The response:** The code doesn't ask about your motives. It asks whether you qualify. §508(c)(1)(a) doesn't say "churches that weren't formed for tax purposes are exempt." It says the filing requirement "shall not apply" to churches — period. The exemption is mandatory and unconditional. The IRS has no authority to evaluate your sincerity or your motives before granting an exemption that the code makes automatic.

The First Amendment backs this up. The Establishment Clause prohibits the government from deciding which churches are "legitimate" and which are "tax dodges." That's exactly the line the government cannot cross. If the IRS could deny your exemption because it didn't believe your religious purpose was sincere, the IRS would be in the business of deciding which churches are real — and that's what the Constitution prohibits.

Walz v. Tax Commission (1970) established that exempting churches creates *less* government entanglement than taxing them. The exemption is the boundary the Constitution draws, not a favor.

### Argument 2: "This isn't a real church."

As you saw in Lesson 6, the Internal Revenue Code doesn't define "church." The IRS uses 14 criteria as *guidelines*, not requirements. When someone says "that's not a real church," they're applying a personal definition — not the code's. The code intentionally doesn't define the term. The First Amendment prohibits the government from imposing a narrow definition.

### Argument 3: "Inurement — you're just benefiting yourself."

This is the argument that sounds the most technical and therefore the most intimidating. Here's what it means and why it doesn't apply when the trust is properly structured.

**What inurement is:** Under 501(c)(3) law, "inurement" means that the net earnings of a tax-exempt organization are benefiting a private individual — an "insider" like a founder, officer, or director. If a 501(c)(3) charity is being used as a personal piggy bank, that's inurement, and it destroys the exemption.

**Why inurement doesn't apply to a properly structured 508(c)(1)(a) trust:**

First, the inurement prohibition in §501(c)(3) applies to organizations that applied for and received tax-exempt status under that section. A 508(c)(1)(a) church never applied for 501(c)(3) status. It's exempt under a different section entirely. The 501(c)(3) rules about private inurement are conditions that come with 501(c)(3) recognition — they're not automatically imported into 508(c)(1)(a).

Second, even if inurement principles were applied, a properly structured trust doesn't violate them. Here's why:

- **The trustee has fiduciary duties, not ownership rights.** A trustee manages trust assets for the benefit of the trust's religious purposes. The trustee doesn't own the assets. The trustee can't use them for personal benefit beyond what the trust document authorizes — typically reasonable compensation for services rendered to the trust.

- **Reasonable compensation isn't inurement.** If a trustee receives reasonable compensation for work performed on behalf of the trust, that's a legitimate expense of the organization — not inurement. Churches pay their pastors. Religious organizations compensate their administrators. This is standard, recognized, and lawful.

- **The purpose test is what matters.** The question isn't whether any individual ever receives anything from the organization. The question is whether the organization is operated exclusively for its exempt religious purposes. A trust that exists to hold and manage assets for religious and family stewardship purposes, governed by trustees with fiduciary duties, is operated for those purposes.

A WingPoint trust is structured with specific governance provisions — trustee duties, purpose limitations, inurement prohibitions in the trust document itself. The trust document prohibits private inurement because WingPoint's structure is designed to be bulletproof on this point. The organization operates for religious purposes. The trustee serves those purposes. Compensation is reasonable and documented. Proper governance — not inurement.

### Argument 4: "You have to file Form 1023 to be tax-exempt."

No. §508(c)(1)(a) says the filing requirement "shall not apply" to churches. The word "shall" is mandatory. Congress could have written "may not apply" or "does not apply unless the organization elects to file." It wrote "shall not apply." A command, not a suggestion.

The IRS's own publication confirms this. IRS Publication 1828 states: "churches that meet the requirements of section 501(c)(3) are automatically considered tax exempt and are not required to apply for and obtain recognition of exempt status from the IRS."

Many churches choose to file Form 1023 anyway — not because they have to, but because the determination letter provides a document they can show to donors who want assurance that their contributions are tax-deductible. That's a convenience, not a requirement. A church that doesn't file is still exempt. The exemption is automatic by law.

### Argument 5: "You can't be a church if it's just your family."

The code doesn't require a minimum number of members. The First Amendment doesn't require a minimum congregation size. There's no statute that says "a church must have at least X members to qualify." The IRS has never successfully challenged a religious organization solely on the basis of family membership.

A family that gathers around shared spiritual beliefs, commits resources to those beliefs, and governs those resources through trustees with fiduciary duties is operating as a religious organization. The structure is what matters — not the size.

## How to Handle IRS Inquiries About Your 508(c)(1)(a) Status

If the IRS contacts you about your trust or your church's tax status, the most important thing to understand is this: **the IRS has special rules for church inquiries that are more protective than anything that applies to other organizations.**

### The §7611 Protections — Your Shield

As you learned in Lesson 6, §7611 (added by Congress in 1984) imposes strict limits on the IRS's ability to even *inquire* into a church's tax status.

You saw the full §7611 framework in Lesson 6. Here's what matters when those protections are tested:

### If the IRS Contacts You — Practical Steps

**1. Don't panic. Don't ignore it. Don't volunteer information.**

The first instinct is either fear or fight. Neither serves you. The IRS operates on administrative procedures — form letters, deadlines, specific protocols. Understanding those protocols gives you the upper hand.

**2. Determine what kind of contact it is.**

Is it a routine notice? A CP letter? A formal inquiry under §7611? The type of contact determines your response. Most IRS contacts are computer-generated form letters — not audits, not investigations, not legal proceedings.

**3. If it's a §7611 inquiry, verify that the procedural requirements have been met.**

Did a Director-level official approve it? Did you receive written notice with specific concerns? Were you offered a conference? If any of these requirements haven't been met, the inquiry itself may be procedurally defective — and you have the right to point that out.

**4. Respond in writing. Keep copies of everything.**

Every communication with the IRS should be in writing. Keep copies. Track dates. If you speak with someone on the phone, follow up with a written summary. This creates a paper trail that protects you.

**5. Know what the IRS can and cannot ask.**

The IRS can ask about your organization's purposes, activities, and governance. They cannot demand that you prove the sincerity of your religious beliefs — that's an Establishment Clause violation. They cannot require you to apply for 501(c)(3) status when §508(c)(1)(a) makes exemption automatic. They cannot bypass the §7611 procedures.

**6. Seek counsel experienced in church tax matters if the inquiry progresses beyond initial correspondence.**

Practical advice, not a pitch for WingPoint's services. If you receive a formal church tax inquiry notice, the procedural requirements are strict on both sides. An attorney who understands §7611 can ensure your rights are protected and the IRS follows its own rules.

## The Objections in Your Own Head

The IRS arguments are one thing. The harder objections are the ones that come from inside. Here are the ones most people hit, and how to think about them:

**"But my accountant says..."**
Your accountant is probably a good person who files honest returns based on what they were taught. But what they were taught is the common understanding — not the statutory definitions. Ask your accountant to show you where §3401(c) defines "employee" as anyone who works for a paycheck. They won't be able to, because it doesn't say that. The question isn't whether your accountant is honest. It's whether they've read the code definitions.

**"But if this were true, everyone would be doing it."**
Everyone doesn't read the code. Everyone doesn't know the definitions. Everyone doesn't know the consent system exists. Most people operate on what they were told — not on what the law actually says. The fact that most people don't do something isn't evidence that it can't be done. It's evidence that most people don't know about it.

**"But the IRS never loses."**
The IRS loses regularly in Tax Court. What you're really saying is "the IRS seems too powerful to challenge." That's a feeling, not a fact. §7611 gives churches specific procedural protections. §7521 gives you the right to stop any interview and bring in a representative. The system has rules, and those rules protect you.

**"But what about the 16th Amendment?"**
Lesson 2 covered this. The 16th Amendment didn't create a new taxing power. The Supreme Court said so in Brushaber. The income tax was always an excise. The amendment removed the argument that it was a direct tax requiring apportionment — because it was never a direct tax in the first place.

**"But this feels too good to be true."**
It doesn't feel too good to be true when you're reading the code definitions. It feels too good to be true when you compare what the code says to what everyone told you it says. The gap between those two things is the gap between what you assumed and what's actually written. That gap is real. The question is whether you're willing to read the code for yourself.

## When Other Government Agencies Push Back

The IRS isn't the only agency you may encounter. Local governments administer property taxes. State agencies administer sales taxes. Courts may encounter your trust in probate or other proceedings. Here's how to handle the most common situations:

### Property Tax Exemption

Many states offer property tax exemptions for properties owned and used by religious organizations. The process for obtaining these exemptions varies by state and county, but the principle is the same: if your trust owns property that's used exclusively for religious purposes, you may qualify.

**Practical steps:**
- Research your county's specific exemption application process — they all have one
- Prepare your trust documents, governance records, and a statement of religious purpose
- Be prepared to explain that your organization is a church under §508(c)(1)(a) — automatically tax-exempt at the federal level, and eligible for state-level religious exemptions
- If the local assessor pushes back, remember: they're applying local ordinances, and local ordinances must comply with both the state constitution and the First Amendment
- Document everything. Keep records of all communications.

### Bank Accounts and EINs

Banks and financial institutions will ask for an EIN and may ask about your organization's tax status. A 508(c)(1)(a) church can obtain an EIN by filing Form SS-4 and checking the "church" box. You don't need a 501(c)(3) determination letter to open a bank account.

If a bank employee says "you need a 501(c)(3) letter," they're mistaken. The code doesn't require it. §508(c)(1)(a) makes the exemption automatic. You may need to educate the bank employee — politely, in writing, with the relevant code section cited.

### State Tax Issues

Some states have their own requirements for church registration or exemption. These vary widely. Research your state's specific requirements. In most cases, a church that's exempt under federal law can also qualify for state-level exemptions — but the process and documentation may differ.

The key principle: **federal exemption under §508(c)(1)(a) is automatic and mandatory. State exemptions may require separate applications.** Don't confuse the two. Your federal exemption doesn't depend on state approval.

## Changing Your Paradigm — From Subject to Steward

This might be the hardest part. Not the law. Not the IRS. Not the paperwork. The paradigm shift.

You've spent your entire adult life operating as an "employee" in a system that treated you as a "taxpayer" by default. You filled out forms without understanding them. You paid amounts you were told you owed. You assumed — because everyone around you assumed — that this was just how it works.

That's the system working as designed. The consent system (Lesson 3) relies on people not understanding what they're consenting to. The information return system (Lesson 3) relies on third parties creating records that don't accurately reflect your status. The withholding system (Lesson 3) relies on money disappearing before you see it.

You're not changing who you are. You're changing how you're classified. You're moving from a default classification that was never meant to describe you to a correct classification that aligns with what the law actually says.

You've had two lessons to sit with this — the anger, the relief, the fear. If it's still there, that's okay. It doesn't have to resolve before you act. What matters is that you're not acting from the confusion you started with. You've read the code. You've seen the definitions. You understand the constitutional framework. The emotion is real, but it's no longer the only thing guiding your decisions.

The shift is from "subject" to "steward." A subject operates under someone else's authority. A steward manages what's been entrusted to them for a defined purpose. Scripture is clear about which one you were made to be. Genesis 1:26 gave you dominion — not submission. Joseph didn't ask Pharaoh's permission to manage Egypt. He was given authority because he was faithful with what he had. The trust structure you've learned about isn't a legal trick. It's the shape your life takes when you stop living as a subject and start living as the steward God made you to be.

## What to Do If You're Being Investigated

If any government agency — federal, state, or local — contacts you for an investigation related to your trust or your tax position:

1. **Don't volunteer information.** Answer what's asked. Don't elaborate. Don't explain your philosophy. Don't try to convince the investigator. You're in a legal process, not a conversation.

2. **Get everything in writing.** If someone calls you, ask them to send their questions in writing. If they show up at your door, ask for their identification and the specific legal authority for their inquiry. Note dates, times, and names.

3. **Know your rights.** For IRS church inquiries, §7611 gives you specific procedural protections. For other agencies, the relevant statutes and constitutional provisions vary. The First Amendment applies to all of them.

4. **Seek experienced counsel.** This course gives you the education. It doesn't give you legal representation. If you're facing a formal inquiry, investigation, or proceeding, get an attorney who understands church tax law and §7611.

### Your Right to Representation — 26 USC §7521

Many people don't know this, but you have specific statutory rights when dealing with the IRS:

**The right to representation (§7521(b)(2)):** If at any time during an IRS interview you clearly state that you wish to consult with an attorney, CPA, enrolled agent, or other authorized representative, the IRS officer **must suspend the interview** — regardless of whether you've already answered questions. You don't have to explain why. You just have to say it.

**The right to have your representative handle it (§7521(c)):** If you give someone a written power of attorney, they can represent you in IRS interviews. The IRS cannot require you to appear in person alongside your representative unless they issue an administrative summons — and even then, there are limits.

**The right to record (§7521(a)):** You have the right to make an audio recording of any in-person IRS interview, at your own expense, with your own equipment — as long as you request it in advance.

**The right to be informed (§7521(b)(1)):** Before any initial interview, the IRS must explain the process and your rights — whether it's an audit process interview or a collection process interview.

These aren't suggestions. They're statutory rights written into the Internal Revenue Code. The IRS is required by law to honor them.

What this means practically: **you never have to face the IRS alone, and you never have to answer questions on the spot.** If an IRS agent contacts you — by phone, by mail, or in person — you have the right to say "I want to consult with my representative" and end the conversation. You have the right to have all further communication go through your attorney. You have the right to understand the process before participating in it.

For churches specifically, this combines with the §7611 protections: the IRS must get high-level approval before even starting an inquiry, must give you written notice, must offer a conference, and must complete the examination within two years. And at any point, you can invoke your right to representation under §7521.

This is not about being adversarial. It's about understanding that the process has rules, and those rules protect you.

5. **Don't let fear make your decisions.** Fear of the IRS is rational — it's a powerful agency. But fear-based decisions (ignoring notices, not responding, making rash filings) are usually worse than informed, deliberate responses. Understand the process. Follow it. Document everything.
