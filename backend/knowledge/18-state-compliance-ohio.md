# Trust Compliance: Ohio

## State Income Tax on Trusts

Ohio imposes a state income tax on trusts that have Ohio nexus. Nonresident trusts with no Ohio-source income generally have no Ohio income tax filing obligation.

Ohio's income tax rates for trusts are graduated. The top rate for trusts is currently 3.125% (as of tax year 2025), which is relatively low compared to states like California and New Jersey. Ohio taxes trusts at the same graduated rate schedule applied to individual taxpayers. Fiduciaries should plan distributions carefully because Ohio allows a deduction for income distributed to beneficiaries, which can shift the tax burden to beneficiaries who may be in lower brackets.

## Filing Requirements

Ohio fiduciary income tax is filed using Form IT 1041, the Ohio Fiduciary Income Tax Return. The filing deadline is April 15 for calendar-year trusts, or the 15th day of the fourth month after the close of the fiscal year. All estates and trusts, including foreign estates and trusts, that are subject to the Ohio fiduciary income tax must file Form IT 1041. An extension can be filed, which provides additional time to file but not to pay. Trustees must also maintain their bank account to ensure they can receive refunds after the trust's closing date.

## Beneficiary Notice Requirements

Ohio does not have a statutory requirement for trustees to provide initial notice to beneficiaries upon accepting trusteeship or when a trust becomes irrevocable. The Ohio Trust Code (Ohio Revised Code Chapter 58) requires trustees to keep qualified beneficiaries reasonably informed about the administration of the trust and the material facts necessary for them to protect their interests. Trustees must respond to beneficiary requests for information and provide reports. While there is no mandatory initial notice deadline, trustees should document all communications with beneficiaries to create a defensible record.

## Court Supervision

Court supervision of trusts in Ohio is optional. A trust does not need to be registered with the court to be valid. Trustees may operate trusts in an unsupervised manner, which is the default for most revocable and irrevocable trusts. However, a trustee or beneficiary may petition the court for supervision if disputes arise or if judicial guidance is needed. Ohio courts can also intervene when a trustee requests instructions or when a beneficiary seeks enforcement of their rights under the trust. ## Key State-Specific Rules

- **Uniform Trust Code:** Ohio adopted the UTC as the Ohio Trust Code, codified in Ohio Revised Code Chapter 58, effective January 1, 2007. Ohio's version includes some state-specific modifications to the model UTC.
- **Spendthrift Protection:** Ohio allows self-settled asset protection trusts, meaning a grantor can place assets in a trust for their own benefit and shield those assets from creditors, subject to certain limitations.
- **Perpetuity Period:** Ohio has abolished the rule against perpetuities, allowing trusts to last indefinitely for multi-generational wealth planning.
- **Community Property:** Ohio is not a community property state, following the equitable distribution model for property division.
- **Pass-Through Entity Tax:** Ohio also has a separate pass-through entity tax regime that may interact with trust taxation in certain circumstances.
