# Trust Compliance: California

## State Income Tax on Trusts

California imposes a state income tax on trusts that have California nexus. A trust is considered to have California nexus if it is a resident trust — meaning the grantor was a California resident when the trust became irrevocable — or if it has California-source income such as real estate, business operations, or rental property located in the state. Nonresident trusts with no California-source income generally have no California income tax filing obligation.

California's income tax rates for trusts are the same as those for individuals, ranging from 1% to 13.3% depending on taxable income. Trusts are taxed at the individual rate schedule, and the threshold for reaching the top bracket is quite low, meaning many trusts hit the maximum rate quickly. Fiduciaries should plan distributions carefully because California allows a deduction for income distributed to beneficiaries, which can shift the tax burden to beneficiaries who may be in lower brackets.

Estimated tax payments are required if the trust expects to owe $1,000 or more in California tax for the year. Form 540ES is used to make quarterly estimated payments, with due dates on April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15 of the following year. Penalties apply for underpayment.

## Filing Requirements

California fiduciary income tax is filed using Form 541, the Fiduciary Income Tax Return. The filing deadline is April 15 for calendar-year trusts, or the 15th day of the fourth month after the close of the fiscal year. Form 541 is due annually. If the trust is a simple trust that distributes all income, it may still need to file if it has California-source income or is a resident trust. An extension can be filed using FTB 3537, which provides an automatic six-month extension to file (but not to pay).

## Beneficiary Notice Requirements

California Probate Code Section 16061.7 requires the trustee to provide notice to beneficiaries when a trust becomes irrevocable or when there is a change of trustee. This notice must be sent within 60 days of the event triggering irrevocability or the change of trustee. The notice must include the identity of the settlor, the mailing address of the trustee, a description of the trust, and a statement that the beneficiary has the right to receive a copy of the terms of the trust and to receive an accounting. This is a critical compliance requirement that trustees must not overlook.

## Court Supervision

Court supervision of trusts in California is optional. A trust does not need to be registered with the court to be valid. Trustees may operate trusts in an unsupervised manner, which is the default for most revocable and irrevocable trusts. However, a trustee or beneficiary may petition the court for supervision if disputes arise or if judicial guidance is needed. Court supervision can provide protection from liability for the trustee but adds cost and complexity.

## Key State-Specific Rules

- **Asset Protection:** California does not allow self-settled asset protection trusts. A grantor cannot place assets in a trust for their own benefit and shield those assets from creditors. The trust must be irrevocable and for the benefit of others for asset protection to apply.
- **Perpetuity Period:** California has not abolished the rule against perpetuities. The default period is 90 years for trusts created after 2009, but California has not adopted the modern dynasty trust approach seen in other states.
- **Community Property:** California is a community property state, meaning trusts involving married couples must carefully track separate versus community property, as this affects taxation and creditor access.
- **Probate Code Compliance:** California has detailed trustee duties under Probate Code Sections 16000-16015, including the duty to account to beneficiaries at least annually unless waived.
