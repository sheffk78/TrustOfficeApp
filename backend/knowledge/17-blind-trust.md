# Blind Trust

## What It Is

A Blind Trust is a trust in which the settlor (the person whose assets are placed in trust) gives up all knowledge of and control over the trust's investments and distributions for the purpose of avoiding conflicts of interest. Once established, the settlor does not know what assets the trust holds, receives no reports on investment decisions, and cannot direct the trustee. The structure is most commonly used by political officeholders, candidates, and senior government officials who must prevent their financial holdings from creating the appearance — or reality — of a conflict between their official duties and their personal financial interests.

## Key Governance Requirements

- The trust must be managed by a fully independent trustee who has no relationship with the settlor that could compromise their independence. The settlor cannot serve as trustee, co-trustee, or investment advisor, and the trustee must make all investment and distribution decisions without the settlor's input or knowledge.
- The trust document must impose a strict information blackout: the settlor receives no account statements, no investment reports, and no information about holdings, transactions, or performance. This blindness is the entire point — if the settlor knows what they own, the conflict of interest the trust is meant to eliminate still exists.
- For public officials, the structure must comply with the applicable ethics rules — federal officials use "qualified blind trusts" governed by the Ethics in Government Act and certified by the Office of Government Ethics (OGE). The OGE requires advance review of the trust agreement, certification of the trustee, and periodic blind reporting.
- Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and congressional disclosure rules may still require the settlor to report the existence and value of the trust in broad categories, even though the specific holdings remain blind. The trustee typically provides a single aggregate value for disclosure without revealing the underlying assets.
- The trust should include a mechanism for the settlor to provide general investment guidelines (such as risk tolerance or ethical investment preferences) at the outset, but these must be fixed before the blindness begins and cannot be updated based on the settlor's official duties.

## Distribution Rules

- The independent trustee has full discretion over distributions to the settlor under the trust's distribution standard, and the settlor cannot request or direct specific distributions.
- Distributions are typically limited to cash, and the settlor does not learn which assets were sold to generate the cash — this preserves the blindness.
- The trust may permit distributions to the settlor's family or other beneficiaries, but all such decisions are the trustee's alone.
- When the trust terminates, assets are distributed per the trust terms; the settlor learns the final composition only at that point.

## Common Pitfalls

- Communicating with the trustee about holdings or performance — even a casual inquiry ("how's the portfolio doing?") can compromise the trust's blindness and trigger an ethics violation for a public official.
- Appointing a trustee who is not truly independent (a family member, business partner, or long-time financial advisor) — ethics offices and courts may reject the trust as a sham if the trustee has ties to the settlor.
- Failing to obtain ethics office certification before taking office, which can leave the official exposed to conflict-of-interest allegations and forced divestiture.
- Updating investment guidelines or directing the trustee to hold or sell specific assets based on the settlor's official actions — this destroys the blind trust's purpose and can create insider trading exposure under SEC rules.
