# Spendthrift Trust

## What It Is

A Spendthrift Trust is a trust that includes a spendthrift clause — a provision that prevents both the trust beneficiary and their creditors from reaching the trust assets before those assets are actually distributed to the beneficiary. The core idea is simple: if the beneficiary cannot assign or sell their future trust interest, and creditors cannot attach it before distribution, the trust creates a protective fence around the assets. This structure is commonly used when a settlor is worried that a beneficiary's poor money management, lawsuits, or divorce could otherwise drain their inheritance.

## Key Governance Requirements

- The trust document must contain valid spendthrift clause language: a clear prohibition on both voluntary and involuntary alienation of the beneficiary's interest. Standard language reads something like: "The beneficiary shall not transfer, assign, or pledge any interest in this trust, and no creditor shall have the right to attach or reach trust assets prior to distribution."
- The trustee must respect the restriction by refusing to honor any assignment, garnishment order, or levy that attempts to reach trust assets before they are distributed. Payments go to the beneficiary only, not to their creditors, unless a recognized legal exception applies.
- The trustee must know the jurisdiction's recognized exceptions to spendthrift protection. Most states carve out claims for child support, alimony, and federal tax debts — these can reach trust assets even with a valid spendthrift clause in place.
- Discretionary trusts (where the trustee has discretion over whether and how much to distribute) provide stronger spendthrift protection than mandatory trusts (where the trustee must distribute set amounts), because a beneficiary has no enforceable right the creditor can attach.
- For self-settled spendthrift trusts (where the settlor is also the beneficiary), most jurisdictions invalidate the spendthrift clause as to the settlor's own creditors — a person cannot shield their own assets from their own creditors by putting them in a spendthrift trust. This differs from asset protection trusts, which use specific state statutes to attempt that result.

## Distribution Rules

- In a mandatory (support) spendthrift trust, the trustee must make distributions according to the trust terms, but pays them directly to the beneficiary — not to creditors, even if the beneficiary asks.
- In a discretionary spendthrift trust, the trustee decides whether, when, and how much to distribute. The trustee should document the reasoning behind each discretionary decision, because discretion is what creates the protection: a beneficiary cannot compel a distribution, so neither can a creditor.
- When a recognized exception applies (child support, alimony, federal tax claims), the trustee may be ordered to redirect distributions to the claimant. The trustee should obtain a court order or formal demand and document compliance rather than paying voluntarily.
- Lump-sum distributions end the spendthrift protection for that amount — once the money is in the beneficiary's hands, it is reachable by creditors.

## Common Pitfalls

- Using weak or ambiguous spendthrift language that fails to prohibit both voluntary and involuntary alienation — courts may find the clause invalid and creditors can reach the assets.
- Honoring a garnishment order or creditor demand without verifying whether a recognized legal exception (child support, federal tax) actually applies — the trustee may breach their duty by paying creditors they were not legally required to pay.
- Making large lump-sum distributions that strip the spendthrift protection from that money once it leaves the trust, exposing it to the beneficiary's creditors.
- Assuming a spendthrift clause shields the trust from the settlor's own creditors in a self-settled trust — in most states it does not, and relying on that belief creates a false sense of security and potential fiduciary liability.
