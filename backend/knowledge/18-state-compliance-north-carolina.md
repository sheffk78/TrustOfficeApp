# Trust Compliance: North Carolina

## State Income Tax on Trusts

North Carolina imposes a state income tax on trusts with North Carolina nexus. Resident trusts and trusts with North Carolina-source income must file. Nonresident trusts with no North Carolina-source income generally have no filing obligation. North Carolina imposes a flat fiduciary income tax rate of 4.75% on the taxable income of resident trusts, applying to all trusts regardless of income level. The rate is lower than California's top marginal rate of 13.3% but higher than many neighboring states. North Carolina allows a deduction for income distributed to beneficiaries, shifting the tax burden to beneficiaries in lower brackets.

## Filing Requirements

North Carolina fiduciary income tax is filed using Form D-407, the Income Tax Return for Estates and Trusts. The filing deadline is April 15 for calendar-year trusts, or the 15th day of the fourth month after the close of the fiscal year. Form D-407 is due annually. An estate or trust must file Form D-407 if it has North Carolina taxable income or if it has gross income exceeding $10,000 (prorated for part-year returns). An extension can be filed using Form D-410P, which provides an automatic six-month extension to file (but not to pay).

## Beneficiary Notice Requirements

North Carolina does not have a statutory requirement for trustees to provide initial notice to beneficiaries upon accepting trusteeship or when a trust becomes irrevocable. However, the North Carolina Uniform Trust Code (Chapter 36C, N.C. Gen. Stat.) requires trustees to keep qualified beneficiaries reasonably informed about the administration of the trust and to respond to beneficiary requests for information. Trustees must provide annual accountings and updates on significant events. While there is no mandatory initial notice deadline, trustees should document all communications with beneficiaries to create a defensible record.

## Court Supervision

Court supervision of trusts in North Carolina is optional. A trust does not need to be registered with the court to be valid. Trustees may operate trusts in an unsupervised manner, which is the default for most revocable and irrevocable trusts. However, a trustee or beneficiary may petition the court for supervision if disputes arise or if judicial guidance is needed. North Carolina courts can also intervene when a trustee requests instructions or when a beneficiary seeks enforcement of their rights under the trust. Court supervision can provide protection from liability for the trustee but adds cost and complexity.

## Key State-Specific Rules

- **Uniform Trust Code:** North Carolina adopted the UTC as Chapter 36C of the N.C. General Statutes, effective 2006. However, North Carolina omitted several UTC provisions, including the 60-day notice-of-trusteeship requirement (UTC Â§813). Trustees should be aware that North Carolina's trust law diverges from the model UTC in important ways.
- **Spendthrift Protection:** North Carolina allows self-settled asset protection trusts, meaning a grantor can place assets in a trust for their own benefit and shield those assets from creditors, subject to certain limitations under Chapter 36C.
- **Perpetuity Period:** North Carolina has abolished the rule against perpetuities, allowing trusts to last indefinitely for multi-generational wealth planning.
- **Community Property:** North Carolina is not a community property state, following the equitable distribution model for property division.
