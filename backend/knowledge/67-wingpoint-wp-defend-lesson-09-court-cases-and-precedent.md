# Lesson 9: Court Cases and Precedent — What Has Actually Been Tested

**Course:** WP-DEFEND — Standing Strong: Defending Your Trust and Your Position  
**Lesson:** 9 of 10  

---

There's a temptation in this space to collect court cases like weapons — to find a ruling that sounds like it supports your position, cite it boldly, and believe that the citation alone makes you right.

Court cases are important. Understanding what the courts have actually said about the tax code, the definitions, and the constitutional framework is important. But understanding what the courts have *not* said — and what people in this space claim they said — is just as important, because bad citations undermine your credibility faster than almost anything else.

This lesson covers four things: the cases that form your foundation, the definitions the courts have established, the one case that shields you, and the counter-argument the IRS will use against you — so you're prepared when it arrives.

The code's definitions are narrow, your filings are consistent, and the burden is on them. These cases explain *why* the definitions are narrow. Your filings and your documentation carry the consistency and the burden-shifting. The cases are the legal backbone; your records are the operational proof. You need both.

---

## Part 1: The Foundation — How the Tax Became an Excise

These four cases form a single legislative chain. They tell one story: the Supreme Court established that the federal income tax is an excise — a tax on privileged activity, not a direct tax on persons — and that story is the foundation of your position.

### Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co. (1895)

**What people claim it held:** "The Supreme Court said income taxes are unconstitutional."

**What it actually held:** The Court held that a tax on income from property (rents, dividends, interest) was a direct tax requiring apportionment among the states — but it distinguished this from taxes on income from active labor, which remained valid as indirect (excise) taxes. Pollock drew the line between taxes on property income (direct) and taxes on active labor (indirect/excise).

**How to use it:** Cite Pollock for the historical distinction between direct taxes on property and indirect (excise) taxes on activity. Do not cite it as a blanket ruling that income taxes are unconstitutional.

### Flint v. Stone Tracy Co. (1911)

**What people claim it held:** "The Supreme Court said only corporations pay income tax."

**What it actually held:** A corporate franchise tax measured by income is an excise tax on the *privilege* of doing business in corporate form — not a direct tax on income. The tax attaches to the activity and the capacity in which business is conducted, not to the person or to income itself. This directly supports the framework that the tax applies to those exercising a federal privilege — not to everyone by default.

**How to use it:** Cite Flint for the proposition that an excise tax attaches to a privileged activity, not to a person simply for existing. Do not cite it for the proposition that only corporations owe income tax.

### The 16th Amendment (1913)

The 16th Amendment removed the apportionment requirement for income taxes. It did not change the nature of the tax — it remains an excise on privileged activity. The Amendment solved a practical problem (apportionment was nearly impossible to administer) without transforming the tax from an excise into a direct tax on persons.

### Brushaber v. Union Pacific Railroad Co. (1916)

**What people claim it held:** "The Supreme Court ruled the income tax only applies to privileged activities, not your private labor."

**What it actually held:** Frank Brushaber challenged the constitutionality of the income tax after the 16th Amendment. The Court held that the income tax was already constitutional as an excise before the 16th Amendment, and that the Amendment merely prevented the tax from being classified as a direct tax requiring apportionment. Brushaber confirms that the income tax is an excise — but what qualifies as a taxable activity is determined by the definitions in the code, not by Brushaber itself.

**How to use it:** Cite Brushaber for the proposition that the income tax is an excise that must attach to a taxable activity. Do not cite it for the proposition that your private labor is automatically exempt.

**The chain in one line:** Pollock established the direct/indirect distinction → Flint established that income taxes are excises on privileged activity → the 16th Amendment removed the apportionment problem → Brushaber confirmed the tax remains an excise after the Amendment → the code's narrow definitions (§3401(c), §3401(a)) determine who is engaged in that activity.

You're not citing disconnected cases. You're tracing a consistent line of reasoning from the Court to the statute.

## Part 2: The Definitions — What "Trade or Business" Actually Means

**A note on the definitions.** Section 3401(c) defines "employee" and §3401(a) defines "wages" for withholding purposes. These are the definitions this course relies on. The phrase "trade or business" has no single codified definition in the IRC — it is defined by judicial interpretation (notably *Higgins v. Commissioner* and *Commissioner v. Groetzinger*). Be precise: cite the code sections that actually contain the definitions you're using, and cite case law where the code is silent.

### Eisner v. Macomber (1920)

**What people claim it held:** "The Supreme Court defined income as 'gain derived from capital, from labor, or from both combined' — so my private labor isn't income."

**What it actually held:** The Court defined income for constitutional purposes as "the gain derived from capital, from labor, or from both combined." But the specific issue was whether a stock dividend constituted taxable income (the Court held it did not, because the shareholder realized no gain). Eisner establishes that "income" requires a realized gain — not just any receipt. This supports the position that not every receipt constitutes "income" within the meaning of the Amendment. But Eisner did not hold that compensation for private labor is automatically excluded. The principle (income requires a gain) is what you cite; the application to your compensation requires the statutory definitions.

**How to use it:** Cite Eisner for the definition of income as requiring a realized gain. Do not cite it as a blanket exemption for private labor.

### Hale v. Henkel (1906)

**What people claim it held:** "Corporations have no constitutional rights, so the government can do whatever it wants with them."

**What it actually held:** A corporation, as a creature of the state, does not possess the same Fourth and Fifth Amendment protections as a natural person. The trust's records belong to the trust entity, not the individual trustee — and the entity's records can be subpoenaed.

**How to use it:** This establishes the principle of entity separateness. You operate in a distinct capacity as fiduciary for a distinct entity. The trust CAN be subpoenaed for its records — but your personal records are protected differently. This isn't a weakness; it's a distinction that empowers you to keep personal and fiduciary records cleanly separated. Cite Hale for the separateness principle. Do not cite it to claim that entities have no rights at all.

### Stanton v. Baltic Mining Co. (1916)

**What people claim it held:** "The Supreme Court ruled that the 16th Amendment conferred no new taxing power."

**What it actually held:** The 16th Amendment did not extend the taxing power to new subjects — it merely removed the apportionment requirement for income taxes that were already excises. This is consistent with Brushaber: the income tax was already an excise, and the 16th Amendment confirmed it doesn't need to be apportioned.

**How to use it:** Cite Stanton for the same reason as Brushaber — it confirms the income tax is an excise, not a direct tax. Do not extend it beyond what it held.

## Part 3: The Shield — Cheek v. United States (1991)

This is the one case that directly protects you. Know it cold.

**What people claim it held:** "The Supreme Court said you can't be prosecuted for not paying taxes if you genuinely believe you don't have to."

**What it actually held:** John Cheek was prosecuted for willfully failing to file. The Court held that a good-faith belief that one is not required to file — even if objectively unreasonable — negates the "willfulness" element required for criminal conviction under §7203. The Court did not say Cheek's belief was correct. It said that a sincere belief lacks the criminal intent required for conviction.

**But the Court also drew a line.** Cheek's good-faith defense does not apply if you know the law exists and choose to defy it as unconstitutional — that demonstrates knowledge of the duty and a deliberate choice to violate it, which satisfies criminal willfulness. Cheek protects you when you genuinely believe the law does not apply based on your reading of the statute. It does not protect you when you know the law and refuse to comply.

**Why the difference matters:** Cheek protects you from criminal prosecution if your belief is sincere — but it does not validate the belief itself. The IRS can still assess taxes, penalties, and interest. Cheek prevents criminal prosecution for willful failure to file when you genuinely believed you weren't required to. That's protection from prison, not protection from assessment. And Cheek does not protect against civil penalties — §6702, §6662, and §6651 each operate on their own terms.

**How to use it:** Cite Cheek for the proposition that a sincere, good-faith belief negates criminal willfulness — and note that this is exactly why you've studied the law and filed from a consistent position. Do not cite Cheek as proof that you don't have to file.

## Part 4: The Counter-Argument — Glenshaw Glass

This is the case the IRS will cite against you. Know it so you're prepared when it arrives.

### Commissioner v. Glenshaw Glass Co. (1955)

**What the IRS will claim it held:** "The Supreme Court said all income is taxable — period. 'All accessions to wealth, clearly realized, over which the taxpayer has complete dominion.' That means your compensation is income and it's taxable."

**What it actually held:** The Court defined gross income broadly as "all accessions to wealth, clearly realized, over which the taxpayer has complete dominion." The specific issue was whether punitive damages constituted taxable income. The Court held they did — that the definition of gross income under §61 is broad and includes gains from "any source whatever." The case was about statutory interpretation of what counts as *income*, not about what counts as a *taxable activity*.

**Your response:** Glenshaw Glass defined income broadly for statutory interpretation purposes; it did **not** redefine the constitutional limits on what constitutes a taxable *activity*. "All income" under §61 still requires the income to arise from a taxable activity under the excise framework — and the definitions of "employee" under §3401(c) and "wages" under §3401(a) determine whether that activity exists. (Note: "trade or business" has no codified definition in the IRC — it is defined by case law.) Glenshaw Glass tells you what counts as income once you're in the taxable activity. It does not tell you that every person receiving money is in a taxable activity by default.

**An honest note on this counter-argument.** The Glenshaw Glass reframing is conceptually coherent — it distinguishes "what counts as income" from "what counts as a taxable activity." But you should know: no court has accepted the argument that compensation for private labor falls outside the excise framework because it doesn't arise from a "taxable activity." The counter-argument is useful for framing your position; it has not been judicially validated. Use it to clarify your reasoning. Don't rely on it as a legal defense.

**How to use it:** Don't cite Glenshaw Glass for your position. Know it so you can respond when the IRS cites it against you. The response is not to argue with Glenshaw Glass — it's to show that the case answers a different question. The question isn't "is this income?" The question is "does this income arise from activity that meets the code's definitions of a taxable activity?"

## Cases That Are Commonly Misused — and Why You Should Not Cite Them

- **"No law requires you to file" cases.** Every court that has considered this argument has rejected it. Your position is not that filing is voluntary — it's that your compensation doesn't meet the statutory definitions that trigger the requirement.
- **Sovereign citizen, admiralty, and "living man" arguments.** (See Lesson 5 for the full discussion.) Claims that you are not a "citizen of the United States," that courts have no jurisdiction over you, or that your NAME in all caps is a separate entity have been litigated hundreds of times and have never succeeded. They will destroy your credibility.

If a case or argument sounds like a "get out of jail free" card, it's almost certainly wrong. Your position is built on definitions, consistency, and documented filings over time.

## How to Use Cases in Your Responses

Citing a case is not the argument. It's evidence that supports one piece of the argument. Use it surgically.

**(a) Cite the case name, year, and the specific proposition your citation supports — nothing more.** "Brushaber v. Union Pacific, 240 U.S. 1 (1916), held that the federal income tax is an excise" is a citation. "Brushaber proves I don't owe taxes" is not.

**(b) One or two cases per response is plenty.** More looks like you're throwing spaghetti at the wall. Pick the case that supports the specific point you're making, and let the code and your documentation do the rest.

**(c) Place the citation in the body of your response, connected to the specific point it supports.** Don't list cases at the end like a bibliography. A citation detached from its point is a citation the examiner will ignore.

**(d) Attach the relevant page if you have it.** "240 U.S. 1, at 16" shows you've read the case. "240 U.S. 1" shows you found the citation somewhere.

**(e) If the examiner ignores your citation, don't keep citing.** Escalate the specific point through the established process. Repeating a citation the examiner has already disregarded doesn't strengthen your position — it weakens your credibility.

## The Honest Reality

There is no single case that says "a private irrevocable trust operated by a living man in private capacity, whose compensation doesn't meet the statutory definition of wages, is exempt from federal income tax." The Supreme Court has never ruled on that specific fact pattern. No court has issued a blanket ruling that validates your position or invalidates it.

What you have is a framework:

- The income tax is an excise, not a direct tax on persons (Pollock → Brushaber → Stanton).
- An excise must attach to a privileged activity, not to a person simply for existing (Flint).
- "Income" requires a realized gain (Eisner v. Macomber); "all income" under §61 applies to income from taxable activity (Glenshaw Glass).
- A sincere, good-faith belief negates criminal willfulness (Cheek).
- The definitions in the code are narrow — "employee," "wages" all point to federal activity (§3401(c), §3401(a)). "Trade or business" has no single codified definition — it is defined by case law (Higgins, Groetzinger). No court has accepted the argument that §3401(c) limits income tax to statutory/federal employees only. Your position relies on reading the statute in a way courts have not endorsed. File from this position in good faith, but understand the legal risk: multiple courts have rejected the §3401(c) argument when it has been litigated.
- Filing requirements attach to those engaged in the taxable activity, not to everyone by default.

This framework is strong. It is supported by the code itself. It is consistent with the constitutional limits on federal taxing power. It is defensible when presented with documentation, consistency, and clean records.

**The absence of a single case that "solves this" works in your favor.** The IRS has no case that says the code's definitions *don't* mean what they say. The burden is on them to show that §3401(c) includes private workers, that "wages" means all compensation. They haven't done that in a case that addresses your specific fact pattern. You don't need a case that says you're right. You need the code to say what it says — and it does.

The honest position is: "I have read the law. I have filed consistently. I have documented my position. I believe my position is correct based on the statutory definitions. I am prepared to defend it through the established process." That's a strong position. It's not an invincible one. Know the difference.

## A Win Story

A trustee responding to a CP2000 cited Brushaber for one proposition — that the income tax is an excise that must attach to a taxable activity. One sentence. One citation. The full passage read:

> *"Under Brushaber v. Union Pacific Railroad Co., 240 U.S. 1 (1916), the federal income tax is an excise that attaches to taxable activity. My corrected return reflects that the compensation in question does not arise from activity meeting the statutory definitions of 'employee' under §3401(c) or 'wages' under §3401(a)."*

The IRS closed the file after reviewing the response and supporting documentation.

One case. One proposition. Connected to the code. That's how it works.

## Your Quick Reference

- **Foundation:** Pollock → Flint → 16th Amendment → Brushaber = the tax is an excise on privileged activity
- **Definitions:** §3401(c) defines "employee," §3401(a) defines "wages," "trade or business" is defined by case law (Higgins, Groetzinger)
- **Shield:** Cheek = sincere belief negates criminal willfulness, but does not protect against civil penalties
- **Counter-argument:** Glenshaw Glass = defines "income" broadly, but does not define what counts as a "taxable activity" — that's where your definitions live
- **Risk:** No court has accepted the §3401(c) argument. Your position is a good-faith reading, not a judicially validated one.
- **Use cases surgically:** one case, one proposition, connected to the code
