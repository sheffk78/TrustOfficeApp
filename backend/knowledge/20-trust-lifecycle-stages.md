# Trust Lifecycle Stages

A trust is not a set-it-and-forget-it arrangement. It moves through distinct stages, each with specific duties, deadlines, and documentation requirements. Understanding where your trust sits in its lifecycle helps you focus on the right tasks at the right time — and avoid the gaps that lead to liability. This guide walks through the four major stages of trust administration.

## Stage 1: Initial Setup (First 90 Days)

The first 90 days after a trust becomes active — whether through a life event, a death, or a funding event — are the most critical for establishing proper administration. Missing early steps creates problems that compound for years.

**Complete these tasks in the first 90 days:**

1. **Obtain an Employer Identification Number (EIN)** from the IRS if the trust is irrevocable or has become irrevocable (e.g., at the grantor's death). A revocable living trust during the grantor's lifetime typically uses the grantor's Social Security number and does not need its own EIN. Apply online at IRS.gov — it takes minutes and is free. Do not use your personal SSN for an irrevocable trust's tax filings.

2. **Open a trust bank account.** Use the EIN, not your personal SSN. All trust income and expenses must flow through this account. Never commingle trust funds with personal funds — commingling is one of the most common and damaging breaches of fiduciary duty.

3. **Record initial minutes.** Document your acceptance of the trusteeship, the date you assumed duties, and your initial actions. This establishes the start of your fiduciary obligations and creates a baseline record.

4. **Inventory all trust assets.** Create a comprehensive list of every asset titled to the trust: real estate, bank and brokerage accounts, business interests, tangible personal property, life insurance, and digital assets. Include estimated values as of the date you became trustee.

5. **Review the trust document thoroughly.** Read every article. Identify the distribution standard, beneficiary class, trustee powers, termination provisions, and any special instructions. Note deadlines or required actions. If anything is unclear, consult a trust attorney — guessing is not acceptable.

6. **Set up the Schedule A.** The Schedule A is the official asset schedule attached to the trust. Record every asset, its value, and its character (principal vs. income). Update it whenever assets are added, removed, or significantly revalued.

7. **File initial tax forms.** Determine the trust's tax filing obligations. An irrevocable trust generally must file Form 1041 annually. Some trusts file Form 56 (Notice of Fiduciary Relationship) to notify the IRS of your role. Consult a CPA or tax professional for your specific situation.

## Stage 2: Ongoing Administration (Years 2+)

After the initial setup, trust administration becomes a cycle of recurring responsibilities. These duties do not expire — they continue for the life of the trust.

**Annual and periodic responsibilities:**

- **Record annual minutes.** Document key decisions, distributions approved or denied, investment reviews, and any significant events each year. Annual minutes are your ongoing evidence of diligent administration.
- **Revalue trust assets.** At least annually, update the valuations on your Schedule A. Real estate, business interests, and investment portfolios fluctuate. Accurate valuations are essential for distributions, accountings, and tax filings.
- **File tax returns on time.** Form 1041 (U.S. Income Tax Return for Estates and Trusts) is generally due April 15 (or the 15th of the fourth month after the tax year end). File extensions if needed, but never miss a filing deadline without one.
- **Communicate with beneficiaries.** Send annual accountings or notices as required by the trust document and applicable state trust code. Respond to beneficiary information requests promptly.
- **Review distributions.** Evaluate each distribution request against the trust's distribution standard. Document your analysis and reasoning. Do not make distributions outside the standard, even for beneficiaries you believe deserve them.
- **Monitor your Defensibility Score.** Regularly check your governance and documentation score. A declining score signals gaps that could become liability. Address flagged items before they become problems.
- **Review investments.** Assess investment performance and the trust's investment policy at least annually. Document the review and any changes made. The Prudent Investor Rule requires you to evaluate the portfolio as a whole, not asset by asset.

## Stage 3: Trust Modification and Amendments

Trusts are not always permanent. Most states provide mechanisms for modification under the Uniform Trust Code, but the rules are strict and vary by jurisdiction.

**When and how trusts can be modified:**

- **Trustee powers.** A trustee generally cannot unilaterally modify a trust. Some trust documents grant the trustee limited administrative powers — like adjusting between principal and income — but these are narrow and must be exercised in good faith and with documented rationale.

- **Beneficiary consent.** In many states, if all beneficiaries consent, a trust can be modified or even terminated, provided the modification does not frustrate a material purpose of the trust. Some states require court approval even with unanimous consent.

- **Court approval.** A trustee or beneficiary can petition the court to modify a trust due to changed circumstances that the grantor did not anticipate. The court will apply a standard — often "circumstances not known to the grantor and consistent with the grantor's probable intent."

- **Decanting.** Decanting is the power to transfer trust assets from one trust to a new trust with different terms. Over 35 states have decanting statutes. It can be a powerful tool, but the rules are complex — including restrictions that protect beneficiaries' rights — and almost always require legal counsel.

- **Trust protector actions.** Some trusts name a trust protector with authority to modify terms, remove and replace trustees, or change governing law. If your trust has a trust protector, understand their powers and when they should be exercised.

- **Amendment documentation.** Every modification must be documented in writing with the legal basis, authority, and effective date recorded. Store the amendment in your trust records and update your minutes.

## Stage 4: Trust Termination and Wind-Down

Termination is the final stage — and cutting corners here can undo years of careful administration. Follow these steps in order.

**Steps for terminating a trust:**

1. **Complete a final accounting.** Prepare a complete accounting covering the period from the last accounting through the date of termination. Every beneficiary entitled to distributions must receive the final accounting.

2. **File the final tax return.** File Form 1041 marked as the final return by checking the "final return" box. The trust's tax year closes on the date of termination. Request a closing letter from the IRS if applicable.

3. **Distribute remaining assets.** Distribute all remaining trust assets to the beneficiaries as directed by the trust document. Obtain receipts for every distribution. Coordinate timing with your CPA to align with the tax year-end.

4. **Obtain beneficiary releases.** Request each beneficiary sign a release and receipt acknowledging they have received their final distribution and approving your accounting. A release significantly limits future claims — though in some states, beneficiaries cannot be required to waive the right to an accounting.

5. **Obtain trustee discharge.** If possible, obtain a court order discharging you as trustee. This provides the strongest protection against future claims. If court discharge is not practical, beneficiary releases serve as a practical alternative.

6. **Retain records.** Do not destroy trust records after termination. Retain all records — including minutes, accountings, tax returns, and correspondence — for at least seven years, or longer if required by your state or if any claims are possible. Store them securely and permanently.
