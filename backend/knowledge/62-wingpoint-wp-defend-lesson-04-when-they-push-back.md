# Lesson 4: When They Push Back — Escalation, Your Rights, and Making the IRS Prove Its Position

**Course:** WP-DEFEND — Standing Strong: Defending Your Trust and Your Position  
**Lesson:** 4 of 10  

---

You know your power position (Lesson 2). You know what notices to expect and how to handle them (Lesson 3). Most people's experience ends at the CP2000 response. The matter resolves, and they move on.

But sometimes it doesn't resolve. Sometimes the IRS disagrees with your response, pushes back, and escalates. This lesson covers what happens then — and more importantly, the rights and tools you have that most people never exercise.

---

## If the IRS Disagrees — The Notice of Deficiency

If the IRS reviews your response and still disagrees with your position, you may receive a **Notice of Deficiency** (sometimes called a "90-day letter"). This is more serious than a CP2000 — it's a formal determination, not a proposal. But it is not a collection action. It is not a levy. It is not a lien. It is the IRS saying: "We've reviewed your position and we disagree. You have 90 days to petition the Tax Court if you want to challenge our determination."

A Notice of Deficiency does not mean:

- **You have to pay immediately.** The IRS cannot levy your assets during the 90-day petition window. You have time.
- **You're wrong.** It means the IRS has made a determination that disagrees with your position. You have the right to challenge that determination.
- **You need a lawyer tomorrow.** You have 90 days to figure out your options — not 90 minutes.

It *does* mean you need to pay attention. The 90-day deadline is strict. If you don't petition, the IRS can proceed to collection on the determined amount. If you do petition, the matter goes to Tax Court for a hearing. The vast majority of disputes that reach this stage are settled before they reach a courtroom.

If you receive a Notice of Deficiency: (1) read it carefully, (2) understand what the IRS is claiming, (3) decide whether to petition Tax Court, (4) prepare your case with documentation.

## Demanding the IRS Prove Its Position — Your Right to Know the Law They're Using

When the IRS sends you a notice proposing an adjustment, they're not just asking you to accept a number. They're making a determination based on specific legal authority — and you have the right to know what that authority is.

Don't assume the IRS's determination is automatically grounded in correct law. Their system matches information returns against what you reported and flags discrepancies automatically. The examiner who reviews your response may or may not understand the specific statutes you've cited. They may be operating on presumption, not analysis.

**You have the right to demand that the IRS identify the specific statutes, regulations, and legal authority they are relying on to make their determination.** This is not a trick. This is not aggressive. This is fundamental due process. The IRS cannot simply say "you owe this amount" — they must be able to articulate *why* under the law.

When you respond to a CP2000, a Notice of Deficiency, or any proposed adjustment, include this in your response:

**"I respectfully request that the IRS identify the specific statutory authority, including Internal Revenue Code sections, Treasury Regulations, and any relevant Revenue Rulings or case law, upon which the proposed adjustment is based."**

Why this matters:

- **The IRS must be able to point to law, not just to a form.** If their entire basis is "the W-2 says one thing and your return says another," that's not a legal determination — that's a matching error. You filed a 4852 that supersedes the W-2. The question isn't what the W-2 says. The question is whether the IRS can cite specific statutory authority for treating the employer's information return as conclusive when you've filed an IRS-authorized substitute that contradicts it.

- **Forcing the IRS to articulate their authority exposes weak positions.** If they can't cite specific law, they're relying on presumption — the same presumption you've already rebutted with your 4852. If they cite §61 (gross income) but can't explain how your compensation meets the statutory definition of wages under §3401(a) and §3121(a), they haven't actually addressed your position. They've restated theirs.

- **This applies at every stage.** CP2000 response. Examination. Appeal. Tax Court petition. At every point, the IRS's determination must be grounded in specific law. If you're in an examination or a CDP hearing, ask: "What specific statute, regulation, or ruling supports the position that the compensation reported on the W-2 constitutes wages as defined in the Internal Revenue Code?" Make them answer it. If they can't, their determination lacks foundation.

- **The burden of proof shifts when you've documented your position.** The IRS starts with the presumption that the W-2 is correct. But when you file a 4852 — an IRS-authorized substitute form — you've created a dispute between two federal documents. At that point, the IRS can't simply rely on the W-2's prima facie status. They must explain why their own form (the 4852) does not rebut the presumption created by their own system (information return matching). Forcing them to articulate this is not obstruction — it's holding them to their own legal standards.

The pattern: every time the IRS makes a determination, demand the authority behind it. Don't just respond to the dollar amount. Respond to the legal basis. If they can't or won't provide one, that gap is your strongest position in any appeal or petition.

## Demanding Validation of the Debt — When the IRS Says You Owe

If the IRS has progressed past a proposal and is now asserting that you owe a specific amount — whether through a balance due notice, a Notice of Deficiency that's become final, or a collection notice — you have another right that most people never exercise: **the right to demand that the IRS validate the debt.**

A debt is not a debt just because the IRS says it is. Under the law, a valid tax debt requires specific elements to be legally enforceable. If any element is missing or defective, the debt is not valid — and the IRS cannot collect what is not legally owed.

### What Makes a Tax Debt Legally Valid?

For the IRS to lawfully assess and collect a tax debt, all of the following must be present:

1. **A valid assessment.** The IRS must have made a valid assessment under §6201 and §6203. This means the assessment must be based on a return, a Substitute for Return, or a deficiency determination — and the assessment must be properly recorded. If the assessment is based on an SFR that your properly filed return supersedes, or if the assessment wasn't made in accordance with §6203 (which requires a signing officer), it may not be valid.

2. **A valid notice.** The IRS must have sent you proper notice of the assessment and your rights. Under §6212, a Notice of Deficiency must be sent to your last known address. Under §6330, a Notice of Intent to Levy must include your right to a CDP hearing. If they didn't send it to the right address, or if it didn't include the required information about your rights, the notice may be defective.

3. **A legal basis for the amount.** The amount owed must be grounded in specific statutory authority. Not "we think you owe this because you reported less than the W-2." The IRS must be able to demonstrate, under specific sections of the Internal Revenue Code, that the compensation in question meets the legal definition of the type of income they're taxing it as. If they can't articulate the statute — if they're relying solely on the W-2's existence rather than the legal classification of the income — the basis for the debt is defective.

4. **Proper procedure followed.** Every step in the assessment and collection process has procedural requirements — deadlines, notice requirements, hearing rights. If the IRS skipped a step, missed a deadline on their end, or failed to provide a required notice, the process is defective and the debt may not be enforceable.

### How to Request Debt Validation

When you receive a notice asserting that you owe a specific amount, respond in writing with a request for validation. This is not the same as disputing the amount — it's asking the IRS to demonstrate that the debt is legally valid in the first place.

**A debt validation request should include:**

- **Request for the specific statutory authority** for the assessment. What IRC sections, Treasury Regulations, and Revenue Rulings is the IRS relying on to classify the compensation as taxable income and as wages?
- **Request for verification of the assessment.** Under §6203, the Secretary shall establish the amount of tax and record it. Request a copy of the assessment record, including the date of assessment and the name and signature of the officer who made it.
- **Request for the information return(s) relied upon.** Which specific W-2, 1099, or other information return is the IRS relying on? What is the source? And how does the IRS account for the Form 4852 that you filed — an IRS-authorized substitute — that contradicts that information return?
- **Request for the delegation of authority.** Who at the IRS has the delegated authority to make this assessment? Under what delegation order?
- **Request for verification that all procedural requirements were met.** Was proper notice sent? Were your rights to appeal and hearing preserved? Were all deadlines on the IRS's side met?

Send this request via certified mail with return receipt. Keep a copy. The IRS is obligated to respond. If they cannot or do not provide the validation you've requested, their ability to enforce the debt is compromised.

### What This Does

**First, it forces the IRS to prove its position, not just assert it.** Most people accept IRS notices at face value. The IRS is accustomed to people either paying or ignoring notices, not demanding legal justification. Many assessments cannot withstand this level of scrutiny — not because the IRS is malicious, but because their systems are automated and their examiners are not always thorough.

**Second, it creates a record of your due diligence.** If the matter progresses to an appeal or Tax Court, your validation request and the IRS's response (or lack thereof) become part of the record. A pattern of documented, lawful requests for the IRS to substantiate its position demonstrates that you are not a frivolous filer — you are someone exercising your legal rights in good faith.

**Third, it shifts the dynamic.** The IRS sends notices that feel like demands. A validation request reframes the interaction: you are not a passive recipient of a government assertion. You are a party to a legal process, and you have the right to see the law behind every claim.

### When to Send a Validation Request

- **When you receive a CP2000** proposing an adjustment — include it as part of your response.
- **When you receive a Notice of Deficiency** — before or alongside your Tax Court petition.
- **When you receive a balance due notice** (CP501, CP503, CP504) — especially if you haven't previously requested validation.
- **When you receive a Notice of Intent to Levy** — alongside your CDP hearing request.
- **Any time the IRS asserts a specific dollar amount** and you have not yet received a satisfactory legal basis for that amount.

## The §6702 "Frivolous Return" Penalty — Intimidation, Not Assessment

You may receive a notice threatening a $5,000 penalty for filing a "frivolous" return. As covered in Lesson 2, "frivolous" has a specific legal meaning under §6702 — and a documented, code-grounded return is not it. If you receive this notice, respond promptly and specifically. Cite the statutes you relied on. Include your calculations. Attach your 4852. Show that your position is grounded in the code, not pulled from thin air.

This notice is designed to intimidate. It is not a valid assessment of your return. Treat it seriously — but understand what it actually means and what it doesn't.

## If the IRS Proposes an Audit

An audit — or "examination," as the IRS calls it — is a review of your return in detail. Most people who file corrected returns never face one. If it happens:

**Correspondence audit** is the most common type. Everything happens by mail. The IRS sends a letter asking for documentation of specific items on your return. You send the documentation. They review it and either accept your position or propose changes. This is procedural, not dramatic.

**Office or field audit** is less common, especially for individual filers. If you receive a notification requesting an in-person meeting, that's a good time to seek counsel.

An audit is a procedural review. It is not a criminal investigation. It is not a raid. No one is showing up at your door. If you've kept clean records, consistent minutes, filed correctly, and documented your position, an audit is manageable. You provide what's asked, you answer what's asked, and you don't volunteer what wasn't asked.

---

## Substitute for Return (SFR) — If the IRS Claims You Didn't File

If you don't file, or if the IRS treats your return as if it was never filed, they may create a "Substitute for Return" under §6020(b). This assesses tax at the highest rate, with no deductions, no credits, and no adjustments — creating a massive liability designed to pressure capitulation.

The correct response: file your own return. A properly filed return supersedes the SFR. Your return, with your corrections, your 4852, and your documented position, replaces the IRS's substitute. This is why you always file. This is why you never skip a year. This is why you keep copies and certified mail receipts. If you filed and the IRS claims they never received it, your proof of filing overrides their system.

## Collections — Liens, Levies, and CDP Hearings

If a Notice of Deficiency becomes final — either because you didn't petition within 90 days or because the Tax Court ruled against you — the IRS moves to collection. This is where notices shift from proposals to actions.

- **A federal tax lien** is a claim the IRS places on your property. It's public record and can affect your ability to sell or refinance property. A lien does not take your property — it stakes a claim against it. You have 5 business days to request a hearing.

- **A federal tax levy** is an actual seizure — the IRS takes money from your bank account, garnishes your wages, or seizes property. Before levying, the IRS must send a Notice of Intent to Levy (LT11 or CP90) and a Notice of Your Right to a Hearing. You have 30 days to request a Collection Due Process (CDP) hearing.

- **A CDP hearing under §6330** is one of your most important rights at the collection stage. You can challenge the collection action and, if you didn't already have the opportunity, the underlying tax liability itself. It is a real proceeding, appealable to Tax Court.

**Do not ignore collection notices.** The lien and levy process has specific timelines and specific rights. Missing a deadline at this stage can mean losing the right to challenge the underlying tax. If you receive a Notice of Intent to Levy, request a CDP hearing immediately.

---

## Penalty Assessments

The IRS may propose penalties on top of the underlying tax. Each penalty has specific criteria and specific defenses:

- **Accuracy-related penalty (§6662)** — for negligence or substantial understatement. Defense: a good-faith position grounded in the Internal Revenue Code, documented with specific citations and a Form 4852.
- **Failure-to-file (§6651)** — if the IRS claims you didn't file. Defense: your certified mail receipt and copy of your return.
- **Failure-to-pay (§6654)** — estimated tax penalty.

Penalties are additive, not automatic. Each one can be challenged — but you have to challenge them specifically, not just the underlying tax.

---

## What You Take Away From This Lesson

- A Notice of Deficiency is more serious than a CP2000 — it's a determination, not a proposal — but it's not a collection action. You have 90 days to petition Tax Court. Use the time.
- You have the right to demand that the IRS identify the specific statutes, regulations, and legal authority they're relying on. Don't just respond to the dollar amount — respond to the legal basis. If they can't cite specific law, their determination lacks foundation.
- You have the right to demand debt validation. A tax debt is not valid just because the IRS says it is. Require: valid assessment, valid notice, legal basis for the amount, proper procedure. If any element is missing, the debt may not be enforceable.
- If things escalate: an audit is a procedural review, not a criminal investigation. An SFR is the IRS creating a crisis — your filed return supersedes it. Collection notices give you the right to a CDP hearing.
- Penalties can be challenged — but challenge them specifically, not just the underlying tax.
- At every stage, you have rights and response windows. The process is designed to give you chances. Use them.
